## Scenario

Every task Level compares two real apartments for **Sam**, a friend deciding whether to move in as a roommate.
Sam's one stated priority: Sam works from home and needs a quiet place to focus. This Scenario is shared across
all 4 lessons in this Set - the same comparison carried from grammar input (Lessons 1-2) through drafting
(Lesson 3) to a finished, published piece (Lesson 4).

- **Apartment A:** the top floor of a converted brownstone, on a quiet residential street. Smaller kitchen, no
  second closet, but thick walls and a bedroom set back from the street behind a short hallway.
- **Apartment B:** bigger, closer to downtown, and about a hundred dollars a month cheaper. Faces a busier
  avenue; traffic and nearby restaurants are noticeable well into the night.
  Levels 4-5 write a single paragraph making one or several comparisons between the two apartments. Levels 6-7
  write a full **comparison essay** weighing the two apartments against each other across several dimensions, for
  Sam, with Sam's stated concern (quiet) in view throughout.

**Real-world writing form:** Levels 4-5, a descriptive/comparative paragraph. Levels 6-7, a comparison essay.

## Grammar Focus A/B (Level 5's row: Levels 4-5 centerpiece; lighter sentence-craft component for Levels 6-7)

**Focus A (this lesson's centerpiece for Levels 4-5, folded lightly into Levels 6-7):** consistent tense control
across a paragraph, and hedging/evaluative vocabulary (seems, tends to, a bit, at least, worth it, admittedly).
This is Level 5's own row in Section 0.4's bank, correctly anchored under the Lesson Generation Prompt's
row-selection rule: Level 5 is Advanced's own native Paragraph Composition floor, not Level 4 (which belongs to
Intermediate).

**Level 4's own required feature (taught in the same whole-class session, not a separate tier):** comparative
adjectives (-er / more) and "because" reason clauses. Grammar is a teaching moment, not a practice moment: the
teacher delivers one Grammar mini-lesson to the entire room, covering the hedging/tense-control content above and
this comparatives + because content back to back, in the same sitting. Every student in the room hears both
parts. It is only the controlled-practice activities and drafting task that follow (Section 0.4's existing
differentiation model) which split by Level, not the teaching input itself.

## Essay Focus A/B (Levels 6-7 centerpiece, Part 1 this lesson)

**Essay Focus A (this lesson):** the five-paragraph essay shape (introduction: hook + connecting information +
thesis; three topic-sentence-led body paragraphs; conclusion); the five hook types; thesis construction. **Level
6** builds a **direct** thesis. **Level 7** builds an **indirect** thesis, plus a stronger hook.

Per Section 0.4c's row-selection rule, Essay Focus A/B is anchored at Level 6's row (Level 6 is Advanced's own
native Essay Composition floor).


### Phase 1: Hook, Skill Spotlight (15 min)

**Hook (8 min):** Describe the two apartments briefly (quiet/smaller vs. bigger/cheaper/louder). Ask: "If a
friend needed a quiet place to work from home, which would you tell them about, and why?" Take 2-3 quick verbal
answers.

**Skill Spotlight (7 min):** "This week, everyone is practicing comparing two places and giving real reasons for
the comparison, in a way that lets a reader sense how you feel about each option, not just what the facts are."

### Phase 2: Focus A mini-lesson (30 min)

**Levels 4-5 (Grammar Focus A: tense control + hedging language):**

| Hedge word | What it does                            | Example                                           |
| ---------- | ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| seems      | softens a claim                         | Apartment A seems quieter at night.               |
| tends to   | describes a pattern, not a certainty    | Apartment B tends to get noisy around dinnertime. |
| a bit      | softens a description                   | The kitchen in Apartment A is a bit small.        |
| at least   | admits a downside while keeping a point | Apartment B is smaller, at least in the kitchen.  |
| worth it   | signals a trade-off is acceptable       | The extra rent is worth it for the quiet.         |

Tense control: keep one tense (past or present) for describing the same apartment within a paragraph; do not
drift between "is quiet" and "was quiet" describing the same, still-current fact without a reason to.
Board-dependent moment (Section 0.7): elicit one hedged sentence about the two apartments from the class
together (for example, "Apartment B tends to feel busier in the evening.").

**Continuing the same whole-class Grammar mini-lesson (8 min inside the 30):** comparative adjectives and
"because," taught to everyone in the room right after the hedging content above, not as a separate lesson for a
subset of students.

| Rule                                       | Example                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Short words: add -er                       | quiet -> quieter                                              |
| Words ending in -y: change y to i, add -er | noisy -> noisier, busy -> busier                              |
| Longer words: use "more"                   | affordable -> more affordable, spacious -> more spacious      |
| Irregular forms                            | good -> better, bad -> worse, far -> farther, little -> less |

Reason words (richer than "because" alone): "because" is Level 4's own required feature and every student hears
it, but three more reason words are taught alongside it so this content reads as genuinely Advanced, not
identical to Intermediate's own "because"-only lesson: **since** ("Since Apartment A is quieter, it's the better
fit for someone working from home"), **as** ("Apartment A is the better choice, as it's quieter than Apartment
B"), and **given that** ("Given that Sam works from home, Apartment A's quiet matters more than Apartment B's
extra space"). "Given that" already appears inside the Level 6 Mentor Essay on Day 2 as a register-shifting
device, so teaching it explicitly here on Day 1 gives Levels 6-7 a name for something they will otherwise only
meet unexplained inside a worked example.

**Levels 6-7 (Essay Focus A, Part 1: essay shape and hooks), running in parallel:**

Present the three-part essay shape: an **introduction** (hook, connecting information, thesis), a **body** (2-3
paragraphs, each with a topic sentence and supporting sentences), and a **conclusion** (restates the thesis;
closes with a suggestion, prediction, question, or opinion).

Present the five hook types with one short example each: a question ("Which matters more: extra space, or a
quiet place to work?"), an observation ("Two apartments a block apart can feel like they belong to different
cities."), a scenario ("At eleven at night, one of these buildings is silent."), a quote (used for a topic the
quote is not obviously "about"), a statistic ("Remote workers report losing nearly an hour of focus a day to
household and street noise."). Note the sample's warning: do not open by flatly stating the thesis.

Also present the direct/indirect thesis distinction here (teaching content must precede the practice that
depends on it, and Phase 3 below already asks students to identify and rewrite a thesis):

| Kind     | Example                                                                                                                         | What the reader knows                                               |
| -------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Direct   | "Apartment A and Apartment B differ most clearly in noise, cost, and space."                                                    | Exactly what the three body paragraphs will cover                   |
| Indirect | "Deciding between them turned out to be less about which apartment is better and more about which trade-off I could live with." | That there is a comparison and a decision, but not yet which points |

A brief sentence-level fold-in for Levels 6-7 (5 min inside the 30): the hedging table above applies inside
essays too, especially useful for a conclusion that leaves the reader with a considered, not overstated,
final impression.

### Phase 3: Controlled practice (30 min)

**Levels 4-5, three activities, centered on Focus A:**

**Activity 1: Fill in the blank with the best hedge word from the box (10 min).** Word bank: seems, tends to, a
bit, at least, worth it, admittedly.

> 1. Apartment A **\_\_\_** quieter than Apartment B, especially after dark.
> 2. Apartment B **\_\_\_** get noisier once the restaurants downstairs open for dinner.
> 3. The kitchen in Apartment A is **\_\_\_** small, but it's manageable.
> 4. Apartment B is smaller, **\_\_\_** in the closet space.
> 5. The extra rent for Apartment A is **\_\_\_** it for the quiet, in my opinion.
> 6. **\_\_\_**, Apartment B's location is hard to beat for a short commute.
> 7. Apartment A's street **\_\_\_** to be calm even on weekends.
> 8. The walls in Apartment A **\_\_\_** thick enough to block most noise.

**Activity 2: Choose the sentence that hints at an opinion without stating it directly (8 min).** For each pair,
circle the sentence that implies a preference rather than announcing one.

> 1. (a) I like Apartment A better. (b) Apartment A tends to feel calmer by evening.
> 2. (a) Apartment B is worse for sleep. (b) Apartment B's traffic noise seems to carry later than expected.
> 3. (a) The kitchen in Apartment A is a bit tight for two people. (b) I don't like Apartment A's kitchen.
> 4. (a) Apartment B's rent is worth it, at least for the extra space. (b) Apartment B is the better deal.
> 5. (a) Apartment A is my favorite. (b) Apartment A's quiet is worth the smaller kitchen, admittedly.
> 6. (a) Apartment B seems busier than the listing suggested. (b) Apartment B is too loud.

**Activity 3 (Level 4's own practice, 12 min): Fill in the blank with the correct comparative form.**

> 1. Apartment A is much **\_\_\_** (quiet) than Apartment B.
> 2. The kitchen in Apartment B is **\_\_\_** (big) than the one in Apartment A.
> 3. Apartment A is **\_\_\_** (safe) feeling at night than Apartment B's street.
> 4. Apartment B's rent is **\_\_\_** (affordable) than Apartment A's.
> 5. Apartment A's bedroom window is **\_\_\_** (bright) than Apartment B's.
> 6. Overall, Apartment A is **\_\_\_** (good) for someone who needs quiet.

**Levels 6-7, three parallel activities:**

**Essay analysis activity (12 min).** Read the short model essay below and answer: What is the essay's purpose?
How many paragraphs does it have, and what is each body paragraph's topic sentence? Is the thesis direct or
indirect?

> _Two Ways to Commute to Work_
>
> Every weekday, millions of people choose between two common ways to get to work: driving a car or riding a
> bike. Each option comes with its own advantages and drawbacks depending on distance, weather, and personal
> priorities. Driving and biking to work differ most clearly in travel time, cost, and effect on the rider's
> health.
>
> The first difference is travel time. For short distances under five miles, biking is often just as fast as
> driving once traffic and parking are factored in. For longer commutes, however, driving is almost always
> quicker, especially on highways where a bike cannot legally travel.
>
> The second difference is cost. A bike requires a one-time purchase and occasional maintenance, while a car
> involves fuel, insurance, and parking fees that add up every month. Over a year, a regular bike commuter can
> save thousands of dollars compared to a regular driver.
>
> The third difference is the effect on health. Biking to work builds regular exercise into the day without
> requiring a separate trip to the gym. Driving, by contrast, adds no physical activity at all and has been
> linked by researchers to more sedentary lifestyles overall.
>
> In the end, neither option is better in every situation. A short, dry commute favors the bike, while a long or
> weather-exposed one often favors the car. The right choice depends on what the rider values most: speed,
> savings, or health.

(Answer key: comparison essay; 5 paragraphs; body topic sentences are "The first difference is travel time," "The
second difference is cost," "The third difference is the effect on health"; thesis is direct.)

**Level 5 bridge activity (5 min, runs alongside the essay analysis activity above, on the same shared essay):**
Level 5 does not analyze the full essay the way Levels 6-7 do. Instead: find and underline the topic sentence of
the second body paragraph (the one about cost), then write, in your own words, one sentence saying what that
paragraph is mainly about. This is receptive only; Level 5's own required output today and in Lesson 3 stays a
single paragraph, unchanged.

**Hook-writing practice (10 min).** Write two candidate hooks for the essay above (it currently opens with
connecting information, not a hook) and compare with a partner.

**Thesis identify/rewrite practice, paired tiers:**

**Level 5's own tier (5 min):** read two sentences (a fresh direct/indirect pair, not the two already labeled in
the table above) and circle the one that is direct, underline the one that is indirect. Receptive only, no
rewriting.

**Levels 6-7 (8 min):** rewrite the commute essay's direct thesis as an indirect one.

### Phase 1 (20 min)

**Levels 4-5, Activity 4: editing a paragraph, 8 errors.**

> Apartment A seem quieter than Apartment B, at least when it come to the evenings. It tend to stay calm even on
> weekends, and the walls is thick enough that I never hear my neighbors. The kitchen were a bit small, but that
> don't bother me much. Apartment B were louder, and it's rent are lower, but the noise is worth avoiding if you
> ask me.

Verified errors (8): "Apartment A seem" -> "seems"; "it come to" -> "it comes to"; "It tend to" -> "It tends to";
"the walls is" -> "the walls are"; "The kitchen were" -> "The kitchen was"; "that don't bother" -> "that doesn't
bother"; "Apartment B were" -> "Apartment B was"; "it's rent are" -> "its rent is."

**Levels 6-7, Essay Focus A Part 2: thesis statement construction (20 min, application, not re-teaching, since
the direct/indirect table already lives on Day 1).** Briefly recall the direct/indirect table from Day 1 (a
one-line reminder, not a re-teach), then Level 6 drafts a direct thesis for their own essay today (a rough
version, refined in Lesson 3); Level 7 drafts an indirect one.

### Phase 2 (25 min)

**Levels 4-5, Activity 5 (choose the correct tense, 6 items) and Activity 6 (original sentences with 6 target
hedge words: seems, tends to, a bit, at least, worth it, admittedly).**

> Activity 5:
>
> 1. Apartment A (was / were) quieter than expected on move-in weekend.
> 2. Apartment B (has / have) always tended to feel busier in the evening.
> 3. The landlord at Apartment A (fix / fixes) problems quickly, and (has / have) for years.
> 4. Apartment B (don't / doesn't) seem to compare to Apartment A for quiet.
> 5. The rent at Apartment B (is / are) lower than at Apartment A, and (was / were) lower last year too.
> 6. Every evening, the street outside Apartment B (get / gets) a bit busier.

**Level 4's own practice (folded into Phase 2, 8 min): write two original sentences using "because" to give a
real reason for a comparison between Apartment A and Apartment B.**

**Levels 6-7, own version of Activity 6 (5 min inside the 25): write two sentences for their essay's conclusion,
each combining one hedge word from the box above with one reason word (because, since, as, given that), so the
sentence gives a real reason and still leaves a considered, not overstated, final impression.** Built from Day
1-2 content only (hedging plus reason words), not Lesson 2's concession subordinators (although, even though),
since this activity sits inside the same lesson as Day 1 and should not require content the class has not
reached yet.

**Levels 6-7, outlining practice (20 min).** Complete a general outline for "Two Ways to Commute to Work":
thesis, then each body paragraph's topic.

### Phase 3: Leveled Mentor Ladder walkthrough (30 min)

Present all four worked models together, in Level order, all on the shared Apartment A/Apartment B Scenario.

**Level 4 Mentor Text**

> My apartment, Apartment A, is quieter than Apartment B, the other place I looked at, and I really notice the
> difference at night. Apartment B is closer to the train station, so the commute would be easier from there. I
> chose Apartment A because it fits Sam's need for a quiet place to work from home. Apartment B has a bigger
> kitchen, but Apartment A has a better layout for two people. Overall, Apartment A is better for us because it
> fits what matters most to Sam.
>
> (5 sentences, 87 words: inside the 40-90 word, 3-6 sentence target in 0.2. Required feature present: one clear
> comparison, one explicit "because" reason, matching Level 4's own row even though today's centerpiece was
> hedging language.)

**Level 5 Mentor Text**

> Apartment A sits on the top floor of an older building, on a quiet residential street. Apartment B is bigger
> and closer to downtown, but the street outside never really goes quiet, even late at night. The walls in
> Apartment A seem thick enough that neither of us has ever heard a neighbor's television, while a friend who
> toured Apartment B mentioned hearing music through the wall on a weeknight. Apartment B is about a hundred
> dollars cheaper a month, which is worth something, and the extra space would be nice for guests. Still, I keep
> coming back to how still Apartment A feels at nine in the morning on a weekday, long after most people have
> already left for work. That's the kind of detail a listing never mentions, and it tends to matter more once
> you're actually living somewhere.
>
> (6 sentences, 141 words: inside the 90-150 word, 6-9 sentence target. Hedge words used, all from today's Focus
> A box: seem, worth, tends to. Implied attitude: a quiet preference for Apartment A, never stated directly.)

**Level 6 Mentor Essay**

> Which matters more when choosing a home: how much room you have, or how well you sleep at night? I toured two
> apartments this month, and they could not be more different in how they'd fit into a daily routine. Apartment A
> is smaller and set back on a quiet street. Apartment B is bigger, closer to downtown, and considerably cheaper.
> Since I know quiet matters to you more than almost anything else, I want to walk you through exactly how the
> two compare. Apartment A and Apartment B differ most clearly in three ways: how quiet each one is, how much
> each one costs, and how much space each one offers.
>
> The first difference is noise. Apartment A sits on the top floor of a converted brownstone, on a residential
> street that empties out by early evening. Apartment B faces a busier avenue, and even with the windows closed,
> the traffic and nearby restaurants are noticeable well into the night. Given that you'd likely be working from
> home most days, this difference alone might decide it: Apartment A offers the kind of quiet that Apartment B,
> however much space it has, simply cannot.
>
> The second difference is cost. Apartment B runs about a hundred dollars less per month than Apartment A, which
> adds up over a full year's lease. In fact, if the rent alone were the deciding factor, Apartment B would be the
> clear choice.
>
> The third difference is space. Apartment B has a larger kitchen and a second closet that Apartment A does not
> have. On the other hand, Apartment A's layout keeps the bedroom separated from the living room by a short
> hallway, which does double duty as extra sound buffering.
>
> In the end, Apartment A and Apartment B each win on a different measure: Apartment B on cost and space,
> Apartment A on quiet. Since quiet is the one thing you told me you're not willing to compromise on, I'd lean
> toward Apartment A, but I wanted you to see the full comparison before deciding.
>
> (5 paragraphs, 340 words: inside the 300-450 word target. Direct thesis. Hook: a question. Sentence-level fold-
> in from today's Focus A/B: "given that," "in fact," "on the other hand" function as the same kind of
> register-shifting device the hedging/concession table sets up for this Level.)

**Level 7 Mentor Essay**

> At eleven o'clock on a Tuesday night, one of these apartments is completely silent, the kind of quiet where you
> can hear the refrigerator cycle on in the next room. The other has a bus idling outside its window, headlights
> sweeping the ceiling every few minutes until well past midnight. I toured two apartments this month, and
> touring them back to back, on the same afternoon, made their differences impossible to ignore in a way that
> reading two listings side by side never quite manages. Admittedly, neither one is perfect, and each asks for a
> different kind of compromise, the sort that only becomes obvious once you've actually stood in both rooms.
> Deciding between them turned out to be less about which apartment is objectively better, since on paper they
> are closer than you'd expect, and more about which trade-off I could actually live with once the novelty of
> moving wore off.
>
> Apartment A's silence is the kind you only notice by its absence elsewhere, the way a room feels different once
> you realize the hum you'd been hearing has finally stopped. ~~It's really quiet.~~ The building sits far enough
> off the avenue that a passing ambulance on a weekend night barely registers, and the bedroom, tucked behind a
> short hallway, seems to absorb whatever noise does get through before it reaches the pillow. What's more, the
> quiet held up even during a Saturday afternoon when the building's other units all seemed to be home at once,
> doors opening and closing, someone's radio drifting up from a lower floor, none of it carrying the way I
> expected it to.
>
> Apartment B, by contrast, asks for a different kind of patience. The extra room is real: a second closet, a
> kitchen you could actually cook in without turning sideways, a living room wide enough for the couch we already
> own instead of one we'd have to replace. So is the noise, which never quite disappears, only softens between
> about two and six in the morning before starting up again with the first delivery trucks. This suggests
> something the listing photos won't show: that square footage and stillness rarely come from the same building,
> and a floor plan that photographs well is not the same promise as a night that actually lets you sleep through
> it.
>
> While the rent gap between the two is real enough to matter, running to roughly a hundred dollars a month once
> you account for parking, it is not, on its own, the kind of number that should decide something this size. A
> hundred dollars a month is worth noticing, worth budgeting around, even worth a second look at the numbers
> before signing anything. It is not worth trading away the one thing that actually determines whether working
> from home feels possible five days a week, particularly on the mornings when a closed door and an ordinary
> amount of quiet turn out to be the entire difference between a productive day and a wasted one.
>
> I already know which set of keys I'd rather be handed, and I suspect I knew it before I'd finished walking
> through the second apartment. What I don't know yet is whether saying so out loud will sound like I've made
> this decision for both of us before you've even seen either place yourself.
>
> (5 paragraphs, 549 words (crossed-out draft sentence excluded from the count): inside the 450-650 word target.
> Indirect thesis. Hook: a scenario/anecdote. Sophisticated cohesion used: admittedly, what's more, this
> suggests. Concession/counter structure: "while the rent gap... it is not, on its own..." Self-revision
> demonstration: "It's really quiet." crossed out and replaced. Show students both the crossed-out and
> replacement text when presenting this Mentor Essay.)

**Board-dependent moment (Section 0.7):** co-construct live a "what changes at each Level" board: "Level 4 gives
one reason. Level 5 hints at how the writer feels without saying it. Level 6 turns the comparison into a full
essay that talks straight to Sam. Level 7 makes the essay itself feel like a decision the writer is still working
through."
