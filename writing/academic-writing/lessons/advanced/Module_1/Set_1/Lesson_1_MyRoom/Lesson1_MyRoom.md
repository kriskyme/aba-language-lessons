## What this lesson establishes for the Set

**Scenario.** Every student describes their own room from memory, comparing it with what it was before, with a
sibling's or friend's room, or with another room at home. The room is a subject, not a prop: nothing is brought,
drawn, or looked at. The Mentor Ladder's models are written for Leo, a cousin coming to stay who wants somewhere
quiet to study, so the reader-and-concern shape of the Set is visible from Lesson 1. Lesson 2 Day 2 introduces
the draft object (the student's home compared with one place to study from a printed list) and its named
reader, Dana.

**Grammar Focus A (Level 5 row, the native Paragraph Composition Level): consistent tense across a paragraph,
and hedging and evaluative language** (seems, tends to, a bit, at least, fairly, mostly, worth it). **Level 4's
row, folded into the same mini-lesson:** comparative adjectives (-er / more, irregular better, worse, less) and
reason connectors (because, since, as, given that), with the comma after an opening reason clause. Delivered to
the whole room in one sitting; differentiation lives in the practice.

**Essay Focus B plus the working thesis (Level 6 row), the parallel track for Levels 6-7:** this Module drafts
the body paragraphs of the Pair's essay, so it teaches the body paragraph (a topic sentence stating one point,
then sentences that support it) and the working thesis the bodies serve, direct at Level 6 (naming the points)
and indirect at Level 7 (Conventions §C). The five-paragraph shape, hook types, the introduction, and the
conclusion are Module 2's Essay Focus A; they are named once here so students know what is coming, and not
practiced. Level 5 gets a receptive bridge on the same model body paragraphs.

**Word Bank for the room** (printed in both units of the packet; the groups are refilled for the draft object at
Lesson 2 Day 2):

- Light and sound: bright, dim, sunny, quiet, noisy, calm
- Space: small, roomy, narrow, cramped, open, shared
- Condition: tidy, messy, bare, crowded, fresh, worn
- Feel: cozy, cold, warm, plain, busy, still
- Use: a desk, a bed, a shelf, a window, a lamp, a door that closes

### Phase 1: Hook, Skill Spotlight (15 min)

**Hook (8 min):** Ask two volunteers to describe their own room in one phrase each, and write the two phrases on
the board ("small and sunny," "big but dark"). Ask the class: "Which room would you rather study in? Why?" Take
2-3 quick oral answers, and push each toward a reason ("because it is quieter," "since the window faces the
street"). Keep the language near Level 5.

**Skill Spotlight (7 min):** "This week we're practicing describing something of our own by comparing it to
something else and giving a real reason for the comparison." One sentence, the same for every Level; the
essay Levels' own features (a named reader, an implication) are stated inside their tasks, not here. Say that
the Closing Transfer Check at the end of Lesson 4 will ask everyone to do exactly this on something new.

### Phase 2: Focus A mini-lesson, rule and examples (30 min)

**Grammar box: Keeping one tense, and softening what you say.** (One box, whole class.)

Rule 1, tense: a description stays in one tense unless the time changes. Present for how the room is now, past
for how it was. Mixing them inside one sentence about one time is the error to hunt.

| Keep it | Not this |
|---|---|
| My room is small, and it gets the morning sun. | My room is small, and it got the morning sun. |
| Last year the room was bare, and it felt cold. | Last year the room was bare, and it feels cold. |
| It used to be my brother's room, so the walls are still blue. | It used to be my brother's room, so the walls were still blue. |

Rule 2, hedging and evaluating: these words tell the reader how sure you are, or how much something matters,
without a feeling word.

| Word | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| seems / seems to | what it looks like, not a fact | The room seems bigger with the desk by the window. |
| tends to | what usually happens | It tends to get noisy after six. |
| a bit / fairly / mostly | how much | The shelf is a bit crowded. The floor is mostly clear. |
| at least | the one good thing in a mixed picture | It is cold in winter, but at least it is quiet. |
| worth it | a judgment | The small desk is worth it for the light. |

**Level 4's row, folded in (the same box, second half):** comparing and giving a reason.

| Comparative | Example |
|---|---|
| short adjective + -er | quieter, smaller, brighter, warmer |
| long adjective: more + adjective | more crowded, more comfortable |
| irregular | good: better; bad: worse; little: less |

| Reason word | Example |
|---|---|
| because | I study at the desk because the lamp is there. |
| since | Since the door closes, I can shut out the TV. |
| as | I keep the window open, as the room gets warm. |
| given that | Given that my brother shares it, the room is never really quiet. |

"Since" and "given that" can open the sentence; put a comma after that opening clause.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** on a fresh prompt (the classroom itself), build one sentence
with the class that uses a hedge and a reason: "This room seems bright in the morning, because the windows
face east." Write the class's version, not a prepared one; underline the hedge and box the reason word.

**Essay Focus B and the working thesis, Levels 6-7 (folded into the same 30 minutes; Levels 4-5 hear it and are
not tested on it):**

**Essay-structure box: Inside a body paragraph, and the working thesis.**

1. A body paragraph opens with a topic sentence that states its one point. Every sentence after it supports
   that point: an example, a detail, a reason, or a contrast. A sentence that supports a different point belongs
   in a different paragraph.
2. The body paragraphs serve one thesis. In this Module you write the thesis as a **working thesis**, one line at
   the top of your outline and your draft; the introduction that carries it, and the conclusion, are written in
   the next Module, around the bodies you keep.
3. A **direct** working thesis names the points the bodies will make (Level 6): "My room is a better place to
   study than it was last year for two reasons: it is quieter, and the desk finally has light." An **indirect**
   working thesis signals that points exist without naming them (Level 7): "My room has changed more than the
   furniture would suggest."
4. What is coming, not practiced here: the finished essay has an introduction (a hook, connecting sentences,
   the thesis last) and a conclusion (the thesis in new words, then a closing move). Those are Module 2's.

### Phase 3: Controlled practice A, part 1 (30 min)

Two tracks run at once; the teacher circulates between them.

**Levels 4-5, Activity 1 (classify, 8 items):** eight sentences about a room; each either keeps one tense and
uses a hedge or comparative correctly, or breaks the rule. **Level 4:** mark each sentence C (a comparison), R
(a reason), or X (a tense mistake), and underline the comparative or reason word. **Level 5 (heavier tier, a
different shape):** for the six correct sentences, write the hedge's job in one word (looks, usually, amount,
one-good-thing, judgment); for the two broken ones, write the corrected verb. Items:

1. My room is quieter than the kitchen. (C)
2. I moved the desk because the old spot was dark. (R)
3. The window seems bigger now that the curtains are gone. (C; hedge: looks)
4. The room tends to get warm by noon, so I open the door. (hedge: usually)
5. It is a bit messy this week, and it was fine last week. (correct; hedge: amount)
6. Last winter the room was cold, and it feels damp too. (X: felt)
7. Since the walls are thin, I hear the street at night. (R)
8. The bed takes half the room, but at least the desk fit. (X: fits; hedge: one-good-thing)

Answer key: 1 C; 2 R; 3 C; 4 hedge; 5 hedge; 6 X (felt); 7 R; 8 X (fits) plus hedge.

**Levels 4-5, Activity 2 (fill in a model paragraph, 8 blanks):** a paragraph about a room with the verbs and
hedges blanked; each blank has the base verb or a hedge choice in brackets. **Level 4:** fill the verbs so the
paragraph stays in one tense and add one comparative where the bracket asks for it. **Level 5:** the same, and
choose the hedge in three bracketed pairs that fits the sentence's job (seems / is; tends to / always; a bit /
very).

> My room (1. be: ___) the smallest in the flat, but it (2. get: ___) the most light. The desk (3. sit: ___) under
> the window, so the mornings (4. be: ___) bright and the afternoons (5. seem / are: ___) long. It (6. tends to /
> always: ___) get noisy when my brother comes home, and the shelf is (7. a bit / very: ___) crowded. Even so, it
> is (8. comparative of good: ___) than the room I had last year.

Key: 1 is; 2 gets; 3 sits; 4 are; 5 seem (looks-like job); 6 tends to; 7 a bit; 8 better.

**Levels 6-7, Activity 3 (body-paragraph analysis):** read the model body paragraphs of "My Room, Then and Now"
(printed in the packet under their working thesis line; an original model on the practice object, written for a
cousin, Leo, who wants somewhere quiet to study). **Level 6:** name the purpose in one line, count the body
paragraphs, say whether the working thesis is direct or indirect, and copy each body paragraph's topic sentence.
**Level 7 (heavier tier, a different shape):** the same reading, then name which single detail carries the
writer's attitude toward the change without stating it, and say in one line what a reader is meant to infer
from it.

**Level 5 bridge (receptive, 5 minutes inside Activity 2's block):** on the same model essay, find and underline
one body paragraph's topic sentence and write one sentence saying what that paragraph is about. Level 5's own
output stays one paragraph; this is exposure, not a task Level.

**Levels 6-7, Activity 4 (working thesis):** the packet prints two body-paragraph topics about a room ("the
noise, which is less than it was" and "the light, which reaches the desk now"). **Level 6:** write a direct
working thesis that names both points, then say in one line which point would come first and why. **Level 7 (a
different shape):** write the same thesis as an indirect one, then say in one line what it withholds that the
bodies will supply.

**Model body paragraphs, "My Room, Then and Now"** (three body paragraphs, about 210 words, printed in the packet
under their working thesis line for Activity 3 and the Level 5 bridge; not a Mentor Essay; the introduction and
conclusion are modeled in Module 2):

> Working thesis: My room is a better place to study than it was a year ago for two reasons: it is quieter, and
> the desk finally has light.
>
> The first change is the noise. A year ago the desk stood against the wall I share with the living room, so
> every evening the TV came through the plaster. Now the desk is under the window on the far wall. The street
> outside tends to be quiet after eight, and the wall by the window is the thick outside wall. It is not silent,
> but it is fairly close.
>
> The second change is the light. The old spot was dark by three in the afternoon, and I read with the lamp on
> all day. Under the window the desk gets the morning sun, which is a bit strong in June but worth it the rest
> of the year. I still use the lamp, but only at night.
>
> You will want to know about the door, Leo, since you said you cannot work with a door open. It sticks, and it
> takes a shove, but it closes, and when it is closed the room is the quietest one in the flat.

Teacher notes for Activity 3: purpose, to describe the room for Leo so he can decide where to study; three body
paragraphs; the working thesis is direct (two points); topic sentences, "The first change is the noise," "The
second change is the light," "You will want to know about the door, Leo"; the register shift is the third
paragraph's direct address; Level 7's attitude-carrying detail is "I still use the lamp, but only at night"
(the writer is pleased and is understating it), or "it takes a shove" (the door is a real flaw the writer plays
down). Activity 4: any direct thesis naming noise and light; an indirect version such as "The room has changed
in two ways nobody sees from the door."

**Controlled-practice answer keys** live here in the Markdown, not in the packet.

### Phase 1: Editing a paragraph on Focus A (20 min)

One paragraph about a room, printed in the packet with **8 verified errors** (tense consistency, comparatives,
hedges used as facts, a missing comma after an opening reason clause). **Levels 4-6:** find and correct the eight.
**Level 7 (heavier tier, a different shape):** the same eight, then rewrite the two sentences marked with an
asterisk so that they connect with a cohesive device (however, in fact, what's more) instead of "and."

> My room is smaller than my sister's room but it is the more bright one. It gets the sun in the morning and it
> got shade after lunch. Since the window faces the yard the room seems quiet at night. Last year it was the
> worse room in the flat, because the heater did not work.* Now the heater works and the room is warmer. It
> tends to be a bit crowded, and it always is tidy on Sundays.* The desk is more good than the old one, and it
> fits under the window. I think it is worth it to keep the bed by the door.

The eight errors: (1) "the more bright one" -> brighter; (2) "got shade" -> gets shade; (3) missing comma after
"Since the window faces the yard,"; (4) "the worse room" -> the worst room (or "worse than the others"); (5)
"more good" -> better; (6) "it always is tidy" -> it is always tidy (a tense-and-hedge sentence that contradicts
"tends to": accept "it is usually tidy"); (7) "smaller than my sister's room but" -> comma before "but"; (8) "I
think it is worth it" -> "it is worth it" (a hedge does not need "I think" stacked on it; accept either
removal). Count checked: 8.

### Phase 2: Original sentences with Focus A / outlining (25 min)

**Level 4, Activity 5:** write one original sentence about your own room for each of these six items:
quieter, more comfortable, better, because, since, given that. Each sentence names your room and one other room
or its earlier self.

**Level 5, Activity 6 (a different shape):** write five sentences about your own room that together stay in one
tense, using each hedge once: seems, tends to, a bit, at least, worth it. Then underline the one sentence a
reader could use to guess how you feel about the room, without a feeling word in it.

**Level 6, Activity 7 (outlining):** write a general outline of the body paragraphs of an essay about your room
for a reader who wants quiet: the working thesis (direct, two points), and one topic sentence per body
paragraph.

**Level 7, Activity 8 (a different shape):** write a specific outline for one body paragraph about your room:
the topic sentence, then three supporting details in the order you would use them, with one detail chosen to
imply your attitude toward the room rather than state it. Then write the indirect working thesis the body
paragraphs would serve.

### Phase 3: Leveled Mentor Ladder walkthrough (30 min, teacher-presented, never printed)

Present the four models in Level order on the board or by reading aloud. Every model describes the writer's
room for Leo, a cousin who wants somewhere quiet to study. The Level 6 and 7 models are body paragraphs under a
working thesis line, the part this Module drafts; Module 2's second look adds their introductions and
conclusions. Students identify which model is their own Level's and the one directly above it. Nothing about
the ladder appears in the packet.

**Level 4 Mentor Text** (5 sentences, 74 words):

> My room is quieter than it was a year ago. The desk used to sit against the living-room wall, and I heard the
> TV every night. Now it is under the window, because the outside wall is thick. The room is still small, and
> the bed takes half of it. For a month of studying, though, the quiet matters more than the space.

Required feature: one comparison ("quieter than") and one explicit reason ("because the outside wall is thick").
Comparative, "because," one compound sentence with "and." Simple present with one past sentence for the earlier
time.

**Level 5 Mentor Text** (8 sentences, 128 words):

> My room is the smallest in the flat, and it is the one I would offer you. It gets the morning sun through a
> single window, and the desk sits right under it. The room tends to be quiet after eight, since the street
> outside empties early. It is a bit crowded: the bed, the desk, and one shelf leave a narrow path to the door.
> The door sticks, but it closes, and a closed door is worth a lot in this flat. The walls are plain, and the
> floor is mostly clear. I still do my own reading at the kitchen table. At least you will have the desk to
> yourself in the mornings.

Details in order: light, sound, space, the door, the walls and floor, then the writer's own habit. Hedges: tends
to, a bit, worth, mostly, at least. The implied attitude: "I still do my own reading at the kitchen table" lets
the reader infer the writer does not love the room, without a feeling word. One tense throughout.

**Level 6 Mentor Essay, this Module's part** (three body paragraphs under a direct working thesis, about 250
words; a sentence that says why quiet matters to Leo; one register shift):

> Working thesis: For a month of studying my room is the right choice for two reasons: it is the quietest room
> we have, and it has the only desk with real light.
>
> The quiet comes from where the room sits. It is at the end of the hall, away from the kitchen and the living
> room, and its only shared wall is the thick one on the outside of the building. Traffic on our street tends to
> die down by eight, and the neighbors above us are an older couple who go to bed early. You said you cannot
> study with a TV in the background; in this room you will not hear one.
>
> The light comes from the window, which faces east. The desk sits directly under it, so the mornings are bright
> enough to read without the lamp, and the afternoons are soft rather than dark. The sun is a bit strong in June,
> but a curtain fixes that, and it is worth it the rest of the year.
>
> Now, the honest part. The room is small: the bed, the desk, and one shelf leave a narrow path to the door. And
> the door sticks. It closes, but it takes a shove, and you will hear that shove from the hall. If you need to
> come and go quietly at night, the shove is the one thing to plan around.

Required feature: "You said you cannot study with a TV in the background; in this room you will not hear one"
states why quiet matters to Leo; "Now, the honest part" is the register shift addressing Leo's concern. Direct
working thesis naming two points; topic sentences open each body paragraph; hedges and one concession ("It
closes, but it takes a shove") inside sustained complex sentences. No introduction and no conclusion: Module 2
adds them.

**Level 7 Mentor Essay, this Module's part** (three body paragraphs under an indirect working thesis, about 330
words; an implication built from selection and emphasis; visible self-revision):

> Working thesis: The room has changed less than the furniture would suggest.
>
> The desk moved from the living-room wall to the window, which is the change everyone notices, and it is a real
> one: the outside wall is thick, and the street tends to empty by eight, so the evenings are quieter than they
> were. What's more, the window faces east, and the desk now catches the first light. On paper this is a study.
> ~~It is a good place to work.~~ It is a reasonable place to work, in the mornings, if you get there before I do.
>
> The bed, admittedly, has not moved. It could not; it fills the wall it stands against, and it takes half the
> floor. Every path in the room bends around it. The shelf beside it holds what I actually reach for at night,
> which is not, if I am honest, my textbooks. The lamp is on the bed side of the room, not the desk side, and
> it has been there since long before the desk moved.
>
> Then there is the door. It sticks, and it takes a shove, and the shove carries down the hall. In a flat where
> everyone is home by seven, that shove is the sound of someone giving up on the living room. I have heard it
> from the kitchen more often than I have made it myself. This suggests something about who uses the room to
> study and who uses it to sleep, and I will leave you to draw the line.

Required feature: the implication (the writer studies elsewhere and the room is really for sleeping) is never
stated; it is built from the selection and emphasis of detail (the bed, the shelf's contents, where the lamp
is, who makes the shove). Indirect working thesis. Visible self-revision: the struck sentence "It is a good
place to work" replaced in place by the hedged version. Sophisticated cohesion: what's more, admittedly, this
suggests. No introduction and no conclusion: Module 2 adds a hook of the anecdote or quote type and a closing
move.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** a "what changes at each Level" board built from student
observations after each model ("Level 4 compares and gives one reason. Level 5 puts details in order and lets us
guess. Level 6 talks to Leo and says why quiet matters to him. Level 7 makes us work out what the room is for.
Levels 6 and 7 have no beginning or ending yet; that is next Module's job").
The teacher writes the class's words, not a prepared list.
