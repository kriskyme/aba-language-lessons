Grammar: Keeping One Tense, and Softening What You Say

One tense at a time

A description stays in one tense unless the time changes: present for how the room is now, past for how it was.

Keep itNot this
My room is small, and it gets the morning sun.My room is small, and it got the morning sun.
Last year the room was bare, and it felt cold.Last year the room was bare, and it feels cold.
It used to be my brother's room, so the walls are still blue.It used to be my brother's room, so the walls were still blue.

Softening and judging

These words tell the reader how sure you are, or how much something matters, without a feeling word.

WordJobExample
seems / seems towhat it looks like, not a factThe room seems bigger with the desk by the window.
tends towhat usually happensIt tends to get noisy after six.
a bit / fairly / mostlyhow muchThe shelf is a bit crowded. The floor is mostly clear.
at leastthe one good thing in a mixed pictureIt is cold in winter, but at least it is quiet.
worth ita judgmentThe small desk is worth it for the light.

Comparing, and giving a reason

ComparativeExample
short adjective + -erquieter, smaller, brighter, warmer
long adjective: more + adjectivemore crowded, more comfortable
irregulargood: better; bad: worse; little: less
Reason wordExample
becauseI study at the desk because the lamp is there.
sinceSince the door closes, I can shut out the TV.
asI keep the window open, as the room gets warm.
given thatGiven that my brother shares it, the room is never really quiet.

"Since" and "given that" can open the sentence. Put a comma after that opening part: Since the door closes, I can shut out the TV.

Inside a Body Paragraph, and the Working Thesis

One point per paragraph, one thesis for all of them

1. A body paragraph opens with a topic sentence that states its one point. Every sentence after it supports that point: an example, a detail, a reason, or a contrast. A sentence that supports a different point belongs in a different paragraph.

2. The working thesis is one line at the top of your outline and your draft that the body paragraphs serve. The beginning and the ending of the essay are written in the next unit, around the paragraphs you keep.

A direct working thesis names the points the paragraphs will make: "My room is a better place to study than it was last year for two reasons: it is quieter, and the desk finally has light."

An indirect working thesis signals that points exist without naming them: "My room has changed more than the furniture would suggest."

Model body paragraphs: My Room, Then and Now

Working thesis: My room is a better place to study than it was a year ago for two reasons: it is quieter, and the desk finally has light.

The first change is the noise. A year ago the desk stood against the wall I share with the living room, so every evening the TV came through the plaster. Now the desk is under the window on the far wall. The street outside tends to be quiet after eight, and the wall by the window is the thick outside wall. It is not silent, but it is fairly close.

The second change is the light. The old spot was dark by three in the afternoon, and I read with the lamp on all day. Under the window the desk gets the morning sun, which is a bit strong in June but worth it the rest of the year. I still use the lamp, but only at night.
