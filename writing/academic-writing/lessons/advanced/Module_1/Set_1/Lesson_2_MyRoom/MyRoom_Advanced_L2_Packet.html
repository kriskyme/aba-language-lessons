Grammar: Joining Both Sides of a Comparison

Two ways to put both sides in one sentence

StructureExampleComma
Compound: two full sentences joined by and, but, soMy room is small, but it is quiet.Before the connector
Complex: although / even though / while + clauseAlthough my room is small, it is quiet.After the opening clause
Complex, clause secondMy room is quiet even though it is small.No comma

"But" states the contrast. "Although" and "even though" tell the reader which side matters less. "While" sets the two side by side.

A softening word often lives inside a concession: Even though it is a bit cramped, the desk is worth it.

A contrast can also open a new sentence: However, the door sticks. On the other hand, it closes. In fact, it is the quietest room we have.

Inside a Body Paragraph

Topic sentence, then support

A body paragraph opens with a topic sentence that states its one point. Every sentence after it supports that point: an example, a detail, a reason, or a contrast.

A sentence that supports a different point belongs in a different paragraph.

A general outline lists the working thesis and one topic sentence per body paragraph. A specific outline adds the supporting details under each topic sentence, in order. The beginning and the ending of the essay are written in the next unit, around the paragraphs you keep.

Two short supporting sentences can often be combined into one with a concession or a connecting phrase, so the paragraph reads as one movement instead of a list.

A body paragraph from a model essay

The first change is the noise. A year ago the desk stood against the wall I share with the living room, so every evening the TV came through the plaster. Now the desk is under the window on the far wall. The street outside tends to be quiet after eight, and the wall by the window is the thick outside wall. It is not silent, but it is fairly close.
