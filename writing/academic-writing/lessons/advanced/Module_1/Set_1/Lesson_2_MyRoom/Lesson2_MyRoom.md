## What this lesson carries forward and adds

**Scenario.** Day 1 stays on the practice object (my room). Day 2 introduces the draft object: the student's
home compared with one place to study from the printed list (the library, a cafe, a classroom after school, a
park, a friend's place), written for Dana, a classmate who needs quiet to study for exams and is deciding where
to study this month. From Lesson 3 on, every piece is about the draft object only.

while), with the comma after an opening concession clause. **Level 4's row, folded in:** simple vs. compound

## DAY 1: GRAMMAR FOCUS B, SENTENCE VARIETY (75 MIN)

```
| Focus B mini-lesson | Controlled practice B         | quiet/quite +            |
| (+ Essay Focus B)   | (two tracks)                  | mixed editing            |
```

### Phase 1: Focus B mini-lesson, rule and examples (20 min)

**Grammar box: Joining both sides of a comparison.** (One box, whole class.)

Yesterday you compared two things and gave a reason. Today you put the two sides in one sentence, so the reader
sees the trade-off.

| Structure | Example | Comma |
|---|---|---|
| Compound: two full sentences joined by and, but, so | My room is small, but it is quiet. | Before the connector |
| Complex: although / even though / while + clause | Although my room is small, it is quiet. | After the opening clause |
| Complex, clause second | My room is quiet even though it is small. | No comma |

Which to choose: "but" states the contrast; "although" and "even though" tell the reader which side matters
less; "while" sets the two side by side without ranking them. A concession is where a hedge often lives: "Even
though it is a bit cramped, the desk is worth it."

Level 6 sentence craft, named inside the same box: a contrast can also open a new sentence with a
register-shifting device: "However, the door sticks." "On the other hand, it closes." "In fact, it is the
quietest room we have." These are for the essay Levels' paragraphs; everyone hears them once.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** take yesterday's board sentence about the classroom ("This
room seems bright in the morning, because the windows face east") and build its concession with the class:
"Although the windows face east, the room is dim by three." Write the class's version.

**Essay Focus B, Levels 6-7 (inside the same 20 minutes):**

**Essay-structure box: Inside a body paragraph.** A body paragraph opens with a topic sentence that states its
one point. Every sentence after it supports that point: an example, a detail, a reason, or a contrast. A
sentence that supports a different point belongs in a different paragraph. A general outline lists the working
thesis and one topic sentence per body paragraph; a specific outline adds the supporting details under each
topic sentence, in order. The introduction and conclusion are written in the next Module, around the bodies you
keep. Level 7: two short supporting sentences can often be combined into one with a concession or
a cohesive device, which makes the paragraph read as one movement instead of a list.

### Phase 2: Controlled practice B (30 min)

**Level 4, Activity 1 (identify, 8 items):** write S next to each simple sentence and CD next to each compound
sentence about a room; add the missing comma where a compound sentence lacks one. Items:

1. My room is small and bright. (S)
2. My room is small, but it gets the sun. (CD)
3. The desk is under the window so the mornings are bright. (CD, add comma)
4. I closed the door and started reading. (S)
5. The walls are thin, so I hear the street. (CD)
6. The shelf is crowded but the floor is clear. (CD, add comma)
7. My brother and I shared this room for years. (S)
8. It is cold in winter, and it is warm in June. (CD)

**Level 5, Activity 2 (identify, a different shape, 8 items):** each sentence joins two ideas; write CON if the
joining word concedes (tells the reader which side matters less) or SIDE if it only sets the two ideas side by
side; then underline the side the writer cares about more in each CON sentence. Items:

1. Although the room is small, it is the quietest in the flat. (CON: quietest)
2. The room is small, and the window is narrow. (SIDE)
3. Even though the door sticks, it closes. (CON: it closes)
4. While my sister's room is bigger, mine gets more light. (CON: more light)
5. The bed is by the door, and the desk is by the window. (SIDE)
6. The heater works now, though it is a bit loud. (CON: works now)
7. The walls are plain, but the shelf is full. (SIDE, accept CON with "full" if argued)
8. Even though it is cramped, the desk is worth it. (CON: worth it)

**Levels 4-5, Activity 3 (combine, 8 pairs):** combine each pair of short sentences about a room into one.
**Level 4:** with and, but, or so, choosing the connector that fits the meaning, comma in place. **Level 5 (a
different shape):** with although, even though, or while, putting the side that matters less in the concession
clause, comma where needed. Pairs:

1. My room is small. It is quiet.
2. The window faces east. The mornings are bright.
3. The door sticks. It closes.
4. The shelf is crowded. The floor is clear.
5. The walls are thin. I hear the street at night.
6. The heater is loud. The room is warm.
7. My sister's room is bigger. Mine gets more light.
8. The desk is old. It fits under the window.

Key for Level 4: 1 but / and; 2 so; 3 but; 4 but; 5 so; 6 but; 7 but; 8 but. Key for Level 5: any concession
placing the lesser side in the although-clause (1 Although my room is small, it is quiet; 3 Even though the door
sticks, it closes; 6 Although the heater is loud, the room is warm; 7 While my sister's room is bigger, mine
gets more light).

**Level 6, Activity 4 (topic and supporting sentences):** on the printed model body paragraph, label its
sentences T (topic) or S (support), then write one more supporting sentence of your own that fits the
paragraph's point (the noise), and one sentence that would not fit it, and say in one line where the misfit
would go instead.

**Level 7, Activity 5 (a different shape, combining for cohesion):** the packet prints three short supporting
sentences about a room ("The desk is under the window. The window faces east. The mornings are bright enough to
read without the lamp."). Combine them into two sentences using one concession or one cohesive device (what's
more, in fact, admittedly), then write the topic sentence they would sit under.

**Level 6 sentence craft (heavier tier inside Activity 3's minutes, 3 items):** rewrite Level 5's pairs 3, 6,
and 7 as two sentences each, the second opening with however, on the other hand, or in fact.

### Phase 3: Confusable pair and mixed editing (25 min)

**Activity 6, all Levels (forced choice, 8 items):** write quiet or quite in each gap. Items 7 and 8 have two
gaps each.

1. My room is ___ small. (quite)
2. It is the ___ one in the flat. (quiet)
3. The street is ___ busy in the morning. (quite)
4. After eight it goes ___. (quiet)
5. The heater is ___ loud, so I turn it off to read. (quite)
6. Keep your voice ___ in the hall. (quiet)
7. The library is ___, and it is ___ far from my house. (quiet; quite)
8. A ___ room is ___ hard to find at home. (quiet; quite)

**Activity 7, Levels 4-6 (mixed editing, 8 errors):** a paragraph about a room with 8 errors mixing Focus A
(tense, comparatives, hedges) and Focus B (comma in a compound sentence; comma after an opening concession
clause; quiet/quite). **Level 7 (a different shape):** the same 8, then combine the two sentences marked with an
asterisk into one sentence with a concession.

> My room is quiet small but it is the quietest one we have. Although the walls are thin the street goes quite
> after eight. The desk is more better under the window than it was by the door. It got the morning sun, and it
> stays bright until noon.* I think it seems bigger now.* The heater is loud even though, it works. The shelf is
> a bit crowded and the floor was mostly clear. At least the door closes, so I can shut out the TV.

The eight errors: (1) "quiet small" -> quite small; (2) missing comma before "but"; (3) missing comma after
"Although the walls are thin,"; (4) "goes quite" -> goes quiet; (5) "more better" -> better; (6) "It got the
morning sun" -> gets; (7) "I think it seems" -> it seems (stacked hedge); (8) "even though, it works" -> the
comma belongs after the clause when it opens, none when it follows: "The heater is loud, even though it works"
is also wrong in meaning; accept "Even though the heater is loud, it works." Count checked: 8. A ninth trap,
"the floor was mostly clear," is a tense slip students may also catch; credit it, but the stated count is
eight (the "was" is corrected under error 6's rule if they take both).

## DAY 2: TASK-LADDER PRACTICE AND PREWRITING (75 MIN)

```

| Required-feature        | Prewriting: the draft object  | Prewriting share +  |
| warm-up (my room)       | (two places to study)         | board synthesis     |
```

### Phase 1: Required-feature warm-up on the practice object (25 min)

Each Level rehearses only its own required feature, about the student's own room, using both focuses.

- **Level 4, Activity 8:** write two comparison sentences about your room (compared with another room at home,
  or with your room a year ago) and two reason sentences, each with a different reason word (because, since,
  as, given that); then join one comparison to its reason with "so" or "but," comma in place.
- **Level 5, Activity 9 (a different shape):** write two sentences about your room, each with one concrete,
  specific detail and no feeling word (no love, hate, like, favorite, boring). A partner reads them and says in
  one word how you feel about the room. If the partner cannot guess, sharpen the detail. Then rewrite one of the
  two with a hedge (seems, tends to, a bit).
- **Level 6, Activity 10:** write a direct working thesis for the body paragraphs of an essay about your room
  addressed to a named reader who wants quiet (use Leo, or name your own), naming two points; then one sentence
  that tells that reader why one of the points matters to them.
- **Level 7, Activity 11 (a different shape):** write an indirect working thesis for the same bodies, then one
  sentence of detail chosen so that a reader would infer your attitude toward the room without being told; a
  partner reads it and says what they inferred.

### Phase 2: Prewriting for the Scenario, the draft object (30 min)

**Introduce the draft object.** From Lesson 3 on, every student writes about two places to study: their own home
and one other place they know. The packet prints the list to circle: **the library, a cafe, a classroom after
school, a park, a friend's place.** Every student can name one; nothing is brought or visited. The reader is
Dana, a classmate who needs quiet to study for exams and is deciding where to study this month; Levels 6-7
address Dana directly, and Levels 4-5 write so that Dana could choose.

**Present the Draft Word Bank** (above; printed in the packet's Day 2 unit before the first task that uses it).
Same groups as the room's bank, refilled for places.

This prewriting is rehearsal. Nothing in Lesson 3 depends on it (Quality Standards §D9): Lesson 3 builds its own
quick plan inside its own packet, and no task here says "for your draft" or "next time."

- **Level 4, Activity 12:** circle one place from the list. Write three Draft Word Bank words that fit your home
  as a study place and three that fit the other place, one from each of three different groups. Then fill a
  two-column list (home / the other place) with two facts each, and write one reason a person who needs quiet
  would pick one of them.
- **Level 5, Activity 13 (a different shape):** circle one place. List five details about the two places in the
  order you would describe them (sound, light, space, comfort, access), and mark the one detail that would let
  a reader guess which place you prefer without your saying so.
- **Level 6, Activity 14:** circle one place. Write a general outline of the body paragraphs of a comparison
  essay to Dana: a direct working thesis naming two points, one topic sentence per body paragraph, and the
  sentence that will say why one point matters to Dana. The introduction and conclusion come in Module 2.
- **Level 7, Activity 15 (a different shape):** circle one place. Write a specific outline of the body
  paragraphs: an indirect working thesis,
  three topic sentences, and under each two supporting details in order; mark the detail whose emphasis will
  carry the implication you want Dana to infer, and name that implication for yourself in one line.

**Teacher circulation:** every Level's plan already carries its required feature (a comparison and a reason; an
implied-preference detail; a why-it-matters-to-Dana sentence; a named implication). This is the teacher's
check and does not reach the packet.

### Phase 3: Prewriting share and board synthesis (20 min)

Two or three students from each Level share one detail or one line from their plan. **Board-dependent moment,
co-constructed live:** an idea board with two columns (home / the other place) filled from every Level's shares,
so the class sees details no single plan contains. Level 6-7 students add one "why it matters to Dana" line
each. The board stays up for reference during the share only; Lesson 3 rebuilds its own plan.
