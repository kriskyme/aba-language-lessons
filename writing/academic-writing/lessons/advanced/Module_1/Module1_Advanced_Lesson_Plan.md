## Scenario: Two Places to Study

Every student describes places they know, for a classmate who has to choose one. **Purpose (Module 1,
Describing):** describe a place by comparing it to another and giving a real reason for the comparison, so that
a reader can picture both and choose.

- **Practice object (Lessons 1-2 and the Mentor Ladder): my room.** The student's own room at home (or the
  room they sleep in), written from memory. Grammar input, controlled practice, and every Mentor Text and Essay
  use it, comparing it to what it was before, to a sibling's or friend's room, or to another room in the home.
  The room is a subject, not a prop (Quality Standards §D8): no task asks for a photo, a drawing to bring, or a
  look around.
- **Draft object (introduced at Lesson 2 Day 2; Lessons 3-4 and Module 2 write about it): two places to
  study.** The student's home compared with one place from a printed list the student circles at the start of
  each drafting lesson: **the library, a cafe, a classroom after school, a park, a friend's place.** Category
  title for Lessons 3-4: "Two Places to Study."
- **Named reader and concern (Levels 6-7's required feature; Level 4-5's implied audience):** Dana, a
  classmate who needs quiet to study for exams and is deciding where to study this month. Every Level writes
  so that Dana could choose; Levels 6-7 address Dana directly and say why one feature matters to Dana.
- **Closing Transfer Check object (Lesson 4 Day 2, a third object):** the student's own desk or table compared
  with a partner's.

Both objects are writable at every Level: a room and a pair of study places each yield a comparison plus a
reason (Level 4), organized detail with an implied attitude (Level 5), the body paragraphs of a comparison essay
for a named reader (Level 6), and body paragraphs with an embedded implication and visible self-revision (Level
7). **The piece is built in parts (Conventions §C):** Module 1 drafts the body paragraphs under a working thesis
(Levels 6-7) or one descriptive paragraph (Levels 4-5); Module 2 drafts the introduction and conclusion (Levels
6-7) or a second, narrative paragraph about studying in one of the places (Levels 4-5), then assembles,
peer-edits, and publishes. Neither Module drafts a complete piece. The draft object sustains the full Pair: two
places, a reader, and a decision give Module 2 a hook, a conclusion, and a narrative to write.

## Genre / real-world writing form (Lesson prompt 0.9, Module 1's row)

Levels 4-5: a descriptive paragraph that compares (continuous prose). Levels 6-7: a comparison essay, two
instances of the described thing (two places to study). Module 2 inherits the form unchanged.

## Grammar Focus A/B (Module 1)

Anchored at **Level 5's row**, Advanced's native Paragraph Composition Level (Level 4 is an extension-down
accommodation; Lesson prompt 0.4 row selection).

- **Focus A (Level 5 row): consistent tense control across a paragraph, and hedging and evaluative language**
  (seems, tends to, a bit, at least, fairly, mostly, worth it). **Level 4's own row, folded into the same
  mini-lesson:** comparative adjectives (-er / more, irregular better, worse, less) and reason connectors
  (because, since, as, given that) with the comma after an opening reason clause.
- **Focus B (Level 5 row): complex sentences with a concession subordinator** (although, even though, while),
  comma after an opening concession clause. **Level 4's own row, folded in:** simple vs. compound sentences
  (and, but, so) and the comma before the connector.
- **Confusable pair (Lesson 2 Day 1):** quiet / quite.
- Levels 6-7 receive Focus A/B as sentence craft inside the same sessions, with Level 6's register-shifting
  devices (however, in fact, on the other hand, given that) named where the concession lesson reaches them.

## Essay Focus A/B (Levels 6-7)

Anchored at **Level 6's row** (Lesson prompt 0.4c). Essay type: comparison essay (Module 1's mapping). Which
Module carries which Focus follows the part it drafts.

- **Module 1 carries Essay Focus B plus the working thesis (Lessons 1-2): topic sentence plus supporting
  sentences** inside a body paragraph (Lesson 1); **working-thesis construction**, direct at Level 6 (naming the
  points the bodies will make) and indirect at Level 7 (Lesson 1); a **general outline** of the body paragraphs
  (Lesson 2); Level 7 additive: a **specific outline** (down to supporting detail) and **combining sentences** for
  cohesion within and across paragraphs (Lesson 2). The working thesis is a line at the top of the outline and
  the draft, not an introduction; the introduction is Module 2's.
- **Module 2 carries Essay Focus A (Lessons 5-6): the five-paragraph shape**, **hook types** in Module 2's
  narrative form (a scenario, an anecdote; a quote or statistic at Level 7), the introduction's connecting
  sentences that place the thesis last, and the **conclusion** (restate the thesis in new words; a suggestion,
  prediction, question, or opinion). Module 2's Lesson 7 drafts those around the kept bodies.
- **Level 5 bridge (receptive only):** on the same model body paragraphs Levels 6-7 analyze in Lesson 1 Day 1
  (the bodies of "My Room, Then and Now," printed under their working thesis; the complete essay is modeled
  only at Lesson 5's second look), Level 5 finds and underlines one body paragraph's topic sentence and states
  in one sentence what the paragraph is about. Level 5's own output stays one paragraph.

## Leveled Mentor Ladder direction (on the practice object, my room)

Presented by the teacher on Lesson 1 Day 2, never printed. Every model is written for a cousin, Leo, who is
coming to stay for a month and wants somewhere quiet to study, so the ladder shows the same reader-and-concern
shape the draft will use. At Lesson 1 the Level 6-7 models are body paragraphs under a printed working thesis
line, the part Module 1 drafts; Lesson 5's second look presents the same ladder with each essay Level's
introduction and conclusion added (and each paragraph Level's second, narrative paragraph), the one time the
complete piece is modeled.

- **Level 4:** a short paragraph comparing my room now with my room a year ago, with exactly one comparison
  ("quieter than") and one explicit reason ("because the window faces the yard now").
- **Level 5:** an extended paragraph with details in a logical order (light, sound, space, what I do there),
  one hedge ("a bit," "tends to"), and one detail that implies a preference without stating it ("I still do my
  reading at the kitchen table").
- **Level 6:** three body paragraphs to Leo under a direct working thesis naming two points (quiet and light),
  each body led by a topic sentence, one sentence that says why quiet matters to Leo, and one register shift to
  address Leo's concern directly ("Now, the honest part").
- **Level 7:** three body paragraphs to Leo under an indirect working thesis, an implication the reader must
  infer from the selection and emphasis of detail (the room is described for study but every detail is about
  the bed), sophisticated cohesion (what's more, admittedly, this suggests), and visible self-revision: one
  crossed-out sentence replaced in place.
- **Lesson 5's second look** (Module 2, not yet planned): the Level 6 and 7 models gain an introduction (a
  scenario or anecdote hook, connecting sentences, the thesis last) and a conclusion; the Level 4 and 5 models
  gain a second, narrative paragraph; students notice what Module 2's focus added.

The ladder is distinct in kind across the 5/6 boundary: Levels 4-5 are one paragraph with no thesis; Levels 6-7
are body paragraphs that serve a thesis, each led by a topic sentence.
