Grammar

Softening a Claim

WordWhat it doesExample
seemssoftens a claimApartment A seems quieter at night.
tends todescribes a pattern, not a certaintyApartment B tends to get noisy around dinnertime.
a bitsoftens a descriptionThe kitchen in Apartment A is a bit small.
at leastadmits a downside while keeping a pointApartment B is smaller, at least in the kitchen.
worth itsignals a trade-off is acceptableThe extra rent is worth it for the quiet.

Comparing Two Things

RuleExample
Short words: add -erquiet → quieter
Words ending in -y: change y to i, add -ernoisy → noisier, busy → busier
Longer words: use "more"affordable → more affordable, spacious → more spacious
Irregular formsgood → better, bad → worse, far → farther, little → less

Giving a Reason

WordExample
becauseI chose Apartment A because it's quieter.
sinceSince Apartment A is quieter, it's the better fit for someone working from home.
asApartment A is the better choice, as it's quieter than Apartment B.
given thatGiven that Sam works from home, Apartment A's quiet matters more than Apartment B's extra space.

Keep one tense (past or present) for describing the same apartment within a sentence or paragraph.

How an Essay Is Built

Introduction: a hook, connecting information, and a thesis.

Body: 2-3 paragraphs, each with a topic sentence and supporting sentences.

Conclusion: restates the thesis, then closes with a suggestion, prediction, question, or opinion.

Five Ways to Open

TypeExample
QuestionWhich matters more: extra space, or a quiet place to work?
ObservationTwo apartments a block apart can feel like they belong to different cities.
ScenarioAt eleven at night, one of these buildings is silent.
Quote(used for a topic the quote is not obviously "about")
StatisticRemote workers report losing nearly an hour of focus a day to household and street noise.

Do not open by flatly stating your thesis.

Task D. ★★★ Read the model essay below.

Two Ways to Commute to Work

Every weekday, millions of people choose between two common ways to get to work: driving a car or riding a bike. Each option comes with its own advantages and drawbacks depending on distance, weather, and personal priorities. Driving and biking to work differ most clearly in travel time, cost, and effect on the rider's health.

The first difference is travel time. For short distances under five miles, biking is often just as fast as driving once traffic and parking are factored in. For longer commutes, however, driving is almost always quicker, especially on highways where a bike cannot legally travel.

The second difference is cost. A bike requires a one-time purchase and occasional maintenance, while a car involves fuel, insurance, and parking fees that add up every month. Over a year, a regular bike commuter can save thousands of dollars compared to a regular driver.

The third difference is the effect on health. Biking to work builds regular exercise into the day without requiring a separate trip to the gym. Driving, by contrast, adds no physical activity at all and has been linked by researchers to more sedentary lifestyles overall.

In the end, neither option is better in every situation. A short, dry commute favors the bike, while a long or weather-exposed one often favors the car. The right choice depends on what the rider values most: speed, savings, or health.

★ Example

My apartment, Apartment A, is quieter than Apartment B, the other place I looked at, and I really notice the difference at night. Apartment B is closer to the train station, so the commute would be easier from there. I chose Apartment A because it fits Sam's need for a quiet place to work from home. Apartment B has a bigger kitchen, but Apartment A has a better layout for two people. Overall, Apartment A is better for us because it fits what matters most to Sam.

★★ Example

Apartment A sits on the top floor of an older building, on a quiet residential street. Apartment B is bigger and closer to downtown, but the street outside never really goes quiet, even late at night. The walls in Apartment A seem thick enough that neither of us has ever heard a neighbor's television, while a friend who toured Apartment B mentioned hearing music through the wall on a weeknight. Apartment B is about a hundred dollars cheaper a month, which is worth something, and the extra space would be nice for guests. Still, I keep coming back to how still Apartment A feels at nine in the morning on a weekday, long after most people have already left for work. That's the kind of detail a listing never mentions, and it tends to matter more once you're actually living somewhere.

★★★ Example

Which matters more when choosing a home: how much room you have, or how well you sleep at night? I toured two apartments this month, and they could not be more different in how they'd fit into a daily routine. Apartment A is smaller and set back on a quiet street. Apartment B is bigger, closer to downtown, and considerably cheaper. Since I know quiet matters to you more than almost anything else, I want to walk you through exactly how the two compare. Apartment A and Apartment B differ most clearly in three ways: how quiet each one is, how much each one costs, and how much space each one offers.

The first difference is noise. Apartment A sits on the top floor of a converted brownstone, on a residential street that empties out by early evening. Apartment B faces a busier avenue, and even with the windows closed, the traffic and nearby restaurants are noticeable well into the night. Given that you'd likely be working from home most days, this difference alone might decide it: Apartment A offers the kind of quiet that Apartment B, however much space it has, simply cannot.

The second difference is cost. Apartment B runs about a hundred dollars less per month than Apartment A, which adds up over a full year's lease. In fact, if the rent alone were the deciding factor, Apartment B would be the clear choice.

The third difference is space. Apartment B has a larger kitchen and a second closet that Apartment A does not have. On the other hand, Apartment A's layout keeps the bedroom separated from the living room by a short hallway, which does double duty as extra sound buffering.

In the end, Apartment A and Apartment B each win on a different measure: Apartment B on cost and space, Apartment A on quiet. Since quiet is the one thing you told me you're not willing to compromise on, I'd lean toward Apartment A, but I wanted you to see the full comparison before deciding.

★★★★ Example

At eleven o'clock on a Tuesday night, one of these apartments is completely silent, the kind of quiet where you can hear the refrigerator cycle on in the next room. The other has a bus idling outside its window, headlights sweeping the ceiling every few minutes until well past midnight. I toured two apartments this month, and touring them back to back, on the same afternoon, made their differences impossible to ignore in a way that reading two listings side by side never quite manages. Admittedly, neither one is perfect, and each asks for a different kind of compromise, the sort that only becomes obvious once you've actually stood in both rooms. Deciding between them turned out to be less about which apartment is objectively better, since on paper they are closer than you'd expect, and more about which trade-off I could actually live with once the novelty of moving wore off.

Apartment A's silence is the kind you only notice by its absence elsewhere, the way a room feels different once you realize the hum you'd been hearing has finally stopped. It's really quiet. The building sits far enough off the avenue that a passing ambulance on a weekend night barely registers, and the bedroom, tucked behind a short hallway, seems to absorb whatever noise does get through before it reaches the pillow. What's more, the quiet held up even during a Saturday afternoon when the building's other units all seemed to be home at once, doors opening and closing, someone's radio drifting up from a lower floor, none of it carrying the way I expected it to.

Apartment B, by contrast, asks for a different kind of patience. The extra room is real: a second closet, a kitchen you could actually cook in without turning sideways, a living room wide enough for the couch we already own instead of one we'd have to replace. So is the noise, which never quite disappears, only softens between about two and six in the morning before starting up again with the first delivery trucks. This suggests something the listing photos won't show: that square footage and stillness rarely come from the same building, and a floor plan that photographs well is not the same promise as a night that actually lets you sleep through it.

While the rent gap between the two is real enough to matter, running to roughly a hundred dollars a month once you account for parking, it is not, on its own, the kind of number that should decide something this size. A hundred dollars a month is worth noticing, worth budgeting around, even worth a second look at the numbers before signing anything. It is not worth trading away the one thing that actually determines whether working from home feels possible five days a week, particularly on the mornings when a closed door and an ordinary amount of quiet turn out to be the entire difference between a productive day and a wasted one.

I already know which set of keys I'd rather be handed, and I suspect I knew it before I'd finished walking through the second apartment. What I don't know yet is whether saying so out loud will sound like I've made this decision for both of us before you've even seen either place yourself.
