## Scenario: My Last Meal, then A Meal That Went Wrong

Every student tells a reader about something they made to eat: what happened, in what order, how it turned out,
and why it mattered. **Purpose (Module 2, Narrating):** narrate a sequence of past events with an outcome and a
real reason for it, and, where the band reaches it, reflect on what the events meant, so a reader who was not
there understands both what happened and why it ended that way.

- **Practice event (Lessons 1-2 and the Mentor Ladder): the last thing I made to eat.** Written from memory.
  Grammar input, controlled practice, and every Mentor Text and Mentor Essay use it. Chosen for Narrating because
  making something to eat generates real, small, ordered events with an outcome and a reason (the water boiled
  over, the timer was ignored, a substitution was made, someone else finished it) where a purely descriptive
  subject would not. A student who cooked nothing recently writes about the last thing they put together or
  heated, which is still their own. It is a **subject, not a prop** (Quality Standards §D8): no task asks the
  student to bring food, cook anything, show a photo, or look at a picture, and no image is provided or needed.
- **Draft event (introduced at Lesson 2 Day 2's prewriting; Lessons 3-4 write about it): a meal that went wrong.**
  One time making or getting something to eat did not go as planned. The student circles the dish from a printed
  list of eight at the start of each drafting lesson: **instant noodles, rice, eggs, soup, pasta, a sandwich,
  curry, pancakes.** The list is deliberately cheap and near-universal, so no student is asked to write about
  something they have never made or eaten. Category title and slug for Lessons 3-4: "A Meal That Went Wrong." The
  Draft Word Bank is built for the category, not one dish.
- **Named reader: Mina**, a friend who wants to make the same thing herself and is sure it is simple. Required,
  not optional: Level 6's own CSV row demands a specified reader whose **likely reaction** the writer adjusts
  register or approach to address, and Mina's likely reaction, that the writer must have done something careless,
  is what Level 6 writes against. Levels 6-7 address Mina directly and reach her objection explicitly; Levels 4-5
  write so that Mina could follow what happened, without addressing her. The name is deliberately not
  near-homophonous with Module 1's Dana.
- **Closing Transfer Check event (Lesson 4 Day 2, a third subject used nowhere else in the Set):** one thing that
  happened with your own water bottle or cup.

**The practice event is never drafted about** (Lesson prompt 0.1a), and the draft event does not change after
Lesson 2 Day 2. The last meal's last appearance is Lesson 2 Day 1.

Both events are writable at every task Level in the band: a sequence of events with one outcome and a brief reason
(Level 4), an extended narration saying why it mattered with one detail that implies a feeling and no feeling word
(Level 5), a narrative that makes explicit why events mattered and shifts register once to meet Mina's likely
reaction (Level 6), and one whose pacing and selection of detail carry an implication about the narrator's own
motive (Level 7). A meal that went wrong supplies all four without changing the subject: it has a clear order, a
real outcome, a reason, and something the writer would rather not say outright.

## Genre / real-world writing form (Lesson prompt 0.9, Module 2's own row)

Levels 4-5: a **personal narrative / journal entry**, chronological prose with time connectors. Levels 6-7: a
**reaction essay** (narrate, then reflect).

## Grammar Focus A/B

Anchored at **Level 5's row**, Advanced's native Paragraph Composition Level (Level 4 is an extension-down
accommodation; Lesson prompt 0.4 row selection). **Retained unchanged from the superseded paired plan.** The
de-pairing changes the Scenario, not the grammar: this pairing is Module 2's own native content, hand-selected
because the bank gives one pair per Level and Module 1 spent that row's entry, it cleared the within-pair,
cross-Module, and cross-Band checks when it was selected and clears them again above, and changing it would have
discarded sound work for no stated reason. Levels 6-7 receive Focus A/B as sentence craft inside the same
sessions, with Essay Focus as their structural centerpiece (0.4c).

- **Focus A (Lesson 1): reported speech and thought in narrative.** What someone said, carried into a narrative
  without quoting it: backshift of tense into reported speech (*she said the rice was done*), the *say / tell /
  ask* patterns and which one takes a person object, and **reporting verbs that carry the narrator's attitude**
  (*admitted, insisted, muttered, decided, promised, complained*), so a narrator can report a line and judge it in
  the same sentence. Reported thought (*I thought I had time; I told myself it would be fine*) is taught alongside
  it as the same structure turned inward. This is the tool Level 6's shift between narration and reflection runs
  on, and the one that lets Level 7 embed a motive the reader must infer: a reporting verb chooses how the writer
  is heard without ever saying so.
- **Focus B (Lesson 2 Day 1): non-finite openers for pacing.** Participial and time-phrase sentence openers with
  the comma that follows them: *Reaching for the pan, I knocked the bowl over.* / *After three tries, I gave up on
  the sauce.* / *By the time anyone else was hungry, there was nothing left.* Where each opener puts the weight of
  a sentence, and why two in a row read as a list. Deliberately **non-finite**, which is what separates it from
  Intermediate Module 2's finite *when / before / after* clauses and from Module 1's *although / even though*
  concession; a short comparison line names that difference rather than re-teaching either.
- **Confusable pair (Lesson 2 Day 1): said / told** (*she said it was easy* / *she told me it was easy*), a
  genuine trap at this Band and directly downstream of Focus A.
- **Level 4's own Module 2 row, folded in as light content in the same sessions:** time connectors the student
  chooses for themselves (*first, later, in the end, the next time*), one outcome, and a brief reason for it,
  reusing Module 1's reason connectors rather than re-teaching them.
- **Level 5 within this Set:** the same two focuses at paragraph scale, with the requirement staying one detail
  that implies a feeling without naming it, and no feeling word anywhere in the paragraph.

## Essay Focus A/B (Levels 6-7)

Anchored at **Level 6's row** (Lesson prompt 0.4c), with Level 7 additive inside the same sessions. Essay type:
reaction essay, from Module 2's own mapping.

A Pair would have split this: Essay Focus B (topic sentences, support, outlining, the working thesis) into Module
1, Essay Focus A (the essay shape, hooks, introduction, conclusion) into Module 2. **A standalone Set drafting a
whole essay needs the whole**, so this Set carries Essay Focus A as its new teaching content and re-anchors Essay
Focus B briefly rather than re-teaching it.

**Amended 2026-09-14.** Essay Focus A was planned to open at Lesson 1 on a printed model essay. That model and
its analysis questions were removed from Lesson 1 by user direction (the questions were not clear enough), so
Essay Focus A now opens at Lesson 2, on the printed model that lesson already carries, with the shape read off
it in one marking step before its missing parts are written. Lesson 1's Levels 6-7 track is reported speech at
paragraph scale instead. Level 5's receptive bridge moved with it.

- **Essay Focus A, the new content (Lessons 1-2):** the **reaction-essay shape** (an introduction, narrative body
  paragraphs in order, a reflective turn, a conclusion), **hook types in a narrative form** (a scenario, an
  anecdote, a withheld detail; a quote or statistic additionally at Level 7), the **introduction's connecting
  sentences** that place the thesis last, and the **conclusion's closing moves** (restate the thesis in new words,
  then a suggestion, prediction, question, or opinion). The thesis here is what the events turned out to mean, not
  a comparison: direct at Level 6 (naming what the experience showed), indirect at Level 7 (signalling that it
  showed something without naming it).
- **Essay Focus B, re-anchored not re-taught (Lesson 1 Day 1, inside the model analysis):** the topic sentence and
  its supporting sentences, and the thesis line the bodies serve. These students met both in Module 1 Set 1, so
  this is one activity on the model essay confirming they transfer to a narrative body, not a second teaching
  sequence. Level 7 additive: combining sentences for cohesion across the narrative-to-reflective boundary, which
  is where a reaction essay's joins actually fail.
- **The reflective turn is this Set's own structural point,** and it is what Module 1's comparison essay had no
  equivalent of: the sentence or short passage where the piece stops narrating and starts saying what the events
  meant. Level 6 makes it explicit and signposted; Level 7 lets it arrive without a fixed template and withholds
  part of what it means.
- **Level 5 bridge (receptive only):** on the same model reaction essay Levels 6-7 analyze in Lesson 1 Day 1,
  Level 5 finds and underlines the sentence where the writer stops telling what happened and starts saying what it
  meant, and states in one sentence what that sentence adds. A few minutes, positioned next to the model, separate
  from Level 5's own paragraph work. Level 5's own required output stays a single paragraph.

## Leveled Mentor Ladder direction (on the practice event, the last thing I made to eat)

Presented by the teacher on Lesson 1 Day 2, never printed. **This Set's only ladder presentation**: a standalone
Set has no Lesson 5 second look, so each model is complete as presented, which is also the one time the complete
piece is modeled. Every model is written for Mina, who wants to make the same thing and is sure it is simple, so
the reader-and-objection shape of the Set is visible from Lesson 1.

- **Level 4 (Mentor Text):** a short paragraph telling what happened when I made it, in order, with the student's
  own time connectors, one clear outcome, and one explicit reason for it ("it came out salty because I used the
  wrong spoon to measure").
- **Level 5 (Mentor Text):** an extended paragraph narrating the same event, saying why it mattered, with one
  detail that implies a feeling without naming it ("I ate it anyway, standing up, and did not mention it to
  anyone") and no feeling word in the paragraph.
- **Level 6 (Mentor Essay, complete):** an introduction with an anecdote hook, connecting sentences, and a direct
  thesis last; two narrative body paragraphs in order, each led by a topic sentence; an explicit reflective turn
  saying why the events mattered; one register shift that addresses Mina's likely reaction that the writer was
  careless; and a conclusion restating the thesis in new words with a suggestion for her.
- **Level 7 (Mentor Essay, complete):** the same shape under an **indirect** thesis, with the outcome's real cause
  withheld until late, an implication about the narrator's own motive that the reader must infer from what is
  dwelt on and what is passed over (every detail is about the timer, and none of them says the writer was not in
  the kitchen), cohesion markers carrying the narrative-to-reflective join, a conclusion whose closing move is a
  question that leaves the implication standing, and visible self-revision: one crossed-out sentence replaced in
  place and left legible.

The ladder is distinct in kind across the 5/6 boundary: Levels 4-5 are one paragraph with no thesis and no
separate reflection; Levels 6-7 are essays whose narrative bodies serve a thesis and whose reflective turn is a
structural part, not a closing sentence.
