## Grammar focuses

**Focus B (today's centerpiece, whole class): non-finite openers for pacing.** Starting a sentence with a
participle or a time phrase, and the comma that follows. *Reaching for the pan, I knocked the bowl over.* /
*After three tries, I gave up on the sauce.* / *By the end of the week, I had stopped cooking altogether.*

**How it pairs with Focus A.** Yesterday students learned to get other people's words into a story. Today they
learn where to put the weight of a sentence, so that a story moves at a speed the writer chooses rather than one
event per sentence forever.

**Deliberately non-finite, and the comparison is named once, not taught.** These openers are not the *when /
before / after* clauses that carry a full verb, and not Module 1's *although / even though*. One line on the
board names the difference and moves on; neither of those is re-taught here.

**The trap, taught with the rule: the dangling opener.** A participial opener attaches to whatever noun comes
next, so *Reaching for the pan, the bowl fell over* says the bowl was reaching. This is the single most common
error with the structure and it is taught in the same beat as the rule, not discovered in the editing paragraph.

**Confusable pair (today): said / told.** *She said it was easy* against *she told me it was easy*. Directly
downstream of Focus A and a genuine trap at this Band.

**Essay Focus A opens here for Levels 6-7 (0.4c; moved from Lesson 1, 2026-09-14):** the reaction-essay shape,
read off this lesson's own printed model before its missing parts are written, then hook types in a narrative
form, the introduction's connecting sentences that place the main point last, and the conclusion's closing
moves. The shape work is one marking step on the printed essay - underline the main point, number the paragraphs
that tell what happened, box the reflective turn - and it precedes the writing, so the essay Levels meet the
whole before they supply a part. Level 7 additive: a quote or a withheld detail as a hook type, and an indirect
main point. Level 5's bridge sits here too: it reads the printed essay's second half and marks the turn.

**Level 4's own row, still folded in:** the student's own time connectors, one outcome, one reason, exercised in
Day 2's warm-up rather than taught again.

### Phase 1: Focus B mini-lesson: non-finite openers (20 min)

Whole room, one sitting, one combined Grammar box in the packet.

_The two shapes (8 min)._

| Opener | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Participial (*-ing*) | **Reaching for the pan,** I knocked the bowl over. | ties two actions to the same moment |
| Participial (*-ed*) | **Left on the heat too long,** the sauce turned to paste. | puts the cause first |
| Time phrase | **After three tries,** I gave up on the sauce. | jumps the story forward |
| Time phrase | **By the end of the week,** I had stopped cooking altogether. | covers a long stretch fast |

The comma is not optional. It marks where the opener ends and the sentence proper begins.

_The dangling opener (6 min)._ A participial opener attaches to the noun that comes next.

> Wrong: **Reaching for the pan,** the bowl fell over. (the bowl was not reaching)
> Right: **Reaching for the pan,** I knocked the bowl over.
> Wrong: **Having burned the first batch,** the second one came out better. (the batch did not burn anything)
> Right: **Having burned the first batch,** I watched the second one more carefully.

Test to say aloud and leave on the board: *who or what is doing the opener? That thing has to be the subject of
the main sentence.*

_Pacing, and why two in a row read as a list (6 min)._ One opener slows a moment down or jumps time forward.
Two in consecutive sentences stop sounding deliberate and start sounding like a pattern. Show the contrast:

> Stirring the sauce, I heard the door. Reaching for a cloth, I turned the heat down. Walking in, my brother
> asked what the smell was.
>
> Stirring the sauce, I heard the door. I turned the heat down and reached for a cloth. My brother walked in and
> asked what the smell was.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live.** Take one flat pair of sentences offered by a student about
their own last meal and rebuild it with the class three ways: joined with a participial opener, joined with a
time-phrase opener, and left as two short sentences. The class says which version they would keep and why. The
three versions stay up for Phase 2.

### Phase 2: Controlled practice B, and the essay track (30 min)

**Activity 1: Combine the pair with an opener (whole class, 12 min).** Eight items, each a pair of short
sentences. Combine each into one sentence using a non-finite opener, and put the comma in.

> 1. I reached into the cupboard. I found the wrong kind of rice.
> 2. I had read the recipe twice. I still measured the water wrong.
> 3. I waited ten minutes. I gave up and turned the heat up.
> 4. The pan was left on the heat. It started to smoke.
> 5. I opened the window. I told my roommate everything was fine.
> 6. Three days passed. I still had not cleaned the pan.
> 7. I tasted the soup. I understood what my brother had meant.
> 8. I had promised to cook. I ordered food instead.

**Answer note:** accept any correct opener with its comma and a subject that matches. Samples: 1. Reaching into
the cupboard, I found the wrong kind of rice. 2. Having read the recipe twice, I still measured the water wrong.
3. After ten minutes, I gave up and turned the heat up. 4. Left on the heat, the pan started to smoke. 5.
Opening the window, I told my roommate everything was fine. 6. Three days later, I still had not cleaned the
pan. 7. Tasting the soup, I understood what my brother had meant. 8. Having promised to cook, I ordered food
instead. Item 4 is the one that takes an *-ed* opener; item 6 takes a time phrase because there is no shared
actor.

**Activity 2, Levels 4-5 (whole class within the block, 8 min): keep or split.** Six items, each already written
with two openers in a row. For each pair, rewrite **one** of the two sentences without its opener, and mark
which one you kept. Level 4 is given the two sentences separately; Level 5 is given them as a short run of four
sentences and must find the pair first.

**Answer note:** there is no single correct choice. The check is that exactly one opener survives in each pair
and that the rewritten sentence is still correct. Ask two students who kept different ones to read theirs, and
let the class hear that both work.

**Activity 3, Levels 6-7 (12 min): write the opening and the ending.** Both halves are printed, taken from one
short model about a meal that went wrong. The introduction has lost its first sentence; the piece has lost its
conclusion.

> **The Cake I Did Not Read About**
>
> _(opening sentence missing)_ I had made it twice before, both times with my mother standing somewhere behind
> me, and I had decided that meant I knew how. The recipe was three lines long on a card in her handwriting, and
> two of those lines turned out to matter. What I learned that afternoon was that I had never actually made this
> cake at all.
>
> The first hour went exactly as I expected. I creamed the butter and sugar, beat in the eggs one at a time, and
> folded the flour through until the bowl looked the way it had always looked. My sister asked whether I was
> supposed to add anything else, and I told her I had the card right there.
>
> The second hour went differently. The cake came out of the oven flat, dense, and about half the height it
> should have been, and it stayed that way while it cooled on the rack. I read the card again, properly this
> time, and found the second line: a teaspoon of baking powder, which I had read past twice because I already
> knew what the card said.
>
> _(conclusion missing)_

Level 6, three steps: (1) write **two** different opening sentences for the introduction, one a short scene and
one a small story, then say in one sentence which you would keep and why; (2) check that the main point still
sits in last position after your opening, and rewrite the sentence before it if the join is rough; (3) write a
conclusion that says the main point again in new words and then does one of these: gives advice, predicts,
asks, or gives an opinion. Name which one you chose.

Level 7, the same three steps with two changes: at step 1 the two openings must be **a line someone said** and
**a detail you do not explain**; at step 3 the conclusion may not explain the detail from your opening, and your
main point in the introduction must signal that there is a reason without naming it.

**Answer note:** no key. Judge step 1 on whether the two openings are genuinely different in kind rather than
two versions of the same sentence, step 2 on whether the main point is still last, and step 3 on whether the
closing move is identifiable and named.

### Phase 3: said / told, and mixed editing (25 min)

**Activity 4: said or told (whole class, 8 min).** Six items, forced choice, one blank each.

> 1. My brother ____ me the oven was already on.
> 2. She ____ that the sauce needed more salt.
> 3. I ____ my roommate I would clean up.
> 4. Nobody ____ anything about the smell.
> 5. He ____ me why I had not used the small pan.
> 6. I ____ that I had made it before, which was not quite true.

**Answer note:** 1. told 2. said 3. told 4. said 5. asked 6. said. Item 5 is the one that takes neither: a
question after a person object needs *asked*, which is the point of including it.

**Activity 5: Find and fix the mistakes (whole class, 17 min).** An eight-sentence paragraph mixing Focus A and
Focus B errors, containing **exactly 8 errors**, stated on the page and counted here. Students cross out each mistake on the printed paragraph with a single line, then write the whole paragraph out again, corrected, on the answer lines below it; nothing is written between the printed lines, which have no room for it (Style Guide §F, "Writing on printed text").

> Last month I tried to make curry for four people. Opening the fridge, the chicken was still frozen. I said my
> friend that we could eat late, and I told him that the chicken will be fine. Checking the clock I put the
> chicken in warm water. Having waited twenty minutes, the chicken was still hard in the middle. My friend
> insisted he was happy to wait, which he had not been asked about. He told that he would chop the onions. By the
> time we ate, I thought the whole thing is my own fault for starting late.

**Answer note, 8 errors verified by count:** (1) *Opening the fridge, the chicken was still frozen* is a dangling
opener, to *Opening the fridge, I found the chicken still frozen*; (2) *I said my friend* to *I told my friend*;
(3) *the chicken will be fine* to *would be fine*; (4) *Checking the clock I put* needs the comma, to *Checking
the clock, I put*; (5) *Having waited twenty minutes, the chicken was still hard* is a dangling opener, to
*Having waited twenty minutes, I found the chicken still hard*; (6) *insisted he was happy to wait* to *said* or
*admitted*, since insisted contradicts a man who was never asked; (7) *He told that he would chop* to *He told me
that he would chop*; (8) *I thought the whole thing is* to *was*. Decoys to leave alone, none of them errors:
*which he had not been asked about*, *By the time we ate* (a finite clause, its comma correct), *for starting
late*, and the opening sentence.

**Levels 6-7 additional item, inside the same 17 min.** Once the eight errors are fixed, the paragraph has
another problem that is not a mistake in any sentence: three of its sentences open the same way in a row, so the
openers stop sounding chosen. Rewrite one of the three without an opener, and write one sentence saying what the
rewrite does to the speed of the paragraph. Level 7 only: also decide which of the two remaining openers earns
its place, and say what the other one was costing.

### Phase 1: Required-feature warm-up (25 min)

Short, targeting only each Level's own required feature, on the practice event one last time.

**Level 4.** Write three sentences about the last thing you made to eat: what you did first, what you did next,
and how it turned out. Use two time words you choose yourself, and end with one sentence beginning *because*.

**Level 5.** Write two sentences about the last thing you made to eat. One must contain a detail that lets a
reader guess how you felt. **No feeling word anywhere.** Trade with a partner, who writes underneath what they
think you felt and which words made them think it.

**Level 6.** Write the one sentence of a piece about the last thing you made to eat that says what the
experience showed you. Then write the sentence that would come immediately before it, so the two read together
as the end of an introduction.

**Level 7.** Write the same sentence as Level 6, but so that it signals there is a reason without naming it.
Then write one sentence saying what your version withholds.

### Phase 2: Prewriting on a new event: a meal that went wrong (30 min)

**This is where the Set's writing subject is fixed.** The practice event carried the grammar; the draft event is
where that grammar moves to something nobody drilled on (Lesson prompt 0.1a). Say once, not printed: *"You have
been practising on the last thing you made. Now you write about a time it went wrong, so the writing is yours
and not the practice."*

**The draft event.** One time making or getting something to eat did not go as planned. Students circle the dish
from a printed list of eight:

> **instant noodles / rice / eggs / soup / pasta / a sandwich / curry / pancakes**

A student who cannot think of a time one of these went wrong writes about a time one of them went differently
than expected, which is the same shape. The event is a subject, not a prop (Quality Standards §D8): nothing is
brought, cooked, or shown, and the piece is written from memory.

**The named reader, restated here because the drafting lessons need it: Mina.** A friend who wants to make the
same thing herself and is sure it is simple. Levels 6-7 write so she reads it; Levels 4-5 write so she could
follow what happened.

**Draft Word Bank (the same five groups, refilled for the draft event; printed here and reprinted in every later
lesson).**

> **Going-wrong verbs:** burned, boiled over, undercooked, oversalted, spilled, dropped
> **Kitchen actions:** forgot, misread, guessed, rushed, swapped, tasted
> **What went wrong:** the timer, the heat, the recipe, the smoke, the pan, the smell
> **Time words:** at first, halfway through, by then, in the end, too late, the next morning, one evening
> **People and places:** my brother, my roommate, my friend, the kitchen, the bin, the shop, the table

(32 words. Three verb-and-action groups holding 18 items, so no task exhausts a bank; padding per Quality
Standards §C4.)

**Planning, by task Level.**

**Level 4.** Circle the dish. Write three Draft Word Bank words that fit. Then a three-line plan: *First ___.
Then ___. In the end ___.* and one line under it: *It turned out that way because ___.*

**Level 5.** Circle the dish. Write three Draft Word Bank words. List four or five things that happened, in
order. Circle the one detail on your list that could show how you felt without you saying so.

**Level 6.** Circle the dish. Write the one sentence saying what the experience showed you. Under it, list the
two or three things that happened, in order, one line each, and write next to each which part of your sentence
it supports. Then write where you will stop telling the story and start saying what it meant, and one line on
what Mina is likely to think of you, which your piece will answer.

**Level 7.** The same as Level 6, with three changes: your sentence signals a reason without naming it; you mark
on your list the one detail you will plant early and not explain; and you write one line naming the motive a
reader should be able to infer, which will appear nowhere in the piece itself.

**This planning is rehearsal.** Lesson 3 rebuilds its own plan inside its own packet (Quality Standards §D9), so
nothing here says "for your draft" or "next time." The one thing that carries is **the choice of dish**, and
Lesson 3 reprints the list so a student who was absent today can choose then.

### Phase 3: Prewriting share and board synthesis (20 min)

Two or three students from each Level read one line from their plan: an event from the list, or the sentence
saying what it showed. Nobody reads a whole plan.

**Board-dependent moment, synthesized live.** Build one shared column from what is read aloud: **what goes wrong
in a kitchen**, in the students' own words, gathered across every Level. No handout contains it, and it is
deliberately about the kind of thing that goes wrong rather than about any one student's piece. It stays up for
Lesson 3.
