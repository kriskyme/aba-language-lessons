## Grammar focuses

**Focus A (this lesson's centerpiece, whole class): reported speech and thought in narrative.** Carrying what
someone said into a story without quoting it. Four parts, taught in one sitting to every Level: backshift, the
*say / tell / ask* patterns, reporting verbs that carry the narrator's attitude, and reported thought as the same
structure turned inward. Anchored at Level 5's row, Advanced's native Paragraph Composition Level (Lesson prompt
0.4 row selection), and retained from the superseded paired plan.

**Why this Module needs it.** A narrator who can only quote is stuck outside the story. Reported speech lets a
writer report a line and judge it in the same sentence, which is the machinery under Level 6's shift between
narrating and reflecting and under Level 7's inferable motive: the choice between *said* and *insisted* tells the
reader what to think of a speaker without the writer ever saying so.

**Level 4's own row, folded into the same mini-lesson (never a separate session):** time connectors the student
chooses (*first, later, in the end, the next time*), one outcome, and a brief reason for it. Reason connectors
(*because, since, as, given that*) were taught in Module 1 and are **assumed here, not re-taught**; they are used
in examples without a rule box.

**Essay Focus A does not open in this lesson (changed 2026-09-14).** It opens at Lesson 2, on that lesson's own
printed model essay, which is where the essay Levels first meet the reaction-essay shape on the page: an
introduction, narrative bodies in order, the **reflective turn**, and a conclusion, alongside the hook types and
closing moves Lesson 2 already taught. The printed model that used to carry the opening here was removed with
its analysis questions, so Phase 3's Levels 6-7 track is reported speech at paragraph scale instead, which is
this lesson's own Focus A. **Essay Focus B is re-anchored, not re-taught:** these students learned the topic
sentence, its support, and the thesis line in Module 1, and Lesson 2's analysis confirms those transfer to a
narrative body rather than teaching them again.

**The reflective turn is this Set's own structural point.** It is the place where the piece stops telling what
happened and starts saying what it meant. Module 1's comparison essay had no equivalent, so it is named,
modeled, planned, and checked by name throughout this Set. Level 6 signposts it; Level 7 lets it arrive without a
fixed template.

**Level 5 bridge (0.4c): not in this lesson, moved to Lesson 2 with Essay Focus A's opening.** It had been a
receptive pass over the printed model essay, underlining the turn; with that model gone from this packet the
bridge has nothing to read here, and Lesson 2's printed essay is where it now sits. Level 5's own output stays
one paragraph all Set either way.

### Phase 1: Hook and Skill Spotlight (15 min)

**Hook (8 min).** Pitched near the band's lower task Level, oral, nothing brought or shown. Ask: *"What is the
last thing you made to eat, and did anyone say anything about it?"* Take four or five answers. Write the reported
lines on the board **in the students' own words, unedited**, as a column:

> my brother said it was too salty
> my roommate told me the rice was hard
> nobody said anything

Then one arc-setting line, said once and not printed: *"For the next four lessons we are writing the story of a
meal. Today we learn how to get other people's words into a story without quoting them."*

**Skill Spotlight (7 min).** One sentence, the same for every Level, naming the skill and not the event (Quality
Standards §D1). Say it, write it on the board, leave it up:

> **"I can tell what happened, in order, and let what people said carry how I felt about it."**

No "some of you will also." A Level's own required feature is stated inside that Level's tasks, not here. Add one
line: this is the skill the last lesson of the Set will ask for on something new.

### Phase 2: Focus A mini-lesson: reported speech and thought (30 min)

Delivered to the whole room in one sitting, Levels 4-7 together (0.4). The packet carries one combined Grammar
box.

_Direct speech, indirect speech, and the three things that change (13 min)._ Do not open on the backshift rule:
at this Band the concept itself is new, so name both forms first, put them side by side, and label the reporting
verb in each. Write both on the board and say which is which.

> **Direct speech** (his exact words, inside quotation marks): My brother **said**, "I burned the rice."
> **Indirect speech** (the same thing inside your own sentence): My brother **said** he had burned the rice.

Point at *said* in both and name it: the **reporting verb**, the word that says who spoke. It stays. Then the
three features of indirect speech, numbered on the board and kept there for the rest of the lesson:

| What changes | His words | Your words |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The quotation marks go. The words are yours now. | "I burned the rice." | he had burned the rice |
| 2. **Tense shift:** the verb steps back one tense, because the reporting verb is in the past. | burned | had burned |
| 3. **Pronoun shift:** pronouns move to your side of the conversation. | I | he |

Which tense a verb steps back to:

| In his words | In your words |
|---|---|
| is, are, am (present) | was, were (past) |
| burned, took (past) | had burned, had taken (past perfect) |
| will | would |
| can | could |
| may | might |
| must, have to | had to |

Then work every example with both shifts named aloud, one column at a time. The last row is the one to spend
time on: two pronouns move, in opposite directions, because the speaker is talking about the listener.

| Direct speech | Indirect speech | Tense shift | Pronoun shift |
|---|---|---|---|
| My brother said, "The soup **is** ready." | My brother said the soup **was** ready. | is to was | none. "The soup" is not a pronoun, so nothing moves |
| He said, "**I burned** the rice." | He said **he had burned** the rice. | burned to had burned | I to he |
| She said, "**I will** fix it." | She said **she would** fix it. | will to would | I to she |
| He said, "**I can** taste the salt." | He said **he could** taste the salt. | can to could | I to he |
| My roommate told me, "**You** used **my** pan." | My roommate told me **I had** used **her** pan. | used to had used | you to I, my to her |

Close the beat with the two smaller facts, **said aloud and not printed**: time and place words move the same
way (*tonight* becomes *that night*, *here* becomes *there*), and the one exception worth knowing, that when the
thing said is still true a writer may leave the tense alone (*he said the stove takes forever to heat*), both
versions being correct. Neither goes in the packet: the printed box carries the three features and the worked
examples, and no task in this Set turns on a time word inside reported speech or on the exception.

The packet prints this whole beat: both named forms with the reporting verb labelled, the three-feature table,
the tense-shift reference, and the five worked examples with their two shift columns. A student who has never
met reported speech can follow it from the page alone (Quality Standards §D14).

_The say / tell / ask patterns (5 min)._ The trap is which verb takes a person object: **two of the three do**
(tell and ask), and *say* never does. State it that way round, since the mistake students make is adding a
person to *say*.

| Verb | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| say | say (that) something, **no person object** | She said the pan was too small. |
| tell | tell **someone** (that) something | She told me the pan was too small. |
| ask | ask **someone** whether / what / why | She asked me why I had used that pan. |

Wrong: *she said me the pan was too small*. Right: *she said the pan was too small* or *she told me the pan was
too small*. This is Lesson 2's confusable pair as well, so it is named here and drilled there.

_Reporting verbs that carry the narrator's attitude (8 min)._ The point of the whole lesson. The reporting verb
is a judgment the writer makes about the speaker, and the reader takes it without noticing.

| Reporting verb | What the reader concludes about the speaker |
|---|---|
| said | nothing in particular; neutral |
| admitted | they did not want to say it |
| insisted | they said it more than once, and were not asked |
| muttered | they said it quietly, perhaps not meaning to be heard |
| complained | they thought it was someone else's fault |
| promised | they committed to something |
| decided | they ended a period of hesitating |

Demonstrate the difference live with one line, three verbs: *My brother said it was fine.* / *My brother
insisted it was fine.* / *My brother admitted it was fine.* Ask what changes about the brother each time, not
about the food. State the rule that follows: **choose the reporting verb on purpose, because the reader will
draw a conclusion from it whether or not the writer meant one.**

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live (inside this beat).** Take one line from the hook column still on
the board and rebuild it with the class into three versions using three different reporting verbs, then have the
class say what each version makes the speaker seem like. The three versions stay on the board for Phase 3.

_Reported thought, and Level 4's own row folded in (4 min)._ Reported thought is the same structure turned
inward: *I thought the water was boiling. I told myself it would be fine. I decided I had time.* It is how a
narrator shows what they believed at the moment without stopping the story. Then, briefly, Level 4's own row:
time connectors the student picks (*first, later, in the end, the next time*), one outcome, and one reason for
it, shown in a single three-sentence example on the board rather than taught as a rule.

### Phase 3: Controlled practice A, part 1, and the Levels 6-7 exchange (30 min)

Two tracks running in the same room. Activities 1 and 2 are whole class; Activity 3 splits.

**Activity 1: Turn the line into reported speech (whole class, 10 min).** Eight items. Each gives a direct line
and the person who said it; students write the reported version and **mark whether it needs say or tell**.

> 1. "The pasta is still hard." (my roommate, to me)
> 2. "I forgot to buy eggs." (my brother)
> 3. "I will clean the pan." (my friend, to me)
> 4. "Why did you use the big pot?" (my brother, to me)
> 5. "I can smell something burning." (my roommate)
> 6. "I bought the wrong kind of rice." (my friend)
> 7. "You should have waited." (my brother, to me)
> 8. "I am not hungry any more." (my roommate)

**Answer note:** 1. told me the pasta was still hard. 2. said he had forgotten to buy eggs. 3. told me he would
clean the pan. 4. asked me why I had used the big pot. 5. said she could smell something burning. 6. said she had
bought the wrong kind of rice. 7. told me I should have waited. 8. said he was not hungry any more. Items 1, 3,
4 and 7 carry a person object and so need *tell* or *ask*; 2, 5, 6 and 8 do not and need *say*.

**Activity 2: Fill in the reported thought (whole class, 8 min).** A short model paragraph with six blanks, each
taking a reported-thought verb from a printed list (*thought, told myself, decided, knew, realised, expected*).
The list holds six for six blanks but each fits more than one slot, so the set is judged on sense, not on
matching.

> One evening I heated some soup that had been in the fridge for two days. I ____ it was still fine, because it
> smelled like nothing at all. I ____ myself that two days was not very long. After the first spoonful I ____ I
> had been wrong. My roommate came in and asked what the smell was, and I ____ I would not be telling her. I had
> ____ the evening to go differently. By the time I had poured it away I ____ that I was going to eat cereal.

**Answer note:** thought / told / knew or realised / decided / expected / knew or decided. More than one verb
fits blanks 3 and 6; accept any that keeps the sequence sensible, and use the disagreement to make the point that
the choice tells the reader something.

**Activity 3, Levels 4-5 (12 min): the reporting verb that fits.** Six items. Each gives a line and a short
description of how the speaker said it; students choose the reporting verb from the taught list and write the
reported sentence. Level 4 chooses from three options printed under each item; Level 5 chooses from the full list
with nothing printed under the item.

> 1. "It was my fault about the salt." (he had not wanted to say it)
> 2. "The recipe said twenty minutes." (she said it three times, nobody had asked)
> 3. "I will make it again tomorrow." (he committed to it)
> 4. "This pan is too small." (said quietly, to nobody)
> 5. "Nobody told me the oven was off." (he thought it was someone else's fault)
> 6. "I am going to start over." (she stopped hesitating)

**Answer note:** 1. admitted 2. insisted 3. promised 4. muttered 5. complained 6. decided. Level 4's three
printed options per item should include one near-miss from the same list, not two obviously wrong verbs.

**Activity 3, Levels 6-7 (12 min): reporting an exchange.** Both Levels work from one printed six-line exchange
between the student-narrator and their roommate about a pan of rice, chosen so the shifts the mini-lesson named
all appear: a present tense, a past tense, a *will*, and two lines where the speaker talks about the listener,
which is where pronouns move in both directions at once.

Level 6 writes the exchange as one narrated paragraph in reported speech, quoting nothing, with a different
reporting verb on at least three of the six lines. Level 7 does that, then writes the same events again as the
**roommate** would narrate them, so every pronoun moves to the other side and the reporting verbs have to be
re-chosen for a narrator who now owns the complaint.

**Answer note:** there is no single right paragraph. Check three things: the backshift is carried through
(*is* to *was*, *started* to *had started*, *will* to *would*, *said* to *had said*), the pronouns are
consistent within each version, and the reporting verbs are chosen rather than six *saids*.

One paragraph that meets Level 6's task, for comparison, not to be handed out:

> My roommate **complained** that the rice **was** still hard. I **told** her I **had** only **started** it
> twenty minutes **before**. She **insisted** I **had used** the small pan again, and I **promised** I **would**
> use the big one next time. She then **told** me I **had said** the same thing **the week before**, and I
> **admitted** I **knew**, and that I **had forgotten**.

Level 7's second version, the same events with the roommate narrating. Every pronoun crosses over, and the
complaining is now the narrator's own:

> **I complained** that the rice **was** still hard. **My roommate told me they had** only **started** it twenty
> minutes **before**. **I insisted they had used** the small pan again, and **they promised they would** use the
> big one next time. **I told them they had said** the same thing the week before, and **they admitted they
> knew**, and that **they had forgotten**.

Shift by shift, so a disagreement at the desk can be settled on the page: *is* to *was*; *started* to *had
started* and *ago* to *before*; *you used* to *I had used* in Level 6 and to *they had used* in Level 7; *will*
to *would*; *you said* to *I had said* / *they had said* and *last week* to *the week before*; *know* to *knew*;
*forgot* to *had forgotten*. The line students most often get half-right is *You used the small pan again*: it
becomes *she insisted I had used* from one side and *I insisted they had used* from the other, and a student
who flips the pronoun but leaves *used* unshifted has done half the work.

Reporting verbs are free as long as three or more differ. The version above uses five of the printed list
(complained, told, insisted, promised, admitted); a student who reaches outside the list for *pointed out* or
*reminded* is right, and worth reading aloud.

### Phase 1: Editing a paragraph on Focus A (20 min)

**Activity 4: Find and fix the mistakes (whole class, 20 min).** A seven-sentence paragraph on the practice
event containing **exactly 7 errors**, stated on the page and counted here. Errors are backshift, *said / told*,
and one reporting verb that contradicts the line it reports. Students cross out each mistake on the printed paragraph with a single line, then write the whole paragraph out again, corrected, on the answer lines below it; nothing is written between the printed lines, which have no room for it (Style Guide §F, "Writing on printed text").

> Last Thursday I made pancakes for the first time since school. My roommate said me that the pan was too cold,
> but I did not listen. She told that I always do this. After the first two came out grey, I thought that the
> problem is the heat. My roommate insisted she was sorry about the first batch, which was kind of her. She asked
> me that why I had not turned the pan up. I said her I will try again on Sunday, and I did.

**Answer note, 7 errors verified by count:** (1) *said me that* to *told me that* or *said that*; (2) *She told
that* to *She told me that*; (3) *the problem is* to *the problem was*; (4) *insisted she was sorry* to
*admitted she was sorry* (insisted contradicts an apology nobody asked for); (5) *asked me that why* to *asked
me why*; (6) *I said her* to *I told her*; (7) *I will try again* to *I would try again* (backshift after a past
reporting verb). Decoys to leave alone, none of them errors: *for the first time since school*, *came out grey*,
*which was kind of her*, *and I did*, and the opening sentence.

**Levels 6-7 additional item, inside the same 20 min:** after fixing, rewrite the **second and third** sentences,
the roommate's two consecutive reported lines, as one sentence so the two ideas join without naming her twice.
The sentences are named by what they contain as well as by position, since a position alone goes wrong the
moment the paragraph is edited: this item said "third and fourth" until 2026-09-14, which pointed at one
roommate line and one of the narrator's own thoughts.

**Answer:** *My roommate told me that the pan was too cold and that I always do this, but I did not listen.* One
subject, one reporting verb, two *that* clauses joined by *and*; *she* disappears because the second clause no
longer needs its own subject. *I always do this* keeps its present tense, as the corrected sentence 3 does: it is
still true, which is the exception the mini-lesson names, and a student who backshifts it to *did* is also
right. A student who fixed sentence 2 as *said that* rather than *told me that* joins it the same way (*My
roommate said that the pan was too cold and that I always do this*). What to mark wrong: any version that keeps
two subjects (*My roommate told me the pan was too cold, and she said I always do this*), since naming her twice
is the one thing the item forbids.

**Level 7 only, and not built on Level 6's join:** working from the same corrected paragraph, change one
reporting verb so a reader can tell what the narrator thought of the roommate, and write one sentence saying
what the change makes a reader think. Level 7 does not also do the join: the two tiers do different work on one
paragraph rather than the same work plus a demand, which is the case Quality Standards §B exempts from naming
the Task below.

**Answer:** three of the corrected paragraph's reported lines judge nobody and are the ones available to change:
*told me that the pan was too cold*, *told me that I always do this*, and *asked me why I had not turned the pan
up*. (*Admitted she was sorry* already judges, which is why error 4 exists.) Any one of the three, swapped for a
verb that carries attitude, is right; what is marked is whether the sentence that follows names the reader's
conclusion rather than the narrator's feeling. Two that work in opposite directions:

> *She **muttered** that I always do this.* A reader concludes she had given up expecting a reply and is tired
> of the narrator rather than angry with them, which puts the reader on her side.
>
> *My roommate **complained** that the pan was too cold, but I did not listen.* A reader concludes she was
> making a fuss, which makes not listening look reasonable and puts the reader on the narrator's side.

**The trap, worth catching at the desk:** the pattern travels with the verb. *Told me that* has a person object;
*muttered* and *complained* do not, so *she complained me that* and *she muttered me that* are wrong, and the
*me* has to go. This is the say / tell / ask rule arriving in the student's own sentence, which is why this item
sits after that beat and not before it.

### Phase 2: Writing the paragraph, with Focus A (25 min)

**Activities 5-6 (inside the 25 min): one paragraph, two demands.** Levels 4 and 5 write a paragraph of the kind
the class has just repaired, about the last thing they made to eat. The shared part is stated once, in the
section's own instruction rather than inside each task: **four to six sentences telling what happened, in order,
how it turned out, and one reason it turned out that way.** Each task then adds only its own demand.

**Both Levels trade and mark (user direction, 2026-09-14).** The partner step sits in the section instruction,
not inside either task, and is the same at both tiers: trade, and underline the reporting verbs in the other
paragraph. It replaced a Level 5-only guess-what-they-felt exchange. Nothing is written, so no answer space is
given: the marking happens on the partner's own page, and what it checks is whether a reader can find the
reported lines at all, which is the thing this lesson taught.

**Level 4 (★).** At least two sentences report something somebody said, using *said*, *told* or *asked* with the
right pattern for each. That is the CSV row exactly: sequence, outcome, brief reason, with the lesson's grammar
carried inside it.

**Level 5 (★★).** At least three reported lines, each reporting verb showing what the writer thought of the
speaker.

**Two of the row's own features were cut from this task by user direction (2026-09-14):** *state why it
mattered*, and *no feeling word anywhere*. What remains of the row's implied-detail requirement is the verb
choice itself, which is the mechanism that carries a feeling without naming one; what is gone is the explicit
ban and the why-it-mattered sentence. The consequence to watch when teaching: a student who writes *I was
annoyed* in the paragraph makes the partner's guess trivial, and the partner step stops testing anything. If
that happens in the room, the ban is the line to put back.

**Levels 6-7 do not write a paragraph here (user direction, 2026-09-14).** Their Day 2 work is the editing
paragraph's two harder tasks - joining the roommate's two reported lines into one sentence, and changing a
reporting verb so a reader can tell what the narrator thought of her, with a line naming the conclusion a reader
draws. Both are genuine essay-Level work on reported speech, and neither is a shorter version of somebody else's
task. Their own production starts at Lesson 3, where the essay regime begins; the **reflective turn** they will
need there is introduced on the Mentor Ladder at the end of this day rather than practised in a paragraph here.

**On length.** The four-to-six-sentence cap is practice sizing inside a 25-minute phase, not a ceiling: Level
5's own CSV length is met by the draft at Lesson 3 and the finished piece at Lesson 4, and nothing in this task
is counted against that row.

**Answer note:** there is no key. Mark by demand, in this order: are the reported lines real reported speech
(backshift, pronouns, no quotation marks); does each reporting verb fit its pattern (*told me that*, but
*complained that*); at Level 5, is each of the three verbs actually chosen, or are they three neutral *saids*
with the attitude left to a feeling word.

### Phase 3: Leveled Mentor Ladder (30 min, teacher-presented, never printed)

**This Set's only ladder presentation.** A standalone Set has no second look, so every model is complete as
presented and this is the one time the finished shape is shown at every Level. Nothing here is printed in the
packet (Style Guide §E; packet prompt 2.6). Present in Level order, naming what makes each model that Level's
version. All models are on the practice event and written for Mina.

**Level 4 Mentor Text** (5 sentences, 68 words; inside the 3-6 sentence, 40-90 word target):

> One evening last week I made instant noodles for my sister and me. First I boiled the water, then I put both
> packets in together. My sister tasted hers and told me it was too salty. In the end we poured half the water
> away and added more, because two packets in one pot is too much salt. The second bowl was fine.

What makes it Level 4: a clear sequence with the student's own time connectors (*one evening, first, then, in
the end*), exactly one outcome, and one explicit, directly stated reason (*because two packets in one pot is too
much salt*). Nothing is implied. One reported line, using *told* correctly.

**Level 5 Mentor Text** (8 sentences, 131 words; inside the 6-9 sentence, 90-150 word target):

> One evening last week I decided to make the rice dish my mother makes, without asking her how. I measured
> nothing, because I have watched her do it a hundred times and it never looked like measuring. The water went
> in first, then the rice, then everything else at once. After twenty minutes the bottom had caught and the top
> was still raw. My sister came in, lifted the lid, and said she would eat later. I scraped the good part into
> two bowls anyway and put one at her place. She ate it without saying anything, which is what she does. I have
> not asked my mother how she does it, and I am not going to.

What makes it Level 5: extended, details in a logical order, why it mattered carried by the last two sentences,
and **one detail that implies a feeling without naming it** (*I have not asked my mother how she does it, and I
am not going to*, which implies pride or embarrassment without either word). No feeling word appears. Reported
speech carries the sister, and the reporting verb *said* is deliberately flat.

**Level 6 Mentor Essay**, complete: **The Soup I Made Twice**, read aloud from the board. It is teacher material
and is not printed in the packet (packet prompt 2.6); the essay Levels meet the reaction-essay shape on the
page in Lesson 2, on that lesson's own printed model, so this is their first sight of a complete Level 6 piece. Name the five things that
make it Level 6: the anecdote hook, the **direct** thesis in last position naming what the experience showed,
narrative bodies each led by a topic sentence, the reflective turn opening paragraph four, and the one register
shift that addresses Mina's likely reaction head-on (*You may be reading this thinking I was careless*).

**Level 7 Mentor Essay**, complete. Present as a variant of the same essay rather than a second full reading, so
the Level 7 difference is visible rather than buried in new content. Read only the three changed parts:

> _Introduction, ending in an indirect thesis:_ ... I made that soup twice in one evening, and the second pot
> was better than the first for a reason that had nothing to do with soup.
>
> _A sentence planted early in body one:_ I had told my brother we would eat at eight, and I had said it in the
> way you say a thing you have already decided.
>
> _Conclusion, closing on a question:_ Start the onions, turn the heat down, and let the first twenty minutes be
> boring. I still do not know why eight o'clock mattered so much to me that evening, when nobody was coming and
> nobody was waiting. Do you ever find that?

What makes it Level 7: the thesis is **indirect**, signalling that there is a reason without naming it; the real
motive is never stated and must be inferred from the planted sentence and the returning clock; the conclusion's
closing move is a question that leaves the implication standing instead of explaining it. Say aloud that Level 7
also requires visible self-revision, which arrives at Lesson 4, and show the one crossed-out sentence left
legible in the margin of the printed model:

> ~~I was embarrassed about the first pot.~~ I put the first pot at the back of the stove and did not mention it.

**Walkthrough (teacher-led, from the board).** One line per Level naming what changes going up: Level 4 tells the
sequence and states the reason; Level 5 keeps the sequence and lets one detail carry the feeling; Level 6 puts
the sequence inside an essay that says what it meant and answers a reader; Level 7 withholds part of what it
means so the reader has to find it. The ladder is distinct in kind across the 5/6 boundary: 4 and 5 are one
paragraph with no thesis and no separate reflection; 6 and 7 are essays whose narrative bodies serve a thesis and
whose reflective turn is a structural part.
