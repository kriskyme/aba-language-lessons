## Scenario: Two Places to Study

Every student describes places they know, for a classmate who has to choose one. **Purpose (Module 1,
Describing):** describe a place by comparing it to another and giving a real reason for the comparison, so
that a reader can picture both and choose. **Purpose (Module 2, Narrating):** tell the story of one time
studying in one of them, so the reader understands why the comparison came out the way it did.

- **Practice object (Lessons 1-2 and the Mentor Ladder): my room.** The student's own room at home (or the
  room they sleep in), written from memory, compared with what it was before, with a sibling's or friend's
  room, or with another room in the home. The room is a subject, not a prop (Quality Standards §D8): no task
  asks for a photo, a drawing to bring, or a look around.
- **Draft object (introduced at Lesson 2 Day 2; Lessons 3-4 and all of Module 2 write about it): two places
  to study.** The student's home compared with one place from a printed list the student circles at the start
  of each drafting lesson: **the library, a cafe, a classroom after school, a park, a friend's place.**
  Category title for the drafting lessons: "Two Places to Study." The object sustains the full Pair: two
  places, a reader, and a decision give Module 1 a comparison and Module 2 the story behind it.
- **Named reader and concern:** **Dana**, a classmate who needs quiet to study for exams and is deciding
  where to study this month. Every Level writes so that Dana could choose; Levels 6-7 address Dana directly
  and say why one feature matters to her. Module 2's introduction and conclusion are written to the same
  Dana, and Level 6's CSV row (a reader's likely reaction) is satisfied by Dana's likely objection: that the
  writer is describing a place they simply prefer rather than one that works.
- **Closing Transfer Check object, Module 1 (Lesson 4 Day 2, already generated): the student's own desk or
  table**, compared with a partner's.
- **Closing Transfer Check object, Module 2 (Lesson 8 Day 2, the combined-skill task): the student's own pen
  or pencil case.** A fourth object, used nowhere else in either Set, and unused in every other Band.

Both objects are writable at every task Level in the band. For Module 2 specifically: a place the student has
actually studied in yields a sequence of events with an outcome and a reason (Level 4), an extended narration
saying why it mattered with one detail implying a feeling (Level 5), a narrative that shifts between narrating
and reflecting for a reader with a likely reaction (Level 6), and one whose pacing and withheld detail carry an
inferable motive (Level 7). **The piece is built in parts (Conventions §C):** Module 1 drafts the body
paragraphs under a working thesis (Levels 6-7) or the one descriptive paragraph (Levels 4-5); Module 2 drafts
the introduction and conclusion (Levels 6-7) or a second, narrative paragraph about studying in one of the
places (Levels 4-5), then assembles, peer-edits, and publishes. Neither Module drafts a complete piece.

## Genre / real-world writing form (Lesson prompt 0.9, Module 1's row)

Levels 4-5: a descriptive paragraph that compares, in continuous prose. Levels 6-7: a **comparison essay**,
two instances of the described thing (two places to study). **Module 2 inherits the form unchanged.** Module
2's own narrative row governs only the second paragraph at Levels 4-5 and the hook and conclusion material at
Levels 6-7: the finished piece is still a comparison, told partly through a story.

## Grammar Focus A/B (Module 1), restated as generated

Anchored at **Level 5's row**, Advanced's native Paragraph Composition Level (Level 4 is an extension-down
accommodation; Lesson prompt 0.4 row selection).

- **Focus A: consistent tense control across a paragraph, and hedging and evaluative language** (seems, tends
  to, a bit, at least, fairly, mostly, worth it). **Level 4's own row, folded into the same mini-lesson:**
  comparative adjectives (-er / more, irregular better, worse, less) and reason connectors (because, since,
  as, given that) with the comma after an opening reason clause.
- **Focus B: complex sentences with a concession subordinator** (although, even though, while), comma after
  an opening concession clause. **Level 4's own row, folded in:** simple vs. compound sentences (and, but, so)
  and the comma before the connector.
- **Confusable pair: quiet / quite.**

## Grammar Focus A/B (Module 2, hand-selected; the bank's Alternate column does not exist)

Anchored at the same **Level 5 row** (0.4 row selection is a property of the Band, not the Module), and
hand-selected for Module 2's own narrating purpose, since 0.4's bank gives one Focus A/B per Level and Module
1 has spent that row's entry. Confirmed distinct from Module 1's pair and from every logged Set (Section 0).
Levels 6-7 receive Focus A/B as sentence craft inside the same sessions, with Essay Focus A as their
structural centerpiece (0.4c).

- **Focus A (Lesson 5): reported speech and thought in narrative.** What someone said, carried into a
  narrative without quoting it: backshift of tense into reported speech (*she said the library closed at
  six*), the *say / tell / ask* patterns and which one takes a person object, and **reporting verbs that carry
  the narrator's attitude** (*admitted, insisted, muttered, decided, promised, complained*), so a narrator can
  report a line and judge it in the same sentence. Reported thought (*I thought I had time; I told myself it
  would be quieter*) is taught alongside it as the same structure turned inward. This is the tool Level 6's
  shift between narration and reflection runs on, and the one that lets Level 7 embed a motive the reader must
  infer: a reporting verb chooses how the writer is heard without ever saying so.
- **Focus B (Lesson 6 Day 1): non-finite openers for pacing.** Participial and time-phrase sentence openers
  with the comma that follows them: *Walking in, I found every table taken.* / *After three tries, I gave up
  on the corner seat.* / *By the end of the week, I had stopped going.* Where each opener puts the weight of a
  sentence, and why two in a row read as a list. Deliberately **non-finite**, which is what separates it from
  Intermediate Module 2's finite *when / before / after* clauses and from Module 1's *although / even though*
  concession; a short comparison line names that difference rather than re-teaching either.
- **Confusable pair (Lesson 6 Day 1): said / told** (*she said it was quiet* / *she told me it was quiet*), a
  genuine trap at this Band and directly downstream of Focus A.
- **Level 4's own Module 2 row, folded in as light content in the same sessions:** time connectors the student
  chooses for themselves (*first, later, in the end, the next time*), one outcome, and a brief reason for it,
  reusing Module 1's reason connectors rather than re-teaching them.
- **Level 5 within Module 2:** the same two focuses at paragraph scale, with the requirement staying one
  detail that implies a feeling without naming it, and no feeling word anywhere in the paragraph.

## Essay Focus A/B (Levels 6-7)

Anchored at **Level 6's row** (Lesson prompt 0.4c), with Level 7 additive inside the same sessions. Essay
type: comparison essay, from Module 1's mapping. Which Module carries which Focus follows the part it drafts.

- **Module 1 carries Essay Focus B plus the working thesis (Lessons 1-2), already generated:** topic sentence
  plus supporting sentences inside a body paragraph, and working-thesis construction, direct at Level 6 and
  indirect at Level 7 (Lesson 1); a general outline of the body paragraphs, then at Level 7 a specific outline
  down to supporting detail and combining sentences for cohesion (Lesson 2). The working thesis is a line at
  the top of the outline and the draft, never an introduction.
- **Module 2 carries Essay Focus A (Lessons 5-6):**
  - **The five-paragraph shape** named as a whole for the first time in the Pair, with the kept bodies placed
    inside it, so students see what their own piece is still missing.
  - **Hook types in Module 2's narrative form:** a scenario and an anecdote at Level 6; a quote or a statistic
    additionally at Level 7. The hook is where Module 2's narrating verb enters the shared piece, so the
    anecdote hook is written with Focus A's reported speech and Focus B's openers, not as a separate exercise.
  - **The introduction's connecting sentences**, moving from the hook to the thesis and placing **the thesis
    last**. The thesis is the one Module 1 already wrote as a working thesis: Lesson 6 revises it into place
    rather than inventing a new one, which is what makes the introduction fit bodies that already exist.
  - **The conclusion's closing moves:** restate the thesis in new words, then one of a suggestion, a
    prediction, a question, or an opinion. At Level 7 the conclusion must not resolve the withheld detail
    flatly; the closing move carries the implication rather than explaining it.
- **Level 5 bridge (receptive only), continuing Module 1's:** on the completed model essay presented at Lesson
  5's second look, Level 5 finds and underlines the sentence that is the thesis and says in one sentence what
  the conclusion does that the bodies did not. A few minutes, positioned next to the model, separate from
  Level 5's own paragraph work. Level 5's own required output stays a single paragraph, and in Module 2 that
  paragraph is the second one.

## CSV-objective coverage (Pair): Lesson 8's separate Module 2 verb task

The shared piece's Module 2 part is an introduction and conclusion (Levels 6-7) or a second narrative
paragraph (Levels 4-5) inside a comparison essay, so Module 2's own row is exercised on a fresh prompt as well,
in Lesson 8 Day 2's Closing Transfer Check. **Prompt: the last time you moved to a different seat, room, or
place to get something done.** Calibrated to the Scenario's subject matter, and used nowhere else in either
Set or in any other Band's Transfer Check (Intermediate used this morning, Beginner last night, Proficient the
last time you were late to something that mattered).

- **Level 4:** three to four sentences telling what happened in order, with time connectors the student
  chooses, one outcome, and a brief reason for it.
- **Level 5:** an extended narration of it, saying why it mattered, with one detail that lets a partner guess
  how you felt and no feeling word anywhere.
- **Level 6:** the same, plus one clear shift from narrating to reflecting, written for a reader you know is
  likely to think you were just being fussy.
- **Level 7:** the same, with the outcome held back until the final sentence and one earlier, unexplained
  detail that hints at why you really moved.

This is separate from Lesson 8's **combined-skill** Transfer Check on the pen or pencil case, which asks for
both Modules' skills at once: describe it against the one you had before, then tell how it got that way.

## Leveled Mentor Ladder direction (on the practice object, my room)

Presented by the teacher on Lesson 1 Day 2, never printed. Every model is written for a cousin, **Leo**, who is
coming to stay for a month and wants somewhere quiet to study, so the ladder shows the same
reader-and-concern shape the draft uses on the draft object. At Lesson 1 the Level 6-7 models are body
paragraphs under a printed working thesis line, the part Module 1 drafts.

- **Level 4:** a short paragraph comparing my room now with my room a year ago, with exactly one comparison
  ("quieter than") and one explicit reason ("because the window faces the yard now").
- **Level 5:** an extended paragraph with details in a logical order (light, sound, space, what I do there),
  one hedge ("a bit," "tends to"), and one detail that implies a preference without stating it ("I still do my
  reading at the kitchen table").
- **Level 6:** three body paragraphs to Leo under a direct working thesis naming two points (quiet and light),
  each body led by a topic sentence, one sentence saying why quiet matters to Leo, and one register shift to
  address his concern directly.
- **Level 7:** three body paragraphs to Leo under an indirect working thesis, an implication the reader must
  infer from the selection and emphasis of detail, sophisticated cohesion, and visible self-revision: one
  crossed-out sentence replaced in place.

### Lesson 5's second look (Module 2)

The same ladder, presented again by the teacher, each model now completed by Module 2's part. This is the one
time the complete piece is modeled, and it is a before-and-after framing of models students already met, not a
new ladder. Still never printed.

- **Level 4:** the model gains a second, short narrative paragraph about one afternoon spent working in that
  room, in order, with an outcome and a reason. Students notice that the second paragraph tells what happened
  where the first only said what things are like.
- **Level 5:** the model gains a second, extended narrative paragraph saying why that afternoon mattered, with
  one detail implying a feeling and no feeling word. Students notice that the implied feeling is carried by a
  chosen detail, exactly as the first paragraph's implied attitude was.
- **Level 6:** the model gains an introduction whose hook is an anecdote about arriving to find the room
  already full, connecting sentences, and the working thesis moved to last position, plus a conclusion that
  restates it in new words and adds a suggestion for Leo. Students notice that the thesis did not change, only
  where it sits and what now leads up to it.
- **Level 7:** the model gains an introduction whose hook is a reported line ("my brother said I would never
  get any work done in here") and a conclusion whose closing move is a question that leaves the implication
  standing. Students notice that the introduction never states the motive the bodies imply, and that the
  conclusion declines to explain it.

The ladder is distinct in kind across the 5/6 boundary in both looks: Levels 4-5 are paragraphs with no thesis,
gaining a second paragraph; Levels 6-7 are body paragraphs serving a thesis, gaining the frame that thesis
needed. It is distinct again between the looks: the first look models a part, the second models a whole.
