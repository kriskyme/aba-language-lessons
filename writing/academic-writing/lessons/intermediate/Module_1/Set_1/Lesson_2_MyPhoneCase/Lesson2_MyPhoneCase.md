## Grammar focuses

**Focus B (this lesson's centerpiece, whole class):** simple versus compound sentences (and / but / so), including
the comma before the connector. This is Level 4's paired sentence-variety row in Section 0.4's bank: a compound
sentence lets the comparison Focus A taught sit next to its reason or its result in one sentence, which is
exactly the shape Level 4's required feature takes.

**Level 5 extension, folded into the same mini-lesson (never a separate session):** the concession subordinator
"although" (and "even though") as an upgrade from a plain "but" compound sentence, with the comma rule for a
fronted clause. Level 5's implied attitude is often a hedge inside a concession clause; Lesson 1's Activity 6
introduced the hedges (a bit, a little, seems, at least) that this lesson's Level 5 tasks reuse.

**Frame-regime content, folded into the same mini-lesson (Levels 2-3):** the "and" inside the two-slot frame
"It is ___ and ___." does the same joining job as the "and" in a compound sentence, joining two words instead of
two sentences. Pointed out in Phase 1, practiced in the parallel frame rounds.

**Confusable pair (this lesson):** it's / its, the highest-frequency spelling error in object description at this
band.

### Phase 1: Focus B mini-lesson: simple and compound sentences (20 min)

_Simple and compound (10 min)._

A **simple sentence** has one subject-verb combination. It can still have two subjects ("My brother and I have
the same case") or two verbs ("I picked it up and put it in my bag") and stay simple, because there is only one
complete sentence inside it. A **compound sentence** joins two complete sentences with a comma and a connector:
and, but, or so.

| Connector | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| and | adds information | My case is blue, and it is thin. |
| but | adds a contrast | My case is thin, but it is heavy. |
| so | gives a result | My case cracked, so I bought a new one. |

The comma goes before the connector, and only when a complete sentence follows it: "My case is blue and thin"
takes no comma, because "thin" is not a sentence.

Tie it to Focus A explicitly: "Yesterday you learned to compare two things and give a reason. Today you'll join
that comparison to its reason, or to its result, in one sentence: 'This case is thicker than my old one, so it
never cracks.'"

_Frame content, folded in (3 min)._ Show "It is ___ and ___." next to "My case is blue, and it is thin." The
"and" does the same joining job in both; in the frame it joins two words, so no comma is needed. Levels 2-3 keep
using the frame; they do not write compound sentences this lesson.

_One more step: although (7 min, whole class)._ "But" puts the two halves side by side as equals. "Although" (or
"even though") pushes one half into the background and makes the other half the point:

| Shape | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| but (compound) | This case is heavy, but I like it. | Two equal facts. |
| although, clause second | I like this case although it is heavy. | The weight is admitted, then set aside; no comma. |
| Although, clause first | Although it is heavy, I like this case. | Same, with a comma after the fronted clause. |

Everyone hears this; only Level 5 is asked to produce it today (Activity 3), since a hint of reservation inside
an "although" clause is one of the ways a Level 5 paragraph implies an attitude without stating it.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** pick an object in the room that is not a phone case (the
classroom clock, a student's water bottle, the door). Elicit two simple sentences about it from call-outs, then
have the class choose the connector and place the comma together, then repeat with a different connector. Neither
sentence is printed anywhere.

### Phase 2: Controlled practice B (30 min)

**Activity 1: Label each sentence simple (S) or compound (CD); where it is compound and the comma is missing, add
it (whole class, 10 min).** Three items are simple sentences with two subjects or two verbs, and one compound
item already has its comma, so the task cannot be done by scanning for "and" or by adding a comma to every line.

> 1. My case is blue and thin.
> 2. My case is blue and my sister's case is red.
> 3. I like this case but I want a different color.
> 4. I picked it up and put it in my bag.
> 5. It cracked so I bought a new one.
> 6. My brother and I have the same case.
> 7. I use it at home, and I use it at work.
> 8. It is thin but it is not cheap.

Answer key: 1 S; 2 CD, comma before "and"; 3 CD, comma before "but"; 4 S (two verbs, one subject); 5 CD, comma
before "so"; 6 S (two subjects, one verb); 7 CD, already correct; 8 CD, comma before "but".

**Activity 2, Levels 4-5: Combine each pair into one compound sentence with and, but, or so (12 min).** The
meaning of each pair forces the connector: a result pair takes "so," a contrast pair takes "but."

> 1. My phone slips out of my hand a lot. I bought a rubbery case.
> 2. The clear case shows the phone's real color. It gets scratched easily.
> 3. My old case cracked in the first month. I did not buy that brand again.
> 4. This case is more expensive than my last one. It has lasted twice as long.
> 5. I clean the case every morning. I wipe it again at night.
> 6. My sister wanted a bright case. I wanted a plain one.
> 7. The thick case protects the phone well. It has a grip on the back.
> 8. My case is light. I forget it is there.

Answer key: 1 so; 2 but; 3 so; 4 but (accept "and": both readings are natural); 5 and; 6 but; 7 and; 8 so. Every
answer needs the comma before the connector.

**Activity 3, Level 5 heavier tier (same 12 minutes, after Activity 2):** take your "but" sentences from items 2,
4, and 6 and rewrite each with "although" or "even though." Write at least one with the clause first (comma after
it) and at least one with the clause second (no comma). Then one line: in item 4, which half did you push into
the background, and does the sentence now sound more or less sure about the case? Built only from Phase 1's
table; no new grammar.

**Activity 2-equivalent, Levels 2-3 (parallel, 12 min).** Working from their own phone case and the Word Bank:

- **Both Levels, first:** four word pairs are written on the board. Circle the pairs that say two *different*
  things about a phone case, and cross out the pairs that say the same thing twice.

  > 1. blue and thin
  > 2. scratched and worn
  > 3. soft and rubbery
  > 4. hard and shiny

  Answer key: 1 and 4 are two different observations (color + thickness; texture + condition); 2 and 3 each name
  the same observation twice (both condition words for the same wear; both texture words for the same feel).
  This is Level 3's own required feature (0.2) made into a genuine choice, and Level 2 does it as the lighter,
  receptive tier before returning to its one-slot frame.
- **Level 2:** complete "It is ___." three times about their own case, one word from a different Word Bank group
  each round (color, then thickness, then condition). Read each finished sentence to a partner.
- **Level 3:** complete "It is ___ and ___." three times about their own case, each pair from two different Word
  Bank groups, so the two slots always carry two different observations; then write one -s sentence about the
  same case ("It looks new."). Read each to a partner.

The teacher circulates and listens to each student sound out the word before writing it.

### Phase 3: Confusable pair and mixed editing (25 min)

**it's / its (5 min).**

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| it's | it is / it has | It's blue. It's been my case for two years. |
| its | belonging to it (possessive, no apostrophe) | Its corner is cracked. |

Test to teach: if "it is" or "it has" fits in the gap, write "it's"; otherwise write "its."

**Activity 4: Choose it's or its (whole class, 6 min).** Items 7 and 8 carry two blanks each, one of each form,
so the answer cannot be copied from the blank before it.

> 1. ___ my favorite phone case.
> 2. ___ corner is a little worn.
> 3. ___ been dropped more times than I can count.
> 4. I like ___ color the best.
> 5. ___ heavier than it looks.
> 6. The case lost ___ shine after a year.
> 7. ___ grip is the reason ___ never slipped out of my hand.
> 8. I know ___ not new, but ___ corners still look sharp.

Answer key: 1 It's; 2 Its; 3 It's; 4 its; 5 It's; 6 its; 7 Its, it's; 8 it's, its.

**Activity 5, Levels 4-5: Find and fix the 8 mistakes (pairs, then whole class, 14 min).** Errors mix Focus A
(comparative forms, -s agreement) and Focus B (missing commas, a comma splice, it's/its). Two of the seven
sentences are error-free, so students cannot assume every sentence hides a mistake.

> My new phone case is more thick than my old one, and it feels a lot sturdier. I picked it because my old one
> break too easily. Its heavier than the old case but I don't mind the weight. The color is darker than my
> sister's case, its corners are rounder too. My sister says her case is more pretty than mine so she will never
> trade. I think mine is safer since it is rubbery, and it bounce when it falls. Given that I drop my phone
> almost every day, a soft case is the better choice for me.

Answer key, 8 errors verified by count: (1) more thick -> thicker; (2) break -> breaks; (3) Its heavier -> It's
heavier; (4) missing comma before "but" in sentence 3; (5) comma splice in sentence 4 -> "case, and its corners"
(or a period); (6) more pretty -> prettier; (7) missing comma before "so" in sentence 5; (8) it bounce -> it
bounces. Decoys that are correct: "its corners" in sentence 4, the comma before "and" in sentence 6, and
sentence 7 (an opening "given that" clause with its comma). Sentences 1 and 7 are error-free.

**Level 5 addition to Activity 5:** after fixing, mark one place where "although" could replace "but," and write
that sentence out in the margin.

**Levels 2-3 during Activity 5 (oral editing pass, 14 min):** read your three frame sentences from Phase 2 to a
new partner, holding up the case. The partner answers two questions out loud: "Does the word match the case?" and,
for Level 3,
"Did I hear two different things?" Fix any sentence the partner could not confirm, then swap roles.

### Phase 1: Frame practice round 2 / required-feature warm-up (25 min)

**Levels 2-3 (frame regime):** the same frames as Day 1, on the student's own phone case again (or a classmate's,
held up), this time choosing words not used on Day 1. Lesson 1 Day 2 used another belonging; this round is the
last one on the phone case, a final rehearsal of the frames before Phase 2 moves to the draft object.

- **Level 2:** "It is ___." three times, each word from a different Word Bank group. Say the word to a partner
  before writing it.
- **Level 3:** "It is ___ and ___." three times, each pair from two different groups (use Day 1's circle test:
  does the pair say two different things?), then one -s sentence about the same case ("It holds a lot." / "It
  looks worn.").

**Activity 6, Level 4 (required-feature warm-up):** write two comparison sentences about your own phone case
(compared with an old case, a friend's case, or a classmate's case) and two reason sentences, each using a different
reason word (because, since, as, given that). Then join one comparison to one reason as a single compound
sentence with "so" or "and": "My case is thicker than my old one, so it never cracks." Four sentences plus one
combined sentence, five lines total.

**Activity 7, Level 5 (implied-attitude warm-up):** write two sentences about your own phone case, each carrying
one concrete, specific detail and no feeling word (no love, hate, like, favorite). One of the two should use a
hedge from Lesson 1 (a bit, a little, seems, at least) or an "although" clause from Day 1. Trade with a partner
and ask, for each sentence: "What does this detail make you think I feel about my case?" Write the partner's
guess under each sentence. If the guess is wrong, the detail is not doing its job yet; note which one to
replace.

### Phase 2: Prewriting for the Scenario, differentiated by task Level (30 min)

**The draft object.** The piece every student drafts in Lesson 3 is about their own jacket, hoodie, or sweater:
whatever outer layer they are wearing or have with them today. A student with none uses a classmate's, taken off
the back of a chair and put on the desk. Nothing is provided or shown by the teacher. The phone case was the
practice object; the jacket is the object the taught grammar now transfers to, and it stays the object through
Lesson 4 and Module 2.

**Draft Word Bank** (posted on the board and printed in the packet directly before the planning tasks; the same
five groups as the Word Bank, refilled for a jacket; Levels 2-3 draw their words from it, Levels 4-5 may use it):

> **Color/pattern:** black, gray, green, red, plain, printed
> **Material:** cotton, fleece, denim, wool, leather, nylon
> **Size/fit:** big, small, long, loose, tight, baggy
> **Condition:** new, faded, worn, torn, stained, clean
> **Feel/quality:** warm, light, heavy, soft, thick, sturdy

Everyone plans directly from the object on the desk; nothing from Lesson 1's packet is needed (Quality Standards
§D9). This prewriting is rehearsal: Lesson 3 builds its own quick plan inside its own packet.

- **Level 2:** on the Draft Word Bank, write three words that fit your own jacket, one from each of three
  different groups. Circle the one word you like best, and tell a partner why that word and not the other two.
- **Level 3:** write three words that fit your own jacket, one from each of three different groups. Circle the
  two you would put in the two-slot frame; the pair must pass Day 1's test (two different things about the
  jacket). Say the sentence aloud to a partner.
- **Level 4:** a two-column note: "What I'm comparing my jacket to" (an old one, a sibling's, a friend's, one in
  a store) / "My real reason." Under it, one planned compound sentence joining the two (Activity 6's shape, on
  the jacket) and one extra detail about the jacket that is not the comparison (the pockets, the zipper, how long
  you have had it).
- **Level 5:** a list of four or five concrete details about your own jacket, in an order that makes sense (what
  it looks like, then what it has been through, then when you wear it). Circle the one detail that most clearly
  hints at how you feel, and write one line under it: "What I want the reader to guess: ___." Then read the list
  to a partner without the last line; the partner says what they guess, and a miss shows which detail to
  sharpen.

### Phase 3: Prewriting share and board synthesis (20 min)

Two or three students from each task Level share one word (Levels 2-3) or one detail (Levels 4-5).

**Board-dependent moment, synthesized live:** a two-column class bank, "Describing words" / "Detail ideas," built
only from what students share, so it contains words and details no single handout has. Level 4-5 details go in
the right column; Level 2-3 words go in the left, added next to the Draft Word Bank group they belong to. The
board is this lesson's; Lesson 3 does not depend on it.
