Grammar: Joining Two Sentences

A simple sentence has one subject-verb combination. It can still have two subjects ("My brother and I have the same case") or two verbs ("I picked it up and put it in my bag") and stay simple, because there is only one complete sentence inside it.

A compound sentence joins two complete sentences with a comma and a connector: and, but, or so.

ConnectorJobExample
andadds informationMy case is blue, and it is thin.
butadds a contrastMy case is thin, but it is heavy.
sogives a resultMy case cracked, so I bought a new one.

The comma goes before the connector, and only when a complete sentence follows it. "My case is blue and thin" takes no comma, because "thin" is not a sentence.

You already know how to compare two things and give a reason. Now you can join the comparison to its reason or its result in one sentence: "This case is thicker than my old one, so it never cracks."

The frame "It is ___ and ___." uses "and" the same way, joining two words instead of two sentences, so it needs no comma.

Grammar: One More Step, Although

"But" puts the two halves side by side as equals. "Although" (or "even though") pushes one half into the background and makes the other half the point.

ShapeExampleWhat it does
butThis case is heavy, but I like it.Two equal facts.
although, clause secondI like this case although it is heavy.The weight is admitted, then set aside. No comma.
Although, clause firstAlthough it is heavy, I like this case.Same, with a comma after the first part.

itsbelonging to it (no apostrophe)Its corner is cracked.
