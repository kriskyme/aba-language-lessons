## Scenario: The Bag I Carry

Every student describes something they own and use daily, for a reader who has to decide about one like it.
**Purpose (Module 1, Describing):** describe an object by comparing it with itself at another time, choosing
and weighting detail so that a named reader can picture both states and decide. **Purpose (Module 2,
Narrating):** tell how it got that way.

- **Practice object (Lessons 1-2 and the Mentor Ladder): my headphones or earbuds.** The pair the student uses
  most, written from memory and compared with the pair they used before, or with the same pair when it was new.
  Grammar input, controlled practice, and every Mentor Text and Mentor Essay use it. A student who uses none
  writes about the device they listen on instead, which is still their own belonging. The headphones are a
  subject, not a prop (Quality Standards §D8): no task asks for them to be out, held up, looked at, or
  photographed, and no image is provided or needed.
- **Draft object (introduced at Lesson 2 Day 2; Lessons 3-4 and all of Module 2 write about it): my bag.** The
  bag the student carries, compared with the same bag when they got it. Category title for Lessons 3-4: "My
  Bag." The student circles one from a printed list at the start of each drafting lesson: **backpack, tote bag,
  shoulder bag, messenger bag, gym bag, purse, laptop sleeve, duffel bag.** Comparing one bag across time
  rather than two different bags means no student is excluded for having owned only one, and it gives Module 2
  its narrative directly: the story of how it changed.
- **Named readers, and the second reader Level 8 requires.** Ladder (practice object): **Tomas**, a friend who
  has asked whether these headphones are worth buying. Draft (draft object): **Priya**, who is about to start a
  year of daily commuting with a laptop to carry and has asked whether a bag like this one lasts. Levels 6-7
  address Priya directly and say why one feature matters to her; Level 5 writes so that Priya could decide,
  without addressing her. **Level 8 writes for Priya and for a second reader with a different stake: Marcus,
  who already carries the same bag and wants to know whether it is worth repairing rather than replacing.**
  Level 8's two body-paragraph groups are one per reader, both reaching the same underlying judgment about the
  bag, and the group written for Marcus is what complicates the framing the Priya group set up. Lesson prompt
  0.2's Level 8 ceiling requires a Scenario with two distinct readers or stakes; Module 1's own Level 8 CSV row
  requires two shifts of tone, register, and abstraction plus an initial framing undercut later in the same
  text. Both are satisfied together here, and neither is dropped: see the Essay Focus section for how.
- **Closing Transfer Check object (Lesson 4 Day 2, a third object used nowhere else in the Set): your keys.**

Both objects are writable at every task Level in the band: a pair of headphones and a bag each yield an
extended paragraph with an implied attitude (Level 5), body paragraphs that explain why a feature matters to a
named reader with one register shift (Level 6), body paragraphs whose selection and emphasis carry an unstated
implication plus visible self-revision (Level 7), and body paragraphs that set up a framing and then
deliberately complicate it, shifting tone and abstraction at least twice (Level 8). **The piece is built in
parts (Conventions §C):** Module 1 drafts the body paragraphs under a working thesis (Levels 6-8) or the one
extended paragraph (Level 5); Module 2 drafts the introduction and conclusion (Levels 6-8) or a second,
narrative paragraph (Level 5), then assembles, peer-edits, and publishes. Neither Module drafts a complete
piece. The draft object sustains the full Pair: an object that has visibly changed carries a description in
Module 1 and the story of the change in Module 2.

## Genre / real-world writing form (Lesson prompt 0.9, Module 1's row)

Level 5: an extended descriptive paragraph that compares, in continuous prose. Levels 6-8: a **comparison
essay**, two instances of the described thing (the bag now and the bag when new). Module 2 inherits the form
unchanged; its own narrative row governs only the second paragraph at Level 5 and the hook and conclusion
material at Levels 6-8.

## Grammar Focus A/B (Module 1, hand-selected off the bank's Level 5 row)

Anchored at **Level 5's row**, Proficient's only Paragraph Composition Level (Lesson prompt 0.4 row
selection). The bank's entry for that row is spent by Advanced Module 1 Set 1 (Section 0), so the pair is
hand-selected from adjacent content serving the same row's purpose. Grammar Focus A/B is **non-centerpiece
content for this Band**: it is taught to the whole room in one sitting each on Lesson 1 and Lesson 2 Day 1,
with Essay Focus (below) as Levels 6-8's structural centerpiece.

- **Focus A (Lesson 1): noun-phrase precision.** The difference between a word that names and a word that
  shows; concrete against abstract nouns; premodification (a stacked adjective order that does not read as a
  list) and postmodification by prepositional phrase and full relative clause ("the strap that has gone shiny
  where my thumb sits"). Level 5's own row is named in one line inside the same grammar box, not taught as the
  point: keep one tense across the paragraph, and let an evaluative word carry attitude.
- **Focus B (Lesson 2 Day 1): reduced clauses and sentence-length variation for detail-packing.** Relative
  clauses reduced to participles ("the strap, fraying at the edge," "worn smooth by three winters"), the comma
  conventions that go with them, and deliberate variation of sentence length so that a short sentence lands
  after two long ones. This is also the tool Level 8's structural variation is built from.
- **Confusable pair (Lesson 2 Day 1): lie / lay** (it lies flat on the desk; I lay it on the desk; it lay there
  all winter), a genuine trap at this Band and directly usable in describing an object at rest.
- Levels 6-8 receive Focus A/B as sentence craft inside the same sessions, with Level 7's cohesion markers
  (what is more, admittedly, this suggests) named where the reduced-clause lesson reaches them.

## Grammar Focus A/B (Module 2, hand-selected; the bank's Alternate column does not exist)

Anchored at the same Level 5 row, hand-selected for Module 2's own purpose and confirmed distinct from Module
1's above and from every logged Set (Section 0).

- **Focus A (Lesson 5): narrative tense management by anteriority.** Past perfect for an event that happened
  before the event being narrated, against past simple for the narrated line itself, so a narrator can reach
  backwards without breaking the sequence. Past continuous for background is named in one line as already
  known, not taught. This is what Levels 7-8's pacing and narrative distance are built on.
- **Focus B (Lesson 6 Day 1): fronting and cleft structures for pacing and emphasis.** Fronted adverbials
  ("Only later did I notice"), it-clefts and wh-clefts ("It was the zip that went first," "What I had not
  expected was how heavy it got"), and where each one puts the weight of a sentence.
- **Contrast pair (Lesson 6 Day 1): used to / would** for habitual past, and why "would" cannot carry a state.
- **Level 5 within Module 2:** the same two focuses at paragraph scale, with the requirement staying one
  detail that implies a feeling without naming it.

## Essay Focus A/B (Levels 6-8)

Anchored at **Level 6's row** (Lesson prompt 0.4c), with Levels 7 and 8 additive inside the same sessions.
Essay type: comparison essay, from Module 1's mapping. Which Module carries which Focus follows the part it
drafts.

- **Module 1 carries Essay Focus B plus the working thesis (Lessons 1-2):** topic sentence plus supporting
  sentences inside a body paragraph, and **working-thesis construction**, direct at Level 6 and indirect at
  Levels 7-8 (Lesson 1); a **general outline** of the body paragraphs, then a **specific outline** down to
  supporting detail, and **combining sentences** for cohesion within and across paragraphs (Lesson 2). The
  working thesis is a line printed at the top of the outline and the draft, never an introduction; the
  introduction is Module 2's.
- **Level 8 additive: the two paragraph groups carry both requirements at once.** The Essay Focus bank's Level 8
  rows, and the Lesson prompt's 0.2 Level 8 ceiling, are written around two named audiences, one body-paragraph
  group each, both reaching the same underlying judgment. **Module 1's own Level 8 CSV row is not
  audience-based**: it requires two deliberate shifts of tone, register, and abstraction, plus a self-revision
  in which an initial framing is deliberately undercut or complicated later in the same text. A Scenario can
  meet both, and this one does: the Priya group frames the bag as worth buying, warmly and somewhat abstractly;
  the Marcus group, written to a reader who already owns one and is weighing repair against replacement, is
  built differently (shorter paragraphs, more concrete, ordered against the first group's order) and its
  accumulating detail complicates the first group's framing without contradicting its judgment. Tone, register,
  and abstraction shift at the group boundary and once inside the Marcus group, giving the row's two shifts.
  The bank's "deliberate structural variation between the sections" is that difference in build. The working
  thesis has to set up both groups without giving away that the second complicates the first. This resolution
  is recorded here so that no lesson re-derives it, and the underlying prompt gap (0.2's Level 8 ceiling assumes
  every Module's Level 8 row is audience-based, which Module 1's is not) is logged in `Index.md`.
- **Module 2 carries Essay Focus A (Lessons 5-6):** the five-paragraph shape; **hook types** in Module 2's
  narrative form (a scenario, an anecdote, a withheld detail; a quote or statistic at Levels 7-8); the
  introduction's connecting sentences that place the thesis last; and the **conclusion** (restate the thesis in
  new words, then a suggestion, prediction, question, or opinion). Module 2's Lesson 7 drafts those around the
  kept bodies. At Level 8 the introduction must set up both framings and the conclusion must not resolve the
  complication flatly.
- **Level 5 bridge (receptive only):** on the same model body paragraphs Levels 6-8 analyze in Lesson 1 Day 1
  (the bodies of the model essay, printed under their working thesis; the complete essay is modeled only at
  Lesson 5's second look), Level 5 finds and underlines one body paragraph's topic sentence and says in one
  sentence what that paragraph is about. A few minutes, positioned next to the model, separate from Level 5's
  own paragraph work. Level 5's own required output stays a single paragraph.

## CSV-objective coverage (Pair): Lesson 8's separate Module 2 verb task

The shared piece's Module 2 part is an introduction and conclusion (Levels 6-8) or a second narrative paragraph
(Level 5), so Module 2's own row is exercised on a fresh prompt as well, in Lesson 8 Day 2's Closing Transfer
Check. **Prompt: the last time you were late to something that mattered.** Unused anywhere else in either Set.

- **Level 5:** three to five sentences narrating it and saying why it mattered, with one detail that lets a
  partner guess how you felt, and no feeling word anywhere.
- **Level 6:** the same, plus one clear shift from narrating to reflecting, written for a reader you know is
  likely to think it was your own fault.
- **Level 7:** the same, with the outcome withheld until the final sentence and one earlier, unexplained detail
  that hints at your real motive.
- **Level 8:** the same, narrated so that a careful reader can tell the account quietly favors the writer
  (a detail left out, your own reaction described more sympathetically than anyone else's), with pacing
  changing at least twice, and the voice never breaking.

## Leveled Mentor Ladder direction (on the practice object, my headphones)

Presented by the teacher on Lesson 1 Day 2, never printed in the packet. Every model is written for Tomas, who
has asked whether these headphones are worth buying, so the ladder shows the same reader-and-decision shape the
draft will use on the bag. At Lesson 1 the Level 6-8 models are body paragraphs under a printed working thesis
line, the part Module 1 drafts; Lesson 5's second look presents the same ladder with each essay Level's
introduction and conclusion added, and Level 5's second, narrative paragraph, the one time the complete piece
is modeled.

- **Level 5 (Mentor Text):** an extended paragraph comparing the headphones now with the pair before them,
  details in a logical order (fit, sound, what they are used for, where they end up at night), one tense
  throughout, and one detail that implies an attitude without stating it ("I still keep the old pair in the
  drawer").
- **Level 6 (Mentor Essay, bodies only):** two body paragraphs to Tomas under a **direct** working thesis
  naming the two points the bodies make, each body led by a topic sentence, one sentence saying why comfort
  over long wear matters specifically to Tomas, and one register shift to address his stated concern.
- **Level 7 (Mentor Essay, bodies only):** the same two bodies under an **indirect** working thesis, an
  implication the reader must infer from selection and emphasis (every detail is about shutting other people
  out, and none of them says so), cohesion markers used sparingly, and visible self-revision: one sentence
  crossed out and replaced in place, left legible.
- **Level 8 (Mentor Essay, bodies only):** four bodies in two groups under a working thesis that sets up both.
  The first group is written for Tomas, who is deciding whether to buy a pair, and frames them warmly and
  somewhat abstractly; the second is written for a friend who already owns the same pair and is deciding
  whether to keep using them, is built differently (shorter, more concrete, ordered against the first group's
  order), and its accumulating details complicate the first framing without ever stating that the writer
  regrets the purchase. Both groups reach the same underlying judgment. Tone, register, and abstraction shift at
  the group boundary and once inside the second group.
- **Lesson 5's second look (Module 2):** each essay Level's model gains an introduction (a hook in Module 2's
  narrative form, connecting sentences, the thesis last) and a conclusion; Level 5's model gains a second,
  narrative paragraph. Students are asked to notice what the added parts do that the bodies could not.

The ladder is distinct in kind across the 5/6 boundary: Level 5 is one paragraph with no thesis; Levels 6-8 are
body paragraphs that serve a thesis, each led by a topic sentence. It is distinct again at the 7/8 boundary:
Levels 6-7 are one continuous group of bodies, Level 8 is two groups whose structures deliberately differ.
