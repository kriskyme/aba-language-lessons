Grammar: Packing the Detail In, and Letting One Sentence Land

Shortening a clause to a participle

Most clauses that come after a noun can drop the pronoun and the verb and keep only a participle. One sentence can then carry two details without reading as a list.

Full clauseShortened
a ring that has hardened over three wintersa ring, hardened over three winters
the fabric that is fraying along the top edgethe fabric fraying along the top edge
the cups, which are covered in a woven fabricthe cups, covered in a woven fabric
the strap that was repaired last springthe strap, repaired last spring

An active clause shortens to an -ing participle: fraying, digging, catching. A passive clause shortens to an -ed participle: hardened, repaired, covered.

The commas travel with the clause

A shortened clause that says which one you mean takes no commas: the strap digging into my shoulder. One that adds information about a thing already named keeps a comma at each end: the left strap, repaired last spring, is the shorter one.

Sentence length

Two long sentences followed by a short one make the short one land. This is the one tool here that changes nothing about the meaning and everything about the weight.

The cups are covered in a woven fabric that has stayed matt where every other pair I have owned went shiny, and the padding under it is firm rather than soft, so it has kept its shape for three years of daily wear. The cable is sunk into a channel along the band, out of the way of coat buttons and door handles alike. Nothing catches.

Put the sentence you want remembered last, and make it short.

The Jobs Inside a Body Paragraph

Every sentence is doing one job

The jobs are: topic sentence (states the paragraph's one point), example, detail, reason, contrast, and link (points forward or back). A paragraph with three sentences doing the same job has a gap somewhere else.

A general outline lists the topic sentences only. A specific outline lists, under each topic sentence, the supporting sentences by job.

Lie and Lay

Which form fits

Lie takes no object: lie, lay, lain, lying. Lay takes one: lay, laid, laid, laying. The past of lie is lay, which is the whole difficulty.

  1. 1.The case (lies / lays) flat in the bottom of my bag.
  2. 2.Last night I (lay / laid) them on the desk without the case.
  3. 3.They have (lain / laid) on that shelf since March.
  4. 4.She (lay / laid) her own pair beside mine to compare them.
  5. 5.The cable (lies / lays) in a channel along the band.
  6. 6.I (lay / laid) down for twenty minutes with them still on.
  7. 7.He has (lain / laid) the manual on top of the box.
  8. 8.The old pair (lies / lays) in a drawer I never open.

Find the Mistakes

A paragraph about a pair of headphones

The old pair, that I kept for the gym, had a ring hardened until it squeaked. The fabric fraying along the top edge of the left cup, was the first thing anyone noticed. The cable of the headphones lay in a channel along the band, and it never caught on anything. The hinge who cracked in the first year was repaired with tape. I laid down with them on most evenings that winter. The case, bought separately, were the only part still in good condition. They were a heavy black old scuffed pair that I wore every day until the port loosened and I finally admitted that the tape on the hinge was not a repair but a decision I had made about how much this pair was worth to me.
