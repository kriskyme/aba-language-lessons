## What this lesson adds

**Grammar Focus B: reduced clauses and sentence-length variation for detail-packing.** Relative clauses reduced
to participles, the comma conventions that go with them, and the deliberate use of a short sentence after two
long ones. Focus B pairs with Lesson 1's Focus A directly: Focus A taught how to put the exact detail after the
noun; Focus B compresses those clauses so a sentence can carry two details without turning into a list, and
then varies the length so that the reader feels which detail matters.

**Confusable pair: lie / lay.** Both describe an object at rest and both come up constantly in describing, and
the past forms are the trap.

**Essay Focus B continued (Levels 6-8):** the inside of the body paragraph again, now from the writer's side.
Level 6 labels what each sentence of a model paragraph is doing; Level 7 combines support sentences for
cohesion; Level 8 rebuilds a paragraph so its shape differs deliberately from the one before it. Then general
and specific outlining side by side.

**The draft object arrives at Day 2 (Conventions §C).** Lessons 1 and 2 Day 1 work on the practice object, the
student's own headphones. Day 2's prewriting introduces the draft object, **my bag**, compared with the same bag
when the student got it, along with its named readers, Priya and Marcus, and the Draft Word Bank. Every later
lesson writes about the bag only. The prewriting is rehearsal: nothing in Lesson 3 depends on any of it being
kept (Quality Standards §D9).

### Phase 1: Focus B mini-lesson, rule and examples (20 min)

**Grammar box: Packing the detail in, and letting one sentence land.** (One box, whole class.)

Rule 1, reducing a relative clause. Yesterday you put the exact detail after the noun in a relative clause.
Most of those clauses can drop their pronoun and their verb and keep only a participle, which lets one sentence
carry two details without reading as a list.

| Full relative clause | Reduced |
|---|---|
| a ring that has hardened over three winters | a ring, hardened over three winters |
| the fabric that is fraying along the top edge | the fabric fraying along the top edge |
| the cups, which are covered in a woven fabric | the cups, covered in a woven fabric |
| the strap that was repaired last spring | the strap, repaired last spring |

An active clause reduces to an **-ing** participle (fraying, digging, catching). A passive clause reduces to an
**-ed** participle (hardened, repaired, covered).

Rule 2, the commas travel with the clause. A defining reduced clause, one that says which one you mean, takes
no commas: "the strap digging into my shoulder." A non-defining one, one that adds information about a thing
already identified, keeps a comma at each end: "the left strap, repaired last spring, is the shorter one."

Rule 3, sentence length. Two long sentences followed by a short one make the short one land. This is the one
tool in this lesson that does nothing to the meaning and everything to the weight.

> The cups are covered in a woven fabric that has stayed matt where every other pair I have owned went shiny,
> and the padding under it is firm rather than soft, so it has kept its shape for three years of daily wear.
> The cable is sunk into a channel along the band, out of the way of coat buttons and door handles alike.
> Nothing catches.

"Nothing catches" is two words after two sentences of thirty. Put the sentence you want remembered last and
make it short.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** take one long sentence offered by the class about something in
this room, and cut it into a long sentence and a short one. Ask which version puts the weight where the class
wanted it. Write the class's cut, not a prepared one.

**Essay Focus B continued, Levels 6-8 (folded into the same 20 minutes; Level 5 hears it and is not tested on
it):** every sentence in a body paragraph is doing one of a small number of jobs, and naming the job is what
makes a paragraph revisable. The jobs are: **topic sentence** (states the paragraph's one point), **example**,
**detail**, **reason**, **contrast**, and **link** (points forward or back). A paragraph with three sentences
doing the same job is a paragraph with a gap somewhere else. Two outlines follow from this: a **general
outline** lists the topic sentences only, and a **specific outline** lists, under each topic sentence, the
supporting sentences by job.

### Phase 2: Controlled practice B (30 min)

Two tracks run at once; the teacher circulates between them.

**Level 5, Activity 1 (identify and reduce, 8 items):** eight phrases about a pair of headphones. Mark each **F**
if the part after the noun is a full relative clause and **R** if it is already reduced. Then rewrite the F ones
as reduced clauses, keeping the commas where they belong.

1. a ring that has hardened until it squeaks
2. the fabric fraying along the top edge of the left cup
3. the cable, which was replaced two winters ago, runs inside the band
4. the padding compressed flat under the headband
5. the hinge that cracked in the first year
6. the charging port, which is loosening at one corner
7. the earcup resting against my glasses
8. the case that was bought separately

**Answer note:** F: 1, 3, 5, 6, 8. R: 2, 4, 7. Reduced versions: 1 "a ring hardened until it squeaks"; 3 "the
cable, replaced two winters ago, runs inside the band"; 5 "the hinge cracked in the first year" (accept, and
point out in circulation that this one now reads as a full sentence, which is why some clauses are better left
whole); 6 "the charging port, loosening at one corner"; 8 "the case bought separately." Item 5 is the trap and
is meant to be discussed, not marked wrong.

**Level 5, Activity 2 (combine, 8 pairs):** combine each pair into one sentence using a reduced clause. Keep the
first sentence's subject.

1. The headband is heavier than it looks. It is wrapped in a thick synthetic leather.
2. The left cup sits lower than the right. It was dropped on a platform two years ago.
3. The mesh has torn along one edge. It covers the driver inside the cup.
4. The cable runs inside a channel. The channel is molded along the top of the band.
5. The padding is firm rather than soft. It is made from a denser foam than most.
6. The ring on the hinge squeaks now. It has hardened over three winters.
7. The case zips shut around them. It came in the same box.
8. The port takes the cable at one angle only. It has loosened at one corner.

**Answer note:** 1 "The headband, wrapped in a thick synthetic leather, is heavier than it looks." 2 "The left
cup, dropped on a platform two years ago, sits lower than the right." 3 "The mesh covering the driver inside the
cup has torn along one edge." 4 "The cable runs inside a channel molded along the top of the band." 5 "The
padding, made from a denser foam than most, is firm rather than soft." 6 "The ring on the hinge, hardened over
three winters, squeaks now." 7 "The case that came in the same box zips shut around them" reduces to "The case
from the same box zips shut around them"; accept either. 8 "The port, loosened at one corner, takes the cable at
one angle only." Commas are part of the answer in 1, 2, 5, 6, and 8, and are absent in 3 and 4.

**Levels 6-8, Activity 3 (the jobs inside a paragraph):** the packet reprints one body paragraph from "The Pair
I Replaced," its sentences numbered. Each Level does a different thing with it.

- **Level 6:** label each numbered sentence with its job (topic sentence, example, detail, reason, contrast,
  link), then say in one line which job the paragraph uses most and whether that is a strength here.
- **Level 7 (a different shape):** combine sentences 2, 3, and 4 into two sentences so that the join carries the
  logic rather than an "and," then say in one line what the new pair does that the original three did not.
- **Level 8 (a different shape):** rewrite the paragraph so that its shape is deliberately different from the
  paragraph before it in the model: if that one builds from general to concrete, build this one the other way,
  and end on the shortest sentence in the paragraph. Then name in one line what the reader now notices first.

**Paragraph reprinted for Activity 3** (the padding body paragraph, sentences numbered):

> (1) The first difference is the padding. (2) The old cups were covered in a soft foam that flattened inside a
> year, so by the end the metal band underneath pressed on the top of my head after about forty minutes. (3) The
> pair I have now uses a firmer foam under a fabric that has not gone shiny, and I have worn them through a whole
> shift without noticing them. (4) That is the test that matters on a long day.

**Answer note:** Level 6, (1) topic sentence, (2) detail with a reason folded in, (3) contrast, (4) link that
states the criterion; the paragraph runs on contrast, which is right for a comparison essay. Level 7, any
two-sentence version in which the old pair's failure and the new pair's fix sit in one sentence and the
criterion in the other, for example: "Where the old cups used a soft foam that flattened inside a year, leaving
the metal band to press on my head after forty minutes, this pair uses a firmer foam under a fabric that has not
gone shiny. I have worn them through a whole shift without noticing them, which is the test that matters on a
long day." Level 8, any rebuild that inverts the order and closes short; the reader now notices the verdict
before the evidence.

**Levels 6-8, Activity 4 (two outlines side by side):** the packet prints the working thesis and the three topic
sentences of "The Pair I Replaced."

- **Level 6:** write the **general outline** as it stands (thesis, then the three topic sentences), then add one
  line saying what a general outline cannot tell you about a paragraph.
- **Level 7 (a different shape):** write a **specific outline** of the second body paragraph, listing each
  supporting sentence by job under its topic sentence, and mark with an arrow the one detail you would cut first
  if the paragraph ran long.
- **Level 8 (a different shape):** write a specific outline of the third body paragraph, then write, underneath
  it, a second specific outline of the same content built for a reader who already owns the pair. Mark the one
  line that changes job between the two.

**Answer note:** Level 6, the general outline is the thesis plus "The first difference is the padding," "The
second difference is the cable," "You asked whether they are worth the money, Tomas"; what it cannot tell you is
whether each paragraph actually supports its own topic sentence. Level 7, any specific outline naming jobs; the
first cut is usually the detail about coat buttons, which is vivid but repeats the point. Level 8, the second
outline turns the price sentence from evidence into a concession, which is the line that changes job.

### Phase 3: Confusable-pair drill and mixed editing (25 min)

**Whole class, Activity 5 (lie / lay, 8 items):** choose the correct form. "Lie" takes no object (lie, lay,
lain, lying); "lay" takes one (lay, laid, laid, laying). The past of "lie" is "lay," which is the whole
difficulty.

1. The case (lies / lays) flat in the bottom of my bag.
2. Last night I (lay / laid) them on the desk without the case.
3. They have (lain / laid) on that shelf since March.
4. She (lay / laid) her own pair beside mine to compare them.
5. The cable (lies / lays) in a channel along the band.
6. I (lay / laid) down for twenty minutes with them still on.
7. He has (lain / laid) the manual on top of the box.
8. The old pair (lies / lays) in a drawer I never open.

**Answer note:** 1 lies; 2 laid; 3 lain; 4 laid; 5 lies; 6 lay; 7 laid; 8 lies. Items 2 and 6 are the pair that
catches people: 6 is the past of "lie" and takes no object, 2 has "them" as its object and is the past of
"lay."

**All Levels, Activity 6 (mixed editing paragraph):** one paragraph about a pair of headphones with **7 verified
errors**, mixing Lesson 1's Focus A with today's Focus B. **Levels 5-7:** find and correct the seven. **Level 8
(a heavier tier, a different shape):** the same seven, then cut the last sentence into a long one and a short one
so that the paragraph ends on the short one.

> The old pair, that I kept for the gym, had a ring hardened until it squeaked. The fabric fraying along the top
> edge of the left cup, was the first thing anyone noticed. The cable of the headphones lay in a channel along
> the band, and it never caught on anything. The hinge who cracked in the first year was repaired with tape. I
> laid down with them on most evenings that winter. The case, bought separately, were the only part still in
> good condition. They were a heavy black old scuffed pair that I wore every day until the port loosened and I
> finally admitted that the tape on the hinge was not a repair but a decision I had made about how much this pair
> was worth to me.

The seven errors: (1) "The old pair, that I kept for the gym," is a defining clause and takes no commas; (2)
"The fabric fraying along the top edge of the left cup, was" has a stray comma between subject and verb, since
the reduced clause here is defining; (3) "the cable of the headphones" to "the headphones' cable"; (4) "The
hinge who cracked" to "The hinge that cracked"; (5) "I laid down" to "I lay down", no object; (6) "The case ...
were" to "was"; (7) "a heavy black old scuffed pair" to "an old scuffed black pair" (age before color, and two
in front is the guidance, so "a scuffed black pair, old and heavy" is also accepted). Count checked: 7.

**Answer note:** Level 8's cut has no single right answer. The last sentence runs to over forty words; any
version that ends on a short independent sentence is accepted, for example: "... until the port loosened. The
tape on the hinge was never a repair. It was a decision."

### Phase 1: Required-feature warm-up (25 min)

Still on the practice object, the student's own headphones, and still for Tomas. Each Level writes only its own
required feature, once, so that the feature is in hand before the prewriting turns to a new object.

**Level 5, Activity 7:** write two sentences about your own headphones. The first names one part exactly and
says what has happened to it. The second is a detail that would let a partner guess how you feel about the pair,
with no feeling word in it. Read only the second to your partner; your partner says what they guessed.

**Level 6, Activity 8 (a different shape):** write two sentences. The first says why one named feature of your
own headphones matters to Tomas, who cannot wear anything that presses after half an hour. The second is the
opening of a register shift, a sentence that turns to address him directly and prepares him for something less
flattering.

**Level 7, Activity 9 (a different shape):** write three sentences about your own headphones that between them
imply something you never state. Underneath, write in one line what you intend a reader to infer. Fold the page
so the line is hidden, hand it to a partner, and have them write what they inferred before you unfold it.

**Level 8, Activity 10 (a different shape):** write one sentence about your own headphones twice: once pitched
high and general, once clipped and concrete. Then write one line naming what the shift between them does that
neither version does alone.

**Answer note:** no key; these are original. Circulation check: Level 5's second sentence contains no feeling
word; Level 6's first sentence names Tomas's concern rather than restating the feature; Level 7's inference line
is a claim about the writer or the object, not a summary of the three sentences; Level 8's two versions say the
same thing and differ only in register and abstraction.

### Phase 2: Prewriting on the draft object, differentiated by task Level (30 min)

**Introducing the draft object.** The Set's writing now moves to a second object of the student's own: **my
bag**, compared with the same bag when they got it. The category and its choice list are printed; the student
circles one. Comparing one bag across time rather than two different bags means nobody is excluded for having
owned only one.

**Circle the one you carry most:** backpack · tote bag · shoulder bag · messenger bag · gym bag · purse ·
laptop sleeve · duffel bag

**The named readers for the draft.** **Priya** is about to start a year of daily commuting with a laptop to
carry, and has asked whether a bag like this one lasts. **Marcus** already carries the same kind of bag and
wants to know whether it is worth repairing rather than replacing. Levels 5 to 7 write for Priya. Level 8 writes
for both, in two groups of body paragraphs.

The Draft Word Bank (above, and printed here in the packet) is the Set's five groups refilled for the bag.
Nothing produced in this phase is needed in Lesson 3: this is rehearsal, and Lesson 3 rebuilds its own plan
from its own page.

- **Level 5, Activity 11:** list six details about your bag in the order you would write them, one line each.
  Beside two of them write the word from the Draft Word Bank that names the part exactly. Put a star beside the
  one detail that would let a reader guess how you feel about the bag without a feeling word.
- **Level 6, Activity 12 (a different shape):** write a direct working thesis to Priya naming two points, then
  one topic sentence for each of your two body paragraphs. Underneath, write the one sentence in which you will
  say why one of those points matters to Priya specifically.
- **Level 7, Activity 13 (a different shape):** write an indirect working thesis, then a specific outline of one
  body paragraph: the topic sentence and three supporting details in order. Mark with an arrow the detail chosen
  to carry the implication, and write in one line what the implication is.
- **Level 8, Activity 14 (a different shape):** write the working thesis that sets up both reader groups without
  revealing the complication. Then two topic sentences for the Priya group and two for the Marcus group, with a
  note beside the Marcus group saying how its paragraphs will be built differently. Mark with an arrow the one

**Answer note:** no key. Circulation check: the bag, not the headphones, is the subject of every line; Level 6's
thesis names its two points; Level 7's marked detail is a fact rather than an adjective; Level 8's two groups
reach the same judgment about the bag, and the note about the Marcus group describes a difference in build, not
in content.

### Phase 3: Prewriting share and board synthesis (20 min)

Two or three students from each Level read one line from their prewriting: a detail, a topic sentence, or a
thesis. **Board-dependent moment:** build one shared idea board from what is read out, grouped not by Level but
by what part of a bag the detail names (strap, base, zip, lining, pocket). The board ends up holding more parts
than any single handout lists, which is the point: the class has just shown itself how much of one ordinary
object is describable. The teacher writes what is said, not a prepared list.
