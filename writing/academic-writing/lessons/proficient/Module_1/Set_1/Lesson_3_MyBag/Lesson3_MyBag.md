## What this lesson produces

Module 1's part of the Pair's piece, on the draft object, complete by the end of Day 1 and improved on Day 2.
The piece is **built in parts** (Conventions §C): this Module writes the body paragraphs under a working thesis
line at Levels 6-8, or the one extended descriptive paragraph at Level 5. **No Level writes an introduction or a
conclusion here.** Module 2 writes those, at Pair position 7, around the bodies kept from this Set.

**The draft object.** The bag the student carries, compared with the same bag when they got it. The choice list
is printed again below and in the packet; the student circles one at the start of Day 1. The bag is a subject,
not a prop: nothing is brought or taken out, and every detail is written from what the student already knows of
it.

**The readers.** **Priya** is about to start a year of daily commuting with a laptop to carry, and has asked
whether a bag like this one lasts. **Marcus** already carries the same kind of bag and wants to know whether it
is worth repairing rather than replacing. Levels 5 to 7 write for Priya. Level 8 writes two groups of body
paragraphs, one for each.

**Nothing from Lesson 2 is needed.** Lesson 2's prewriting was rehearsal (Quality Standards §D9). This lesson
rebuilds its own plan on its own page, reprints the Draft Word Bank, and restates the working-thesis kinds. A
student who was absent on Day 2 of Lesson 2 can start every task here.

**Draft Word Bank for the bag** (reprinted in both units of the packet):

- Shape and size: slouched, boxy, deep, flat, bulging, narrow
- Fit and feel: padded, stiff, soft, heavy, digging, balanced
- Condition: frayed, scuffed, faded, stretched, stained, split
- Use: commuting, the gym, groceries, travel, a laptop, overnight
- Parts to name exactly: the strap, the base, the zip, the lining, the buckle, the front pocket

**Drafting targets (teacher circulation check only; no word range reaches the packet, Style Guide §E).** Level
5, one paragraph, 6 to 9 sentences, roughly 90 to 150 words. Level 6, two or three body paragraphs, roughly 200
to 300 words. Level 7, three body paragraphs, roughly 300 to 450 words. Level 8, four or five body paragraphs in
two reader groups, roughly 450 to 650 words. Students count paragraphs, or sentences at Level 5, and nothing
else.

### Phase 1: Quick plan (10 min)

**Circle the one you carry most:** backpack · tote bag · shoulder bag · messenger bag · gym bag · purse ·
laptop sleeve · duffel bag

Then, on this page:

- **Level 5, Task A:** write four details about your bag in the order you will use them, one line each. Beside
  one of them write a star: that is the detail a reader will use to guess how you feel about the bag.
- **Level 6, Task B:** write your direct working thesis, naming two points, then one topic sentence for each
  body paragraph.
- **Level 7, Task C:** write your indirect working thesis, then one topic sentence per body paragraph, then
  three supporting details under one of them.
- **Level 8, Task D:** write your working thesis, then two topic sentences under the heading "For Priya" and two
  under the heading "For Marcus."

**Reminders printed with the plan** (nothing here is recalled from an earlier lesson): a **direct** working
thesis names the points the bodies will make. An **indirect** working thesis signals that points exist without
naming them. At Level 8 the thesis sets up both reader groups and does not reveal that the second will
complicate the first. The working thesis is one line at the top of the draft; it is not an introduction.

_Teacher circulation, not printed:_ does each plan already carry that Level's required feature? A Level 5 plan
with a star on an adjective rather than a fact needs one more minute. A Level 8 plan whose two groups say the
same thing about the bag in the same order has not yet split.

### Phase 2: Independent drafting, complete today (50 min)

Every Level writes Module 1's part in full today. Level 5 writes the extended paragraph. Levels 6 to 8 write
the body paragraphs under the working thesis line, with no introduction and no conclusion.

- **Level 5, Task E:** write your paragraph about your bag as it is now and as it was when you got it. Put the
  details in the order you planned. Somewhere in it, one detail lets a reader guess how you feel about the bag,
  and no feeling word appears anywhere. Count your sentences at the end and write the number in the box.
- **Level 6, Task F:** write your body paragraphs under your working thesis line. Each one opens with its topic
  sentence. In one of them, say why one feature matters to Priya specifically, given that she will carry a
  laptop every day for a year. Once, turn and address her directly, and let the sentence sound different from
  the ones around it. Count your paragraphs at the end.
- **Level 7, Task G:** write your body paragraphs under your indirect working thesis. Choose and weight the
  details so that a reader can work out something you never write down. Use at least two of these where they
  fit: admittedly, what's more, this suggests, in fairness. When you change something as you write, cross it out
  and write the new version beside it. Do not erase. Count your paragraphs at the end.
- **Level 8, Task H:** write your two groups of body paragraphs under your working thesis. The Priya group comes
  first. The Marcus group is built differently from it: if the first group runs from general to concrete, run
  the second the other way, and let its sentences be shorter. Somewhere in the Marcus group, one detail
  complicates what the Priya group established, without contradicting your judgment. Both groups end in the same
  place. When you change something as you write, cross it out and write the new version beside it. Count your
  paragraphs at the end.

**Board-dependent moment:** a running "strong sentence" board. As students volunteer a line from the draft in
progress, write it up unedited and underline the part that does the work: a reduced clause, an exact part name,
a short sentence after two long ones. The board grows through the block and is not prepared in advance.

_Teacher circulation, not printed:_ check the required feature is appearing while the draft is being written,
not only at the end. At Level 8, check by the halfway point that the second group has actually started to look
different on the page; a student who has written two groups of identical shape has written one group twice and
needs to be caught before the block ends.

### Phase 3: Mid-draft share-out (15 min)

Three or four students, one from each Level where possible, read one sentence from their draft. The class says
what the sentence does, not whether they like it. Add the sentences to the strong-sentence board.

## DAY 2: CHECK AND IMPROVE YOUR WRITING (75 MIN)

```
0                    25                              55                      75  (minutes)
|-------Phase 1-------|-----------Phase 2-------------|-------Phase 3--------|
| Self-edit checklist | Improve the piece from the    | Final line check     |
| pass                | marks                         |                      |
```

The draft is complete from Day 1. This day opens directly on checking it. Marks go on the page: circle,
underline, box. Cross out rather than erase, so the first version stays legible beside the second.

### Phase 1 and 2 combined into one Task per Level: check, then improve (55 min)

Each Level has one lettered Task holding both halves: the check questions first, then the improvement steps,
answered on the same page (Style Guide §F).

**Level 5, Task A.**

1. Underline every verb. Is the paragraph in one tense, apart from the sentences about when you got the bag?
2. Circle one reduced clause. Does it have its commas right: none if it says which one you mean, two if it adds
   information about a thing already named?
3. Box the one detail a reader would use to guess how you feel. Is it a fact about the bag rather than an
   adjective about you?
4. Read the boxed detail to a partner. Ask what they guessed. Write their guess here: ______________________
5. Now improve it. Replace one flat word with a more exact one from the Draft Word Bank. Add one more concrete
   detail about a part you have not yet named. If your partner's guess was not what you meant, change the boxed
   detail rather than adding a sentence that explains it.

**Level 6, Task B.**

1. Read your working thesis. Does it name the points your body paragraphs actually make? If a paragraph makes a
   point the thesis does not name, mark the paragraph with an arrow.
2. Underline the first sentence of each body paragraph. Does each one state that paragraph's single point?
3. Circle the sentence that says why a feature matters to Priya. Does it name what Priya will be doing with the
   bag, rather than repeating the feature?
4. Box the sentence where you turn and address Priya. Does it sound different from the sentences around it?
5. Now improve it. Rewrite any topic sentence that states a topic rather than a point. Add to the circled
   sentence one thing about Priya's year of commuting that the feature answers. If nothing is boxed, write the
   sentence that turns to her and mark where it goes.

**Level 7, Task C.**

1. Read your indirect working thesis. Does it signal that points exist without naming them?
2. Underline the three or four details you are relying on to carry the implication. Are they facts about the
   bag?
3. Hand the draft to a partner and ask them to write in the margin what they think you are not saying. Write
   what they wrote here: ______________________
4. Circle each cohesion word you used (admittedly, what's more, this suggests, in fairness). Is each one doing
   the job it names?
5. Now improve it. If your partner did not reach the implication, do not add a sentence stating it: change or
   add one detail so the reader has more to work from. Replace one plain word with a more exact one. Leave every
   crossing-out visible.

**Level 8, Task D.**

1. Read your working thesis. Does it set up both groups without revealing that the second complicates the first?
2. Draw a line across the page where the Priya group ends and the Marcus group begins. Are the two groups built
   differently: sentence length, order, level of abstraction?
3. Mark with a T each place where the tone or the level of abstraction changes. You need at least two.
4. Box the detail in the Marcus group that complicates what the Priya group established. Does it complicate the
   framing without contradicting your judgment?
5. Hand the draft to a partner. Ask them to write down the judgment each group reaches. Are the two judgments
   the same? Write what they wrote here: ______________________
6. Now improve it. If the two groups are not yet built differently, shorten every sentence in one paragraph of
   the Marcus group and put its most concrete line last. If your partner found only one tone change, sharpen the
   one you meant rather than adding a third. Leave every crossing-out visible.

**Answer note:** none of these has a key; they are checks on the student's own draft. Circulation priorities, in
order: a Level 5 paragraph that states the feeling outright; a Level 6 paragraph whose topic sentence names a
topic rather than a point; a Level 7 implication that has been written down somewhere; a Level 8 draft whose two
groups differ only in who is addressed.

### Phase 3: Final line check (20 min)

Each student reads their last sentence to a partner, then reads the partner's. The only question asked is
whether that sentence is the one the piece should end on for now, given that Module 2 will add an ending later.
Two or three pairs are cold-called to read their closing lines aloud; add them to the strong-sentence board.

Nothing is collected. The draft stays with the student for Lesson 4.
