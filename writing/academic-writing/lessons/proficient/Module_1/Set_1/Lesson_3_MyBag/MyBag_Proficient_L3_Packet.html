Unit 3A: Describing My Bag

Writing · Describing Proficient 1.1.3.0

Objective: I can describe something of my own by choosing which details to give and how much weight to give them, so that a reader sees it the way I want them to.

Circle the one you carry most

backpack · tote bag · shoulder bag · messenger bag · gym bag · purse · laptop sleeve · duffel bag

Write about this bag as it is now and as it was when you got it.

Your readers

Priya is about to start a year of daily commuting with a laptop to carry, and has asked whether a bag like this one lasts.

Marcus already carries the same kind of bag and wants to know whether it is worth repairing rather than replacing.

The Working Thesis

Which kind you are writing

A direct working thesis names the points the body paragraphs will make.

An indirect working thesis signals that points exist without naming them.

A thesis for two readers sets up both groups of body paragraphs and does not reveal that the second group will complicate what the first establishes.

The working thesis is one line at the top of your draft. It is not a beginning: the beginning and the ending are written in the next unit, around the paragraphs you keep.

  2. 2.Write one topic sentence for each body paragraph.

  1. 1.Write your indirect working thesis:
  2. 2.Write one topic sentence per body paragraph.

Task D. ★★★★ Plan two groups of body paragraphs.

  1. 1.Write your working thesis:
  2. 2.Under the heading "For Priya", write two topic sentences.
  3. 3.Under the heading "For Marcus", write two topic sentences.

Check and Improve Your Writing

Task A. ★ Check your paragraph, then improve it.

  1. 1.Underline every verb. Is the paragraph in one tense, apart from the sentences about when you got the bag?
  2. 2.Circle one shortened clause. Does it have its commas right: none if it says which one you mean, two if it adds information about a thing already named?
  3. 3.Box the one detail a reader would use to guess how you feel. Is it a fact about the bag rather than an adjective about you?
  4. 4.Read the boxed detail to a partner. Write what they guessed:
  5. 5.Replace one flat word with a more exact one from the Draft Word Bank.
  6. 6.Add one more concrete detail about a part you have not yet named.
  7. 7.If your partner's guess was not what you meant, change the boxed detail rather than adding a sentence that explains it.

Task B. ★★ Check your body paragraphs, then improve them.

  1. 1.Read your working thesis. Does it name the points your body paragraphs actually make? Mark with an arrow any paragraph that makes a point the thesis does not name.
  2. 2.Underline the first sentence of each body paragraph. Does each one state that paragraph's single point?
  3. 3.Circle the sentence that says why a feature matters to Priya. Does it name what she will be doing with the bag, rather than repeating the feature?
  4. 4.Box the sentence where you turn and address Priya. Does it sound different from the sentences around it?
  5. 5.Rewrite any topic sentence that states a topic rather than a point.
  6. 6.Add to the circled sentence one thing about Priya's year of commuting that the feature answers.
  7. 7.If nothing is boxed, write the sentence that turns to her and mark where it goes.

Task C. ★★★ Check your body paragraphs, then improve them.

  1. 1.Read your indirect working thesis. Does it signal that points exist without naming them?
  2. 2.Underline the three or four details you are relying on to carry the implication. Are they facts about the bag?
  3. 3.Hand the draft to a partner and ask them to write in the margin what they think you are not saying. Write what they wrote:
  4. 4.Circle each of these you used: admittedly, what's more, this suggests, in fairness. Is each one doing the job it names?
  5. 5.If your partner did not reach the implication, do not add a sentence stating it. Change or add one detail so the reader has more to work from.
  6. 6.Replace one plain word with a more exact one. Leave every crossing-out visible.

Task D. ★★★★ Check your two groups, then improve them.

  1. 1.Read your working thesis. Does it set up both groups without revealing that the second complicates the first?
  2. 2.Draw a line across the page where the Priya group ends and the Marcus group begins. Are the two built differently in sentence length, order, and level of abstraction?
  3. 3.Mark with a T each place where the tone or the level of abstraction changes. You need at least two.
  4. 4.Box the detail in the Marcus group that complicates what the Priya group established. Does it complicate the framing without contradicting your judgment?
  5. 5.Hand the draft to a partner. Ask them to write down the judgment each group reaches. Are the two the same? Write what they wrote:
  6. 6.If the two groups are not yet built differently, shorten every sentence in one paragraph of the Marcus group and put its most concrete line last.
  7. 7.If your partner found only one tone change, sharpen the one you meant rather than adding a third. Leave every crossing-out visible.
