## What this lesson establishes for the Set

**Scenario.** Every student describes the headphones or earbuds they use most, from memory, comparing them with
the pair they used before or with the same pair when they were new. A student who uses none writes about the
device they listen on instead. The headphones are a subject, not a prop: nothing is brought, taken out, held
up, or looked at, and no picture is provided or needed. The Mentor Ladder's models are written for Tomas, a
friend who has asked whether this pair is worth buying and who has said he cannot wear anything that presses on
his head for more than half an hour, so the reader-and-concern shape of the Set is visible from Lesson 1.
Lesson 2 Day 2 introduces the draft object (my bag, compared with the same bag when it was new) and its named
readers, Priya and Marcus.

**Grammar Focus A (hand-selected off the Level 5 row): noun-phrase precision.** Naming words against showing
words; concrete against abstract nouns; premodification and its order; postmodification by prepositional phrase
and by relative clause. Level 5's own row is named in one line inside the same box, not taught as the point:
keep one tense across the paragraph unless the time changes, and let an evaluative word carry attitude instead
of a feeling word. Grammar Focus A/B is non-centerpiece content for this Band (Lesson prompt 0.4): it is
delivered to the whole room in one sitting, and Levels 6-8 take it as sentence craft rather than as their
structural centerpiece.

**Essay Focus B plus the working thesis (Level 6 row), the centerpiece track for Levels 6-8:** this Module
drafts the body paragraphs of the Pair's essay, so it teaches the body paragraph (a topic sentence stating one
point, then sentences that support it) and the working thesis those bodies serve, direct at Level 6, indirect at
Levels 7-8, and at Level 8 a thesis that sets up two reader groups (Conventions §C). The five-paragraph shape,
hook types, the introduction, and the conclusion are Module 2's Essay Focus A; they are named once here so
students know what is coming, and not practiced. Level 5 gets a receptive bridge on the same model body
paragraphs.

**Word Bank for the headphones** (printed in both units of the packet; the groups are refilled for the draft

### Phase 1: Hook, Skill Spotlight (15 min)

**Hook (8 min):** Ask two volunteers to name one thing they own and use every day, and to describe it in one
phrase from memory. Write both phrases on the board exactly as they are said ("old black headphones," "a
scratched blue bottle"). Ask the class: "Which of these two do you actually see? What would you have to add to
the other one to see it?" Take 2-3 quick oral answers and push each toward one concrete part ("the ring on the
hinge has gone grey," "the lid does not sit straight any more"). Keep the language near Level 5.

**Skill Spotlight (7 min):** "This week we're practicing describing something of our own by choosing which
details to give and how much weight to give them, so that a reader sees it the way we want them to." One
sentence, the same for every Level; the essay Levels' own features (a named reader, an implication, two
shifts) are stated inside their tasks, not here. Say that the Closing Transfer Check at the end of Lesson 4
will ask everyone to do exactly this on something new.

### Phase 2: Focus A mini-lesson, rule and examples (30 min)

**Grammar box: Saying it exactly.** (One box, whole class.)

Rule 1, naming against showing: a naming phrase tells the reader the category. A showing phrase makes the
reader see the thing. Most flat description is a string of naming phrases.

| Naming (tells) | Showing (makes you see) |
|---|---|
| good headphones | headphones that still sit flat after three years |
| the strap is old | the headband has gone shiny where my thumb sits |
| nice sound | a low end you feel more than hear |
| the case is damaged | the zip catches on the same tooth every time |

Rule 2, concrete against abstract nouns: comfort, quality, and design are abstract; the padding on the left cup
is concrete. An abstract noun is earned by putting a concrete one next to it. "The comfort is good" says
nothing; "the padding is firm rather than soft, so it has not flattened" is the same claim with something to
see.

Rule 3, premodification and its order: adjectives stack in a fixed order (opinion, size, age, shape, color,
material). Two are usually enough. Three or more read as a list rather than a description, and the fix is to
move the extras behind the noun.

| Stacked, reads as a list | Two in front, the rest behind |
|---|---|
| a worn heavy black plastic scratched headband | a heavy black headband, worn and scratched where my thumb sits |
| a cracked small grey rubber ring | a small grey ring, cracked right through |

Rule 4, postmodification: the most exact part of a description usually sits after the noun.

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| prepositional phrase | the crack in the left hinge; the mesh inside the right cup |
| relative clause, defining (no commas) | the pair that I keep for the bus |
| relative clause, non-defining (commas both sides) | the left cup, which I dropped on the platform, is looser |
| reduced to a participle | a ring, hardened by three winters |

Use "that" or "which" for a thing, never "who". A defining clause takes no commas; a non-defining clause takes
a comma at each end.

**Level 5's own row, named here and not drilled:** a description stays in one tense unless the time changes, and
an evaluative word (a bit, at least, worth it, rather than) carries attitude without a feeling word.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** on a fresh prompt (something visible in this room, not a
student's own belonging), build one noun phrase with the class that carries two premodifiers and one
postmodifier: "a heavy grey door that only closes if you lift it." Write the class's version, not a prepared
one; box the two adjectives in front and underline the part after the noun.

**Essay Focus B and the working thesis, Levels 6-8 (folded into the same 30 minutes; Level 5 hears it and is
not tested on it):**

**Essay-structure box: Inside a body paragraph, and the working thesis.**

1. A body paragraph opens with a topic sentence that states its one point. Every sentence after it supports that
   point: an example, a detail, a reason, or a contrast. A sentence supporting a different point belongs in a
   different paragraph.
2. The body paragraphs serve one thesis. In this Module you write that thesis as a **working thesis**, a single
   line at the top of your outline and your draft. The introduction that will carry it, and the conclusion, are
   written in the next Module, around the bodies you keep.
3. A **direct** working thesis names the points the bodies will make (Level 6): "For the way you use headphones,
   this pair is worth the money for two reasons: the padding holds its shape, and the microphone is usable
   outdoors." An **indirect** working thesis signals that points exist without naming them (Levels 7-8): "These
   have earned their place for reasons that have very little to do with sound."
4. At Level 8 the working thesis has one more job: it sets up **two groups of body paragraphs written for two
   different readers**, and it does so without giving away that the second group will complicate what the first
   group establishes. Both groups reach the same underlying judgment.
5. What is coming, not practiced here: the finished essay has an introduction (a hook, connecting sentences, the
   thesis last) and a conclusion (the thesis in new words, then a closing move). Those are Module 2's.

### Phase 3: Controlled practice A, part 1 (30 min)

Two tracks run at once; the teacher circulates between them.

**Level 5, Activity 1 (classify and repair, 8 items):** eight phrases and sentences about a pair of headphones.
Mark each **N** if it names and **S** if it shows. Then, for the three marked N, write a showing version using
one part named exactly.

1. a comfortable pair
2. the padding is firm rather than soft, so it has not flattened
3. the sound quality is excellent
4. the cable runs inside a channel along the band, so it does not catch on a coat button
5. they are in bad condition
6. a small grey ring on the left hinge, hardened until it squeaks
7. the mesh inside the right cup has torn along one edge
8. the design is very good

**Answer note:** N: 1, 3, 5, 8. S: 2, 4, 6, 7. Showing versions for 1, 3, 5, and 8 vary; accept any that names a
part exactly and says what that part does or has become. Item 8 is the hardest to repair, because "design" has
to be traded for a decision the object visibly makes.

**Level 5, Activity 2 (fill in a model paragraph, 8 blanks):** a paragraph about a pair of headphones with eight
blanks. Each bracket offers either a base verb, or a flat phrase against a precise one. Fill it so that the
paragraph stays in one tense and chooses the more exact phrase every time.

> These are the pair I (1. buy: ___) three years ago, and they (2. be: ___) the ones I reach for first. The cups
> are covered in (3. a nice material / a woven fabric that has stayed matt: ___), and the padding underneath
> (4. be: ___) firm rather than soft. The headband is (5. quite heavy / heavier than it looks: ___), which is a
> bit much on a hot train. The cable (6. run: ___) inside a channel along the band, so (7. it is convenient /
> nothing catches on a coat button: ___). A ring on the left hinge (8. is bad now / has gone hard and grey:
> ___).

**Answer note:** 1 bought; 2 are; 3 a woven fabric that has stayed matt; 4 is; 5 heavier than it looks; 6 runs;
7 nothing catches on a coat button; 8 has gone hard and grey. The tense frame is present, with one past
sentence for the time of buying and one present perfect for a change that reaches now.

**Levels 6-8, Activity 3 (body-paragraph analysis):** read the model body paragraphs of "The Pair I Replaced"
(printed in the packet under their working thesis line; an original model on the practice object, written for
Tomas). Each Level does a different thing with the same text.

- **Level 6:** name the purpose in one line, count the body paragraphs, say whether the working thesis is direct
  or indirect, and copy out each body paragraph's topic sentence.
- **Level 7 (a different shape):** name the single detail that carries the writer's attitude toward the old pair
  without stating it, and say in one line what a reader is meant to infer from it.
- **Level 8 (a different shape):** mark the two places where the writer's tone or level of abstraction changes,
  and say in one line what each change does for the reader.

**Answer note:** purpose, to describe the current pair against the pair it replaced so Tomas can decide whether
to buy. Three body paragraphs. The working thesis is direct (two points, padding and cable). Topic sentences:
"The first difference is the padding," "The second difference is the cable," "You asked whether they are worth
the money, Tomas." Level 7's attitude-carrying detail is "I still keep the old pair in the drawer": the reader
infers the writer is not as finished with the old pair as the verdict suggests. Level 8's two changes are the
move into direct address at the third paragraph, and the move from concrete parts to money and time inside it;
the first makes the reader a party to the decision, the second lifts the piece from a description to a
judgment.

**Level 5 bridge (receptive, 5 minutes inside Activity 2's block):** on the same model body paragraphs, find and
underline one body paragraph's topic sentence, then write one sentence saying what that paragraph is about.
Level 5's own output stays a single paragraph; this is exposure, not a task Level.

**Levels 6-8, Activity 4 (working thesis):** the packet prints two body-paragraph topics about a pair of
headphones: "the padding, which has outlasted two cheaper pairs" and "the cable, which stays out of the way."

- **Level 6:** write a direct working thesis naming both points, then say in one line which point would come
  first and why.
- **Level 7 (a different shape):** write the same thesis as an indirect one, then say in one line what it
  withholds that the bodies will supply.
- **Level 8 (a different shape):** write a working thesis that sets up two groups of bodies, one for a reader
  deciding whether to buy a pair and one for a reader who already owns one and is deciding whether to keep
  repairing it, without revealing that the second group will complicate the first. Then name, in one line, the
  complication you are holding back.

**Answer note:** Level 6, any direct thesis naming padding and cable, with a stated reason for the order (the
padding is the reason to buy; the cable is the reason to keep wearing them). Level 7, an indirect version such
as "This pair has lasted for reasons that are not the ones on the box"; it withholds which two features. Level
8, a thesis of the "whether you are deciding to buy or deciding to give up, the answer is the same, though not
for the same reason" shape; the withheld complication is that the same quality that makes them worth buying is
what keeps an owner paying for small repairs.

**Model body paragraphs, "The Pair I Replaced"** (three body paragraphs, about 185 words, printed in the packet
under their working thesis line for Activity 3 and the Level 5 bridge; a model for analysis, not a Mentor Essay;
the introduction and conclusion are modeled in Module 2):

> Working thesis: The pair I use now is better than the pair it replaced for two reasons: the padding has
> lasted, and the cable finally stays out of the way.
>
> The first difference is the padding. The old cups were covered in a soft foam that flattened inside a year, so
> by the end the metal band underneath pressed on the top of my head after about forty minutes. The pair I have
> now uses a firmer foam under a fabric that has not gone shiny, and I have worn them through a whole shift
> without noticing them. That is the test that matters on a long day.
>
> The second difference is the cable. The old one ran from the left cup and caught on every coat button between
> the door and the platform. This pair keeps its cable in a channel along the headband, and the plug sits flush
> against the cup. Nothing catches.
>
> You asked whether they are worth the money, Tomas. They cost about twice what the old pair did. I have had
> them three years, and the only part I have replaced is a rubber ring that costs almost nothing. I still keep
> the old pair in the drawer.

### Phase 1: Editing a paragraph on Focus A (20 min)

One paragraph about a pair of headphones, printed in the packet with **8 verified errors** (adjective order, a
relative pronoun, commas on defining and non-defining clauses, a heavy "of" possessive, a tense break, number
agreement, and one stack of five adjectives). **Levels 5-7:** find and correct the eight. **Level 8 (a heavier
tier, a different shape):** the same eight, then rewrite the two sentences marked with an asterisk so that each
abstract or flat noun is replaced by a concrete noun phrase carrying one postmodifier.

> My old pair had a black big cracked earcup, and I kept them anyway. The hinge who cracked first was the one on
> the left. The left cup which I dropped on the platform is looser than the right one. The pair, that I keep for
> the bus, is the older one.* The cable of the headphones has frayed near the plug. I use them every day on the
> train, and last month I used them for a call that lasts an hour. It is a good headphones for the price.* On
> top there is a worn heavy black plastic scratched headband that I have stopped noticing.

The eight errors: (1) "a black big cracked earcup" to "a big cracked black earcup" (opinion, size, age, color
order); (2) "The hinge who cracked" to "The hinge that cracked"; (3) "The left cup which I dropped on the
platform is looser" needs a comma at each end of the non-defining clause; (4) "The pair, that I keep for the
bus, is" is a defining clause and takes no commas; (5) "the cable of the headphones" to "the headphones'
cable"; (6) "a call that lasts an hour" to "lasted", the tense break inside a past sentence; (7) "It is a good
headphones" to "They are good headphones"; (8) "a worn heavy black plastic scratched headband" stacks five, so
two stay in front and the rest move behind, for instance "a heavy black headband, worn and scratched." Count
checked: 8.

**Answer note:** Level 8's two asterisked sentences have no single right answer. Accept any rewrite that trades
"the older one" and "good ... for the price" for a named part plus a postmodifier, for example "The pair I keep
for the bus is the one with the taped hinge" and "They are the only pair I have owned whose padding outlasted
the cable."

### Phase 2: Original sentences with Focus A / outlining (25 min)

**Level 5, Activity 5:** write six original sentences about your own headphones, one for each of these: a
prepositional phrase after the noun; a defining relative clause; a non-defining relative clause with its two
commas; two premodifying adjectives in the right order; a concrete noun phrase where you would normally write
comfort, quality, or design; one evaluative word (a bit, at least, worth it). All six stay in one tense. Then
underline the one sentence a reader could use to guess how you feel about the pair, with no feeling word in it.

**Level 6, Activity 6 (outlining):** write a **general outline** of the body paragraphs of an essay about your
own headphones for a reader who is deciding whether to buy a pair like them: the working thesis (direct, two
points), then one topic sentence for each body paragraph.

**Level 7, Activity 7 (a different shape):** write a **specific outline** for one body paragraph about your own
headphones: the topic sentence, then three supporting details in the order you would use them, with one detail
chosen to imply your attitude toward the pair rather than state it. Then write the indirect working thesis those
bodies would serve.

**Level 8, Activity 8 (a different shape):** write a **two-group outline**. Name your two readers (one deciding
whether to buy, one who already owns a pair and is deciding whether to keep repairing it). Give two topic
sentences for the first group and two for the second. Then write the working thesis that sets both groups up
without revealing the complication, and mark with an arrow the one detail in the second group that does the
complicating.

**Answer note:** all four are original work on the student's own object; there is no key. Circulation check:
Level 6's thesis names two points and each topic sentence states one point rather than a topic; Level 7's chosen
detail is a fact about the object, not an adjective about the writer; Level 8's two groups reach the same
judgment, and the marked detail belongs to the second group.

### Phase 3: Leveled Mentor Ladder walkthrough (30 min, teacher-presented, never printed)

Present the four models in Level order on the board or by reading aloud. Every model describes the writer's own
headphones for Tomas, who has asked whether the pair is worth buying and has said he cannot wear anything that
presses on his head for more than half an hour. The Level 6, 7, and 8 models are body paragraphs under a working
thesis line, the part this Module drafts; Module 2's second look adds their introductions and conclusions.
Students identify which model is their own Level's and the one directly above it. Nothing about the ladder
appears in the packet.

**Level 5 Mentor Text** (7 sentences, 139 words):

> These are the over-ear pair I bought three years ago, and they are the ones I reach for first. The cups are
> covered in a fabric that has stayed matt, and the padding underneath is firm rather than soft, so it has not
> flattened the way foam does. The headband is heavier than it looks, which is a bit much on a hot train but
> worth it in winter. The cable runs inside a channel along the band, so it does not catch on a coat button. A
> rubber ring on the left hinge has gone hard and grey; it costs almost nothing to replace, and I have not
> replaced it. I wear them on the bus, at my desk, and in the kitchen. I put them on before I decide whether I
> actually want to listen to anything.

Details in order: what they are, the cups and padding, the headband, the cable, the one worn part, where they
are worn, then the writer's own habit. Evaluative words: a bit, worth it, rather than. The implied attitude sits
in the last sentence, which lets the reader infer the pair is used to be left alone rather than to listen, with
no feeling word anywhere. One tense throughout, with a past sentence for the buying and a present perfect for
the ring.

**Level 6 Mentor Essay, this Module's part** (three body paragraphs under a direct working thesis, about 290
words; a sentence that says why the padding matters to Tomas; one register shift):

> Working thesis: For the way you use headphones, Tomas, this pair is worth the money for two reasons: the
> padding holds its shape, and the microphone is usable outdoors.
>
> The padding is the first reason. The cups use a firm foam under a woven fabric rather than the soft foam most
> pairs at this price use, and firm foam does not flatten. Three years in, the band still sits on my head rather
> than pressing into it. You said you cannot wear anything that presses after half an hour; I have worn these
> through a full shift and taken them off because I had finished, not because they hurt.
>
> The microphone is the second reason. It sits on the underside of the left cup rather than on the cable, so it
> is out of the wind, and it picks up less of the street than the cable microphones I have used. A cable
> microphone swings against a coat and turns every step into a thump at the other end; this one does not move at
> all, because it is fixed to something that is already fixed to my head. On a call from a bus stop, the person
> at the other end has never once asked me to move somewhere quieter, and I take three or four of those calls a
> week.
>
> Now, the part the reviews leave out. The rubber ring on the left hinge goes hard at about two years, and mine
> squeaks when I turn my head, which is audible to me and, I am told, to the person sitting next to me. It is a
> two-pound part and it takes a minute to change. I have simply not bothered. If a squeak on the left side would
> bother you the way a pressing headband would, that is the one thing to know before you buy.

Required feature: "You said you cannot wear anything that presses after half an hour; I have worn these through
a full shift" states why the padding matters to Tomas. "Now, the part the reviews leave out" is the register
shift addressing his concern about being sold something. Direct working thesis naming two points; each body
paragraph opens with its own topic sentence. No introduction and no conclusion: Module 2 adds them.

**Level 7 Mentor Essay, this Module's part** (three body paragraphs under an indirect working thesis, about 310
words; an implication built from selection and emphasis; visible self-revision):

> Working thesis: These have earned their place for reasons that have very little to do with sound.
>
> The first thing anyone notices is the seal. The cups are deep enough that my ears do not touch the inside, and
> the fabric ring meets the side of my head all the way round rather than resting on it. There is a moment when
> you put them on, before anything is playing, when the room drops by half. What this does to music is real but
> modest: a slightly fuller low end, a little less of the train, nothing a reviewer would build a paragraph
> around. What it does to the carriage is more dramatic. ~~It makes the journey quieter.~~ It makes the journey a
> different journey, one in which nobody leans over to ask whether the seat beside me is taken.
>
> The controls are on the left cup, and they are deliberately hard to work by accident. There is a single button
> for pause, and it needs a firm press with the flat of the thumb rather than the brush of a fingertip. Cheaper
> pairs put a touch panel on the outside of the cup, where a coat collar can find it. I have never once paused
> these by mistake while reaching for a ticket, which matters more than it sounds: the two seconds in which the
> carriage comes back are the two seconds in which somebody starts a conversation, and a conversation begun is
> very hard to end.
>
> Admittedly, the battery is not what it was. It runs about fourteen hours now rather than the twenty it managed
> new, and I charge them on Sunday nights, which used to be enough for the week. What's more, I have started
> charging them on Wednesdays as well, and I want to be careful here, because that is not a complaint about the
> battery. Fourteen hours is a great deal of listening. It is a statement about how many hours a week I have them
> on.

Required feature: the implication, that the pair is worn to be left alone rather than to listen, is never
stated; it is built from the selection and emphasis of detail (what the seal does to the carriage, why an
accidental pause matters, the second charge). Indirect working thesis. Visible self-revision: the struck
sentence "It makes the journey quieter" replaced in place by the sharper version, left legible. Sophisticated
cohesion: admittedly, what's more. No introduction and no conclusion: Module 2 adds them.

**Level 8 Mentor Essay, this Module's part** (four body paragraphs in two reader groups under a working thesis
that sets up both, about 460 words; two shifts of tone and abstraction; the second group complicates the first
framing and both reach the same judgment; visible self-revision):

> Working thesis: Whether you are deciding to buy this pair, Tomas, or deciding whether to keep the one you
> already own, Nadia, the answer comes out the same, though the reasons do not.
>
> Tomas, what you are really buying is restraint. Almost everything on this pair is one thing rather than three.
> One button, which pauses. One material on the cups, a woven fabric that neither shines nor sheds. One cable
> route, sunk into the band so that it has nowhere to catch. There is a confidence in a design that declines to
> give you options, and after three years I have come to read it as a manufacturer who knew exactly which forty
> minutes of anyone's day these would be worn, and built for those and nothing else.
>
> That restraint is also why they age as well as they do. An object that does a small number of things has a
> small number of things that can go wrong, and the failures, when they come, are legible: nothing on this pair
> has ever failed in a way I could not name within a minute of noticing it. ~~That is reassuring.~~ For someone
> buying a first serious pair, that legibility is worth more than the extra feature you are giving up, and it is
> the reason I would tell you to buy them.
>
> Nadia, here is the list.
>
> The rubber ring on the left hinge goes hard at about two years and squeaks. Two pounds, one minute. The
> battery drops from twenty hours to fourteen at roughly the same point, and then stays there, which is more
> than most batteries have the manners to do. The fabric on the right cup, the side I set them down on, wears
> through a good year before the left, and the replacement pads are the one part the manufacturer prices as
> though it knows you have nowhere else to go. The headband padding goes flat next, though slowly enough that
> you will not notice the day it happens. The charging port loosens last, at around four years, and it goes the
> way ports go: fine, fine, fine, then only at one angle. That one is not a two-pound part.
>
> So the legibility I sold Tomas on is real, and it cuts the other way for you. Every fault is nameable, which
> is another way of saying that you will always know exactly what is wrong and roughly what it will cost, and
> you will keep saying yes to small amounts right up until the port goes. I am on my third ring. I have not once
> sat down and decided to keep this pair, and I have not once decided to replace it either. Whatever that is, it
> has kept a working pair of headphones on my desk for three years, and I would keep repairing them, which is
> the same answer I gave Tomas.

Required feature: two body-paragraph groups, one per named reader, deliberately built differently. The Tomas
group is two long discursive paragraphs pitched high and abstract (restraint, confidence, legibility); the
Nadia group opens with a four-word address, then a paragraph of short declaratives with prices and years in it,
then one reflective paragraph. The two shifts are at "Nadia, here is the list" (tone and abstraction drop
together) and at "So the legibility I sold Tomas on is real" (back up into reflection). The complication: the
quality sold to Tomas as a reason to buy is shown to be the mechanism that keeps an owner paying for small
repairs, without contradicting the verdict, and both groups end on the same judgment. Visible self-revision:
"That is reassuring" struck and replaced. Indirect layer of implication: the writer never says they are unable
to make a decision about the pair; the last three sentences let the reader work it out.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** a "what changes at each Level" board built from student
observations after each model ("Level 5 puts the details in order and lets us guess at the end. Level 6 talks to
Tomas and says why the padding matters to him. Level 7 makes us work out what the pair is really for. Level 8
writes to two people at once and turns its own argument over. None of them has a beginning or an ending yet;
that is next Module's job"). The teacher writes the class's words, not a prepared list.
