Grammar: Saying It Exactly

Naming and showing

A naming phrase tells the reader the category. A showing phrase makes the reader see the thing.

Naming (tells)Showing (makes you see)
good headphonesheadphones that still sit flat after three years
the strap is oldthe headband has gone shiny where my thumb sits
nice sounda low end you feel more than hear
the case is damagedthe zip catches on the same tooth every time

Concrete and abstract

Comfort, quality, and design are abstract. The padding on the left cup is concrete. An abstract word is earned by putting a concrete one next to it: "the comfort is good" says nothing; "the padding is firm rather than soft, so it has not flattened" is the same claim with something to see.

Adjectives in front of the noun

Adjectives stack in a fixed order: opinion, size, age, shape, color, material. Two are usually enough. Three or more read as a list, and the fix is to move the extras behind the noun.

Stacked, reads as a listTwo in front, the rest behind
a worn heavy black plastic scratched headbanda heavy black headband, worn and scratched where my thumb sits
a cracked small grey rubber ringa small grey ring, cracked right through

Detail after the noun

TypeExample
prepositional phrasethe crack in the left hinge; the mesh inside the right cup
relative clause that says which one (no commas)the pair that I keep for the bus
relative clause that adds information (commas both sides)the left cup, which I dropped on the platform, is looser
reduced to a participlea ring, hardened by three winters

Use that or which for a thing, never who. A clause that says which one takes no commas; a clause that adds information takes a comma at each end.

A description stays in one tense unless the time changes, and an evaluative word (a bit, at least, worth it, rather than) carries attitude without a feeling word.

Inside a Body Paragraph, and the Working Thesis

One point per paragraph, one thesis for all of them

1. A body paragraph opens with a topic sentence that states its one point. Every sentence after it supports that point: an example, a detail, a reason, or a contrast. A sentence that supports a different point belongs in a different paragraph.

2. The working thesis is one line at the top of your outline and your draft that the body paragraphs serve. The beginning and the ending of the essay are written in the next unit, around the paragraphs you keep.

A direct working thesis names the points the paragraphs will make: "For the way you use headphones, this pair is worth the money for two reasons: the padding holds its shape, and the microphone is usable outdoors."

An indirect working thesis signals that points exist without naming them: "These have earned their place for reasons that have very little to do with sound."

A thesis for two readers sets up two groups of body paragraphs written for two different people, and does not give away that the second group will complicate what the first group establishes. Both groups reach the same judgment.

Model body paragraphs: The Pair I Replaced

Working thesis: The pair I use now is better than the pair it replaced for two reasons: the padding has lasted, and the cable finally stays out of the way.

The first difference is the padding. The old cups were covered in a soft foam that flattened inside a year, so by the end the metal band underneath pressed on the top of my head after about forty minutes. The pair I have now uses a firmer foam under a fabric that has not gone shiny, and I have worn them through a whole shift without noticing them. That is the test that matters on a long day.

The second difference is the cable. The old one ran from the left cup and caught on every coat button between the door and the platform. This pair keeps its cable in a channel along the headband, and the plug sits flush against the cup. Nothing catches.

You asked whether they are worth the money, Tomas. They cost about twice what the old pair did. I have had them three years, and the only part I have replaced is a rubber ring that costs almost nothing. I still keep the old pair in the drawer.

Two body-paragraph topics

1. The padding, which has outlasted two cheaper pairs.

2. The cable, which stays out of the way.
