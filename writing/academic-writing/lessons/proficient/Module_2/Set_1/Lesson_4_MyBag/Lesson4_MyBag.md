## What this lesson works from

Every student arrives with both parts of the piece, checked once together at Lesson 3 Day 2: at Level 5 a
descriptive paragraph and a narrative paragraph about their bag; at Levels 6 to 8 the body paragraphs of a
comparison essay with an introduction and a conclusion now written around them. "The bag" means whichever bag
that student circled; it is a subject, not a prop (Quality Standards §D8).

**No new grammar is taught, and Lesson 3 Day 2's merged checklist is not run again.** This lesson assembles the
piece, trades it, revises it for effect, and publishes it. The revision moves use only what the Pair already
taught:

- **Module 2 Focus A (Lesson 1):** the past simple line and the past perfect reach-back, with cue words.
- **Module 2 Focus B (Lesson 2):** fronting, with inversion only after a negative or restrictive adverbial; the
  it-cleft and the wh-cleft, one per paragraph at most; used to and would.
- **Essay Focus A (Lessons 1-2):** hook types, the connecting sentences, the thesis last, the four closing moves.
- **Module 1's tools:** naming a part exactly, the detail after the noun and its shortened form, sentence-length
  variation, the topic sentence, and the working thesis.

**This is the Pair's one Peer Editing exchange** (0.6). It did not happen at position 4, because the piece was
not finished then, and the pieces carry no marks from that position, so today's form has to name **both**
Modules' features for a reader to find.

**Draft Word Bank, Module 2** (reprinted; posted on the board and printed in both units of the packet):

- Time and sequence: that winter, the week after, by then, until then, ever since, the first month
- Reaching back: already, up to that point, the year before, earlier that autumn, before that, since the spring
- What happened: split, snapped, gave way, soaked through, held, survived
- How it turned out: worth it, a nuisance, no trouble, the last straw, fine in the end, still going
- Parts to name exactly: the strap, the base, the zip, the lining, the buckle, the front pocket

**Output targets for the finished piece (Section 0.2), checked by the teacher on the final copy.** Level 5, two
paragraphs, the first 90 to 150 words and the second the same. Level 6, a five-paragraph essay of roughly 300 to
450 words. Level 7, roughly 450 to 650 words across five paragraphs. Level 8, roughly 650 to 900 words across
six or seven, in two reader groups. The ranges are the teacher's; students count nothing today.

## DAY 1: ASSEMBLE, TRADE AND CHECK, REVISE FOR EFFECT (75 MIN)

```
0              25                            60         75  (minutes)
|---Phase 1-----|-----------Phase 2-----------|-Phase 3--|
| Assemble the  | Revise for effect with       | Revision  |
| whole; Peer   | Module 2's tools, before/    | share     |
| Editing       | after evidence               |           |
```

### Phase 1: Assemble the whole piece, then Peer Editing (25 min)

**Assemble (7 min).** Write a title on the packet's title line: the bag's name as you would give it to someone
else ("The Bag I Have Carried for Three Years," "My Grey Backpack"). Then put the parts in order underneath it
and number them in the margin.

- **Level 5:** 1, the description. 2, the story.
- **Levels 6 to 8:** 1, the introduction. 2, the body paragraphs, in the order they already stand. 3, the
  conclusion. Cross out the working thesis line at the top of the bodies: the introduction now carries that
  sentence, and it should appear once.

This is the first time the piece exists as a whole; nothing is copied yet.

**Peer Editing exchange (18 min), the Pair's one exchange (Section 0.6),** covering both Modules' features.
Partners trade whole pieces and read them once through. Then the reader fills the printed form on the writer's
page:

1. In the first part, underline the detail that tells you most about the bag, and circle the part of the bag it
   names.
2. If you are reading a **two-paragraph piece**: number the events of the second paragraph in the order they
   happened, and box the sentence that says why it mattered. If you are reading an **essay**: box the hook,
   underline the thesis, and box the closing move.
3. If you are reading a **Level 7 or Level 8 piece**: write what you think the writer is not saying outright.
   For a Level 8 piece, also write the answer each of the two groups reaches, and whether the ending settles
   between them.
4. One specific compliment: copy out one sentence you would keep exactly as it is.
5. One specific suggestion: one thing you want to know more about, or one place where you could not tell what
   happened first, or what the ending was doing.

The pieces go back. This is Peer Editing, not a reader's request: it has the form, the compliment, and the
suggestion, and it runs once in the Pair, here.

**Teacher-facing:** while pairs read, note two or three suggestions to use in Phase 3. Watch for a reader who
cannot find the thesis; that is usually a writer whose introduction previews the points instead of narrowing to
them, and it is worth catching now rather than in the final copy.

### Phase 2: Revise for effect with Module 2's tools (35 min)

Every change goes in the before-and-after table on the page, so the revision is visible rather than claimed
(0.4a). Cross out on the piece, do not erase. At Levels 7 and 8 the completed table is required evidence.

| What I first wrote | What I changed it to | What the change did |
|---|---|---|

**Level 5, Task A: three moves, on the whole piece.**

1. **Front or cleft.** Take one sentence in the second paragraph and rewrite it either with its adverbial at the
   front or as a cleft, so the reader leans on the part you want. Check the inversion rule if you fronted a
   negative or restrictive adverbial.
2. **Upgrade.** Replace one plain word with a more exact one from the Draft Word Bank, or with the name of the
   part it is really about.
3. **Answer the reader.** Make the change your partner's suggestion asked for.

**Level 6, Task B: three moves, on the whole piece.**

1. **Front or cleft.** Choose a sentence in the introduction or the conclusion, so the change falls where the
   reader arrives or leaves.
2. **Upgrade.** Replace one plain word in your closing move, so the suggestion names something the reader can
   actually do.
3. **Answer the reader.** Make the change your partner's suggestion asked for. If it asks for something that
   belongs in a body paragraph, put it there and leave the conclusion alone.

**Level 7, Task C: four moves.**

1. **Front or cleft**, in the introduction, so the hook's detail arrives in the position you want it in.
2. **Upgrade** one plain word, choosing one that carries the implication rather than one that only sounds
   better.
3. **Answer the reader**, and check that your answer does not accidentally state the thing you are implying.
4. **Reorder for pacing.** Move one sentence so that what the reader works out arrives later than it did. In the
   table, say what they now work out, and when.

**Level 8, Task D: five moves.**

1. **Front or cleft**, in the Marcus group, so the two groups' sentence shapes pull further apart.
2. **Upgrade** one plain word in the introduction, so the thing your hook names belongs more clearly to both
   readers.
3. **Answer the reader.**
4. **Reorder for pacing.** Move one sentence so the complication lands later than it did.
5. **Open the introduction wider.** Rewrite one connecting sentence so that both readers' questions stay open
   through it. Write that sentence before and after in the table, and say in the third column which reader was
   being shut out.

**Answer note:** no key; every row is on the student's own piece. Circulation priorities, in order: a "what the
change did" column that repeats the change instead of naming its effect; a change that only corrects a comma or
a spelling, which is Lesson 3's business and not a move, so send the student back to the list; a Level 7 reorder
that pushed the implication past the end of the essay; a Level 8 move 5 that opened the introduction by naming
both readers outright, which is the flat solution the hook was built to avoid.

### Phase 3: Revision share (15 min)

Two or three volunteers read one row of their table aloud: the before, the after, and nothing else. The class
names what the change did. **Board-dependent moment:** build a before-and-after board from what is read out,
with the effect named in the class's own words ("now you get to the zip last," "it stops telling us and lets us
work it out"). Not a prepared list.

## DAY 2: FINAL COPY, PUBLISH, CLOSING TRANSFER CHECK (75 MIN)

```
0                    25                    50                   75  (minutes)
|-------Phase 1-------|-------Phase 2-------|-------Phase 3------|
| Final copy of the   | Publish / share     | Closing Transfer   |
| whole piece         | (read two pieces)   | Check + share      |
```

### Phase 1: Final copy of the whole piece (25 min)

Write a clean copy of the whole piece with your revisions in place, under your title, in the order you numbered
yesterday. Capitals and full stops where they belong.

There is no proofreading list and nothing to count: the piece was checked once, together, at Lesson 3 Day 2, and
revised for effect yesterday.

### Phase 2: Publish / share (25 min)

The piece is genuinely finished.

**Pass and read.** Each final copy is passed to a neighbor, read, and passed on again, so that every student
reads two pieces that are not their partner's, from their seat. Nothing goes on a wall and nobody moves.

On a card, for each piece you read, one line:

- **Level 5 reading anything:** the one detail that made you see the bag.
- **Level 6:** the one detail that made you see the bag, and what the ending asked you to do.
- **Level 7:** the one detail that made you see the bag, and one thing the writer did not say outright.
- **Level 8:** the one detail that made you see the bag, and whether the piece ended up recommending one thing
  or two.

Then three or four writers are cold-called to read their favorite sentence aloud from their seats. Nothing is
collected for grading; the copies go home.

### Phase 3: Closing Transfer Check (25 min)

Two short checks (Section 0.8), both on one card, both demonstrations. First the two skills of the Pair together
on a new object, then Module 2's own skill on a new prompt. Neither the object nor the prompt appears anywhere
else in either Set.

**Check 1, both skills together: your own wallet or card holder.** In pairs, each student writes on one side of
a card:

- **Level 5:** two sentences describing it, one of which lets your partner guess how you feel about carrying it
  with no feeling word, then two sentences telling one thing that happened with it, in order.
- **Level 6:** one sentence describing it for someone about to buy one, then two sentences telling one thing
  that happened with it, with one clear shift from telling what happened to saying why it mattered.
- **Level 7:** the same three sentences, with the outcome held back to the last one and one earlier detail that
  hints at why you kept it.
- **Level 8:** the same three sentences, narrated so that a careful reader can tell the account is quietly on
  your side, with the distance you stand at changing once.

Partners swap cards and say what they got: the guess, the reason it mattered, the withheld outcome, or the side
the account is on.

**Check 2, Module 2's own skill on a new prompt: the last time you were late to something that mattered.** On
the other side of the same card:

- **Level 5:** three to five sentences narrating it and saying why it mattered, with one detail that lets a
  partner guess how you felt, and no feeling word anywhere.
- **Level 6:** the same, plus one clear shift from narrating to reflecting, written for a reader you know is
  likely to think it was your own fault.
- **Level 7:** the same, with the outcome withheld until the final sentence and one earlier, unexplained detail
  that hints at your real motive.
- **Level 8:** the same, narrated so that a careful reader can tell the account quietly favors you (a detail
  left out, your own reaction described more sympathetically than anyone else's), with the pacing changing at
  least twice, and the voice never breaking.

**Transfer share (inside the 25 min):** the teacher cold-calls two or three pairs to read a card aloud, one side
each, from their seats. Demonstration only; nothing is collected.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** capture two or three of the read-aloud instances on the board
under yesterday's before-and-after board, one per Level where possible, so the class sees the same two skills on
a wallet and a late morning that it just saw on a bag.

**Answer note:** neither check has a key. What the teacher is watching for, one item per Level: a Level 5 card
with a feeling word in it; a Level 6 card whose shift into reflection is a feeling word rather than a reason; a
Level 7 card where the partner reads the motive straight off the page because it was written down; a Level 8
card that says outright that the account is one-sided, which breaks the voice the row asks to be sustained.

**The Pair is finished.** Both Modules' Sets are complete, the piece is published, and both objectives have been
demonstrated on new material.
