Grammar: Putting the Weight Where You Want It

Moving a part to the front

Every structure here says the same thing as its plain version. What changes is which part the reader leans on.

PlainFronted
I noticed the fraying only later.Only later did I notice the fraying.
The lining did not split until the second winter.Not until the second winter did the lining split.
I have never carried anything heavier in it since.Never since have I carried anything heavier in it.
The strap gave way at the station, of all places.At the station, of all places, the strap gave way.

The inversion rule

A negative or restrictive word at the front (only, never, not until, rarely, hardly, no sooner) pulls the verb in front of the subject: "Only later did I notice." A plain time or place phrase at the front does not, and just takes a comma: "At the station, of all places, the strap gave way." Getting this backwards is the mistake to hunt.

Two ways to spotlight one part

StructureFrameWhat it does
it-cleftIt was X that Y.names the one part that mattered and pushes the rest into the background
wh-cleftWhat Y was X.holds the point back to the end, so the reader arrives at it

The zip went first. → It was the zip that went first.

I had not expected the weight. → What I had not expected was the weight.

Use one, not three. Two of these in a row cancel each other out: if everything is in the spotlight, nothing is. One fronted part or one cleft per paragraph is usually the whole budget.

Used to and would

A repeated actionA state
used toI used to carry it everywhere.It used to be blue.
wouldI would carry it everywhere.not "It would be blue."
The Connecting Sentences and the Closing Move

What a beginning and an ending are made of

1. The connecting sentences are the two or three between the hook and the thesis. Their job is to narrow: the hook is one moment, the thesis is the whole piece, and these carry a reader from one to the other. They do not repeat the hook, and they do not preview the body paragraphs.

2. The thesis goes last in the introduction, in the place the reader is already leaning toward.

3. The closing move is what comes after the conclusion has said the thesis again in new words.

Closing moveWhat it does
Suggestiontells the reader one thing to do with what they have read
Predictionsays what will still be true later
Questionhands the reader something the piece has not settled
Opinionstates a judgment the piece has earned but not yet said outright

4. A conclusion adds no new information. If a fact appears there for the first time, it belonged in a body paragraph.

5. At ★★★ and ★★★★, a question or an opinion usually serves your kind of thesis better than a suggestion does. At ★★★★ the ending has to leave the complication standing.

A paragraph about a bag
