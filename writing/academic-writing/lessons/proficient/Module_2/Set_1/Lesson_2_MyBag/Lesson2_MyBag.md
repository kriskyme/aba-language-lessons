### Phase 1: Focus B mini-lesson, rule and examples (20 min)

**Grammar box: Putting the weight where you want it.** (One box, whole class.)

Focus A gave you two lines to run a story on. Focus B decides which part of a sentence the reader leans on.
Every structure here says the same thing as its plain version; what changes is where the weight falls.

Rule 1, fronting. Move an adverbial to the front of the sentence.

| Plain | Fronted |
|---|---|
| I noticed the fraying only later. | Only later did I notice the fraying. |
| The lining did not split until the second winter. | Not until the second winter did the lining split. |
| I have never carried anything heavier in it since. | Never since have I carried anything heavier in it. |
| The strap gave way at the station, of all places. | At the station, of all places, the strap gave way. |

Rule 2, the inversion rule. A **negative or restrictive** adverbial at the front (only, never, not until, rarely,
hardly, no sooner) pulls the auxiliary in front of the subject: "Only later **did I** notice." A plain time or
place phrase at the front does not: "At the station, of all places, the strap gave way." Getting this backwards
is the error to hunt.

Rule 3, the it-cleft: **It was X that Y.** It names the one element that mattered and pushes everything else
into the background.

> The zip went first. → **It was the zip that went first.**

Rule 4, the wh-cleft: **What Y was X.** It holds the point back to the last position in the sentence, so the
reader arrives at it rather than starting there.

> I had not expected the weight. → **What I had not expected was the weight.**

Rule 5, use one, not three. Two clefts in a row cancel each other out: if everything is in the spotlight,
nothing is. One fronted adverbial or one cleft per paragraph is usually the whole budget.

**Contrast pair: used to / would.** Both describe a repeated past. **Used to** covers repeated actions *and*
states. **Would** covers repeated actions only, and never a state.

| | Repeated action | State |
|---|---|---|
| used to | I used to carry it everywhere. | It used to be blue. |
| would | I would carry it everywhere. | not "It would be blue." |

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** take one plain sentence offered by the class about something
that happened in this room this term, and write its fronted version and its cleft version underneath it. Ask
which of the three the class would use, and for what. Write the class's three, not a prepared set.

**Essay Focus A continued, Levels 6-8 (folded into the same 20 minutes; Level 5 hears it and is not tested on
it):**

**Essay-structure box: the connecting sentences, and the closing move.**

1. **Connecting sentences** are the two or three between the hook and the thesis. Their job is to narrow: the
   hook is one moment, the thesis is the whole essay, and these sentences carry the reader from one to the
   other. They do not restate the hook, and they do not preview the body paragraphs.
2. **The thesis goes last** in the introduction, in the position the reader is already leaning toward.
3. **The closing move** is the one sentence or two after the conclusion has restated the thesis in new words.
   Four kinds:

| Closing move | What it does |
|---|---|
| Suggestion | tells the reader one thing to do with what they have read |
| Prediction | says what will still be true later |
| Question | hands the reader something the essay has not settled |
| Opinion | states a judgment the essay has earned but not yet said outright |

4. **A conclusion introduces no new information.** If a fact appears there for the first time, it belonged in a
   body paragraph.
5. **At Levels 7 and 8** the closing move should not flatten what the bodies built: a question or an opinion
   usually serves an indirect thesis better than a suggestion does. At Level 8 it must leave the complication
   standing.

### Phase 2: Controlled practice B (30 min)

Two tracks run at once; the teacher circulates between them.

**Level 5, Activity 1 (identify and rebuild, 8 items):** mark each sentence **F** if it fronts an adverbial, **C**
if it is a cleft, and **P** if it is plain. Then rewrite the two you marked P, one as a cleft and one with a
fronted adverbial.

1. Only later did I notice the strap was fraying.
2. It was the zip that went first.
3. I carried it every day that winter.
4. What I had not expected was how heavy it got.
5. Not until the second winter did the lining split.
6. At the station, of all places, the strap gave way.
7. The base had gone grey by then.
8. It was the front pocket that I used most.

**Answer note:** F: 1, 5, 6. C: 2, 4, 8. P: 3, 7. Rebuilds are open; "It was that winter that I carried it every
day" and "By then the base had gone grey" are the obvious pair. Item 6 is the trap: it is fronted but takes no
inversion, because "at the station" is a plain place phrase.

**Level 5, Activity 2 (transform, 8 items):** rewrite each sentence using the structure named in brackets. Keep
the meaning identical.

1. I only noticed the fraying in the spring. [front it]
2. The zip went first. [it-cleft]
3. I did not use it for a laptop until that winter. [front it]
4. I had not expected the weight. [wh-cleft]
5. The strap gave way at the station. [front the place phrase]
6. I used the front pocket most. [it-cleft]
7. I have never carried anything heavier in it since. [front it]
8. The lining mattered more than the strap in the end. [wh-cleft]

**Answer note:** 1 "Only in the spring did I notice the fraying." 2 "It was the zip that went first." 3 "Not
until that winter did I use it for a laptop." 4 "What I had not expected was the weight." 5 "At the station,
the strap gave way" (no inversion, comma after the phrase). 6 "It was the front pocket that I used most." 7
"Never since have I carried anything heavier in it." 8 "What mattered more than the strap in the end was the
lining." Items 1, 3, and 7 need inversion; item 5 must not have it, and is the one to check first.

**Levels 6-8, Activity 3 (hook-writing practice):** the packet prints an introduction with its connecting
information and thesis in place but **no hook**, written for the same model essay Lesson 1 analyzed.

- **Level 6:** write two candidate hooks, one a scenario and one an anecdote. Then say in one line which you
  would keep and why.
- **Level 7 (a different shape):** write one quote hook and one statistic hook. Then say in one line what each
  makes a reader expect that the other does not.
- **Level 8 (a different shape):** write one withheld-detail hook that opens two readers' questions at the same
  time. Then say in one line what it withholds, and what a reader is likely to assume in the meantime.

**Introduction printed without its hook, for Activity 3:**

> ___________________________________________
>
> I had been putting off a replacement since the spring, so the decision got made for me that evening. Three
> years later I can say exactly what the money bought, which is what you asked me. For the way you use
> headphones, Tomas, this pair is worth the money for two reasons: the padding holds its shape, and the
> microphone is usable outdoors.

**Answer note:** all three are open. Circulation check: a hook that summarizes the essay is not a hook; a hook
longer than two sentences is a body paragraph; at Level 7 a "statistic" invented on the spot is fine for the
drill and would need a real source in a real essay, which is worth saying aloud once. Level 8's strongest
answers name a thing rather than a scene, since a thing can belong to two readers at once.

**Levels 6-8, Activity 4 (conclusion practice):** the packet prints a thesis and the three topic sentences of a
set of body paragraphs, with **no conclusion**.

- **Level 6:** restate the thesis in new words, then add a closing move that is a suggestion.
- **Level 7 (a different shape):** restate the thesis in new words, then close on a question. Say in one line
  what your question leaves open that the bodies did not settle.
- **Level 8 (a different shape):** restate the thesis so that it covers both reader groups at once, then close
  without settling which reading of the object is the right one. Say in one line what you deliberately did not
  resolve.

**Thesis and topic sentences printed for Activity 4:**

> **Thesis:** For the way you use headphones, Tomas, this pair is worth the money for two reasons: the padding
> holds its shape, and the microphone is usable outdoors.
>
> 1. The padding is the first reason.
> 2. The microphone is the second reason.
> 3. Now, the part the reviews leave out.

**Answer note:** open. Circulation check: a conclusion that names a fourth reason has added new information and
has to go back; a Level 7 question that could be answered yes or no is usually not doing the work; a Level 8
restatement that picks one reader has flattened the essay.

### Phase 3: Contrast-pair drill and mixed editing (25 min)

**Whole class, Activity 5 (used to / would, 8 items):** choose the correct form, and mark **S** beside the two
items where only one form is possible because the sentence describes a state.

1. It (used to / would) be a plain canvas bag before I had it dyed.
2. Every Friday I (used to / would) empty it out completely.
3. The front pocket (used to / would) hold a paperback and nothing else.
4. In that first year I (used to / would) carry it to the gym as well.
5. The strap (used to / would) be shorter than it is now.
6. On the walk home I (used to / would) swap it from shoulder to shoulder.
7. There (used to / would) be a small tear in the lining, which I never fixed.
8. Most mornings I (used to / would) check the zip twice before leaving.

**Answer note:** states, where only "used to" works: 1, 5, 7, and arguably 3. Mark S on 1, 5, and 7; accept 3
marked either way and use it to ask whether "hold" is an action here or a description of the pocket. Repeated
actions, where both forms work: 2, 4, 6, 8.

**All Levels, Activity 6 (mixed editing paragraph):** one paragraph about a bag with **7 verified errors**,
mixing Lesson 1's Focus A with today's Focus B. **Levels 5-7:** find and correct the seven. **Level 8 (a heavier
tier, a different shape):** the same seven, then rewrite the two sentences marked with an asterisk so that only
one of them keeps its cleft and the other carries its weight by fronting instead.

> It used to be a plain canvas bag, and I would carry it everywhere that first year. Only later I noticed the
> base had started to go. It was the zip who went first, though.* Not until the second winter the lining split.
> What I had not expected were how heavy it got with a laptop in it.* Before that, I never carried anything
> heavier than a book. At the station of all places, the strap gave way. It would be blue once, before the sun
> got to it.

The seven errors: (1) "Only later I noticed" to "Only later did I notice", the missing inversion after a
restrictive adverbial; (2) "It was the zip who went first" to "that went first", since a zip is a thing; (3)
"Not until the second winter the lining split" to "did the lining split"; (4) "What I had not expected were" to
"was", the wh-cleft takes a singular verb here; (5) "Before that, I never carried" to "I had never carried", the
reach-back from Focus A; (6) "At the station of all places," needs a comma at each end of the aside: "At the
station, of all places, the strap gave way"; (7) "It would be blue once" to "It used to be blue once", since
blue is a state. Count checked: 7, in 8 sentences.

**Answer note:** Level 8's rewrite is open. The likeliest good version keeps the it-cleft on the zip, because
that sentence names one element, and refronts the other as "Never had I expected how heavy it would get with a
laptop in it." Accept any version in which exactly one cleft survives.

### Phase 1: Required-feature warm-up on the kept part (25 min)

Each Level writes only its own required feature, once, worked against the part it already has. Students take the
kept part from their folder and re-read it before starting.

**Level 5, Activity 7:** re-read your own paragraph and choose one sentence in it that names a part of the bag.
Write two new sentences. The first tells one thing that happened to that same part, using the past perfect to
reach back to something earlier. The second is a detail that would let a partner guess how you feel about the
bag, with no feeling word in it. Read only the second to your partner; your partner says what they guessed.

**Level 6, Activity 8 (a different shape):** re-read your own body paragraphs and find the sentence where you
turn and address Priya. Write the one sentence your introduction will use to tell her what she is about to read,
without repeating that sentence and without previewing your two points.

**Level 7, Activity 9 (a different shape):** re-read your own body paragraphs and find the detail you are relying
on to carry your implication. Write one sentence that could open the essay and point at that detail without
explaining it. Then write one line saying what a reader would assume about it on a first pass.

**Level 8, Activity 10 (a different shape):** re-read both of your groups. Write two versions of an opening
sentence: one that names both readers' questions outright, and one that names neither but leaves both feeling
addressed. Then write one line saying which you would use and what the other would cost you.

**Answer note:** no key; all four are original work on the student's own kept part. Circulation check: Level 5's
reach-back is genuinely earlier than the event; Level 6's sentence does not list the two points, which is the
commonest slip; Level 7's detail is a fact from the bodies rather than a new one; Level 8's second version does
not simply drop both readers.

### Phase 2: Planning Module 2's part against the kept part (30 min)

Not prewriting on a new object: every student already has their part of the piece, and the plan here is for the
part Module 2 adds to it, on the same bag. Students keep the kept part in front of them while planning, so the
new part fits what is already written. Nothing from Lesson 1's packet is needed (Quality Standards §D9). This
planning is rehearsal: Lesson 3 builds its own quick plan inside its own packet, so no task here says "for your
draft" or "next time."

**What Module 2's part is, by Level.** At Level 5, a second paragraph telling one real thing that happened with
the bag, in order. At Levels 6 to 8, the introduction and the conclusion of the essay whose bodies you already
have. Nobody rewrites the part they already have.

**Level 5 only, two choices printed to circle:** **the day I got it** / **one day I carried it that mattered.**

- **Level 5, Activity 11:** circle one of the two choices. List four or five events in the order they happened,
  one line each. Then write "Why it mattered: ___." Circle the one detail on your list that will let a reader
  guess how you feel, and write "What I want the reader to guess: ___." Test that last line on a partner by
  reading them only the circled detail.
- **Level 6, Activity 12 (a different shape):** name the hook type you will use and write its first line. Write
  your two connecting sentences in note form. Copy your working thesis line out unchanged; it becomes the last
  sentence of your introduction. Then name your conclusion's closing move.
- **Level 7, Activity 13 (a different shape):** name your hook type, which must be a quote, a statistic, or an
  anecdote, and write its first line. Write one line saying what the hook withholds. Copy your indirect working
  thesis out unchanged. Then name your closing move and write one line saying what it will leave open.
- **Level 8, Activity 14 (a different shape):** name your hook type and write the sentence that opens both
  readers' questions at once. Write in note form the connecting sentences that keep both open. Copy your working
  thesis out unchanged. Then write one line naming what your conclusion will refuse to settle.

**Answer note:** no key. Circulation check: the bag, not the headphones, is the subject of every line; nobody is
rewriting the kept part; a Level 6 plan whose connecting sentences preview the two points needs one more minute;
a Level 8 plan whose conclusion "answers both readers" has flattened the thing Module 1 built.

### Phase 3: Plan share and board synthesis (20 min)

Two or three students from each Level read one line from their plan: a hook's first line, a closing move, or an
event from a list. **Board-dependent moment:** build a two-column class board from what is read out, headed **How
we get a reader in** and **How we let them out**, grouped by what the line does rather than by Level, so that a
Level 5 first event and a Level 8 hook can sit in the same column. The board ends up holding more openings and
endings than any single handout prints. The teacher writes what is said, not a prepared list. The board is this
lesson's; Lesson 3 does not depend on it.
