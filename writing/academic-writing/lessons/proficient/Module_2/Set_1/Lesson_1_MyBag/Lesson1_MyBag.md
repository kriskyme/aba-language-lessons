## What this lesson works from

Every student arrives with Module 1's kept part, unmarked, in their folder: at Level 5 one extended descriptive
paragraph about their bag, now against when they got it; at Levels 6 to 8 the body paragraphs of a comparison
essay under a working thesis line, in two reader groups at Level 8. Below, **"the bag"** means whichever bag
that student circled from the printed list. The bag is a subject, not a prop (Quality Standards §D8): nothing is
brought or taken out, and every detail is written from what the student already knows of it. The headphones,
Module 1's practice object, appear in this lesson only as the printed model essay and the Mentor Ladder, both of
which are texts the class reads rather than objects anyone owns; keeping the models on a different object is
what stops them leaking content into the students' own pieces.

**How Module 2's verb lands, which is different at Level 5 and at Levels 6 to 8, and is meant to be.** Module 2
is Narrating, but the shared piece keeps Module 1's genre (Conventions §C: the piece is built in parts, and
neither Module drafts a whole one).

- **Level 5 genuinely narrates in the shared piece.** Its Module 2 part is a **second paragraph in Module 2's own
  form**: one real thing that happened with the bag, told in order, with why it mattered and one detail that
  implies a feeling. The finished piece is two paragraphs, a description and a story.
- **Levels 6 to 8 do not.** Their Module 2 part is the **introduction and conclusion of Module 1's comparison
  essay**. The only narrating in it is the hook's form. This is exactly why Lesson 4 of this Set carries a
  **separate task on Module 2's own verb** (0.8): that card, not the shared piece, is what gives Module 2's
  objective genuine coverage at the essay Levels.
- **Module 2's Focus A earns its place at every Level even so.** An anecdote hook has to reach behind its own
  opening sentence, and a closing move often reaches back the same way. Both need the past perfect.

**This lesson drafts nothing.** It re-engages the piece, teaches Module 2's grammar focus, teaches the essay's
missing two parts to Levels 6 to 8, and shows on the ladder what Module 2 will add. Module 1's tools stay
available and are not re-taught: noun-phrase precision (naming against showing, concrete against abstract,
premodification and its order, postmodification by prepositional phrase and relative clause), reduced clauses
and their commas, sentence-length variation, the topic sentence, and the working thesis, direct at Level 6 and
indirect at Levels 7 and 8.

## Draft Word Bank, Module 2 (teacher-provided; posted on the board and printed in every Module 2 packet)

The Set's five groups refilled for the part Module 2 adds. Thirty words, more than any one task uses.

- Time and sequence: that winter, the week after, by then, until then, ever since, the first month
- Reaching back: already, up to that point, the year before, earlier that autumn, before that, since the spring
- What happened: split, snapped, gave way, soaked through, held, survived
- How it turned out: worth it, a nuisance, no trouble, the last straw, fine in the end, still going
- Parts to name exactly: the strap, the base, the zip, the lining, the buckle, the front pocket

## Grammar focuses

**Focus A (this lesson's centerpiece, whole class): narrative tense management by anteriority.** Past perfect for
an event that happened before the event being narrated, against past simple for the narrated line itself, so a
narrator can reach backwards without breaking the sequence.

**Essay Focus A, the parallel track for Levels 6 to 8 (folded into the same sessions):** the five-paragraph
shape, where the introduction and conclusion sit around the bodies already written, and hook types in Module 2's
narrative form.

**Level 5 within Module 2:** the same focus at paragraph scale, with the required feature staying one detail that
implies a feeling without naming it.

### Phase 1: Re-engagement and Skill Spotlight (15 min)

**Re-engagement (8 min), not a hook.** Students take Module 1's kept part out of their folder and re-read it
silently, once. Then the teacher gives a short recap from the board, in these four lines, not a student note:

1. **What this is.** A piece about your own bag, written for Priya, who is about to spend a year commuting with
   a laptop and asked whether a bag like it lasts. At Level 8 it is also written for Marcus, who already carries
   one and is deciding whether to keep repairing it.
2. **What is already written.** Your description of the bag, now against when you got it. That part stays
   exactly as it is.
3. **What the last four lessons added.** Naming a part exactly instead of naming a category; putting the detail
   after the noun and then shortening it; letting a short sentence land; and, at Levels 6 to 8, a topic sentence
   for every paragraph under a working thesis.
4. **What we add now.** At Level 5, a second paragraph telling one thing that happened with the bag. At Levels 6
   to 8, the beginning and the ending of the essay: the part that gets a reader into it, and the part that lets
   them out.

**Skill Spotlight (7 min):** "This week we're practicing telling what happened with something of our own, in
order, so that the order itself carries what mattered." One sentence, the same for every Level (Quality
Standards §D1). The one-line reminder of Module 1's skill: "You already know how to choose which details to
give and how much weight to give them; that skill stays in the part you have already written." Each Level's own
required feature is introduced inside its own tasks, not here. Say that the Closing Transfer Check at the end of
Lesson 4 will ask everyone to do both skills on something new.

### Phase 2: Focus A mini-lesson, rule and examples (30 min)

**Grammar box: Reaching back without losing the thread.** (One box, whole class.)

Rule 1, the two lines a narrative runs on. The events you are telling, in the order they happened, are **past
simple**. An event that happened *before* the one you are telling is **past perfect**: had plus the past
participle.

| The narrated line (past simple) | Reaching back (past perfect) |
|---|---|
| The strap gave way on the platform. | It had frayed quietly for months. |
| I bought the bag in my first week. | I had carried a plastic one until then. |
| The zip stuck for the last time in November. | By then I had already had it repaired twice. |
| I looked inside that evening. | Someone had left the receipt in the front pocket. |

Rule 2, you only need the past perfect once. Once the reach-back is established, the sentences inside it can go
back to past simple without confusing anyone.

> I had bought it in a hurry. The shop was closing, I had twenty minutes, and it was the only one in my size.

Rule 3, cue words that signal a reach-back and make the tense do less work on its own: already, by then, until
then, up to that point, before that, the year before, earlier that autumn, since the spring.

Rule 4, the reverse error is the common one at this Band. A past perfect used for the narrated line itself
stalls the story: "Ever since then, I had checked the strap every few months" should be "I checked." Reach back
only for what is genuinely earlier.

**Already known and not taught here:** past continuous marks what was going on around an event ("I was running
for the bus when the strap went"). One line, named so nobody thinks it has been forgotten.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** on a fresh prompt (something that happened in this building
this week), build one two-sentence pair with the class in which the second sentence reaches back. Write the
class's version, not a prepared one; underline the past perfect and box the cue word if they used one.

**Essay Focus A, Levels 6-8 (folded into the same 30 minutes; Level 5 hears it and is not tested on it):**

**Essay-structure box: the two parts you have not written yet.**

1. **The shape.** An essay is an introduction, the body paragraphs, and a conclusion. You already have the
   bodies. The working thesis line at the top of them is not an introduction; it is a note to yourself that the
   introduction will absorb.
2. **The introduction** runs hook, then connecting information, then the thesis **last**. The connecting
   sentences are the two or three that carry a reader from the hook down to the thesis, narrowing as they go.
3. **The conclusion** restates the thesis in new words and then makes one closing move: a suggestion, a
   prediction, a question, or an opinion. It introduces no new information.
4. **Hook types, in Module 2's narrative form:**

| Hook type | What it does | Available at |
|---|---|---|
| Scenario | drops the reader into a single moment, in progress | 6, 7, 8 |
| Anecdote | tells one short true incident and stops | 6, 7, 8 |
| Withheld detail | names something without explaining it yet | 6, 7, 8 |
| Quote | opens on someone's words | 7, 8 |
| Statistic | opens on a number that reframes the subject | 7, 8 |
| Plain question | asks the reader something | 6 only |

5. **At Level 8 the introduction has one more job:** it sets up both reader groups and does not reveal that the
   second group will complicate the first. The conclusion must not resolve that complication flatly.

### Phase 3: Controlled practice A, part 1 (30 min)

Two tracks run at once; the teacher circulates between them.

**Level 5, Activity 1 (order and check, 8 items):** eight sentences, each holding two events. Write **1** above
the verb for the event that happened first and **2** above the later one. Then mark **X** beside any sentence
whose verb forms do not show that order, and write the correction.

1. The strap had frayed for months before it finally gave way.
2. I bought the bag in September; I had carried a plastic one until then.
3. By the time I noticed the hole, the lining tore right through.
4. I had repaired the zip twice before I gave up on it.
5. The base already went grey by the time I got it back from the shop.
6. I had packed it the night before, so the morning was easy.
7. When I looked inside, I saw that someone left the receipt in the front pocket.
8. Until that winter I never carried anything heavier than a book in it.

**Answer note:** correct as printed: 1, 2, 4, 6. X: 3 (had torn), 5 (had already gone), 7 (had left), 8 (had
never carried). In every X sentence the earlier event is the one in the wrong form, which is the pattern worth
naming aloud rather than marking silently.

**Level 5, Activity 2 (fill in a model paragraph, 8 blanks):** a narrative paragraph about a bag with the verbs
blanked. Fill each one so the order of events is clear from the forms alone.

> I (1. buy: ___) the bag in my first week at the new job, mostly because the shop (2. be: ___) on my way home
> and I (3. carry: ___) a plastic one until then. For the first year nothing (4. happen: ___) to it. Then, on a
> wet Tuesday in November, the strap (5. give: ___) way at the station. It (6. fray: ___) quietly for months
> before that, and I (7. notice: ___) nothing. By the time I (8. get: ___) home, I was carrying it under one arm
> like a parcel.

**Answer note:** 1 bought; 2 was; 3 had carried; 4 happened; 5 gave; 6 had frayed (accept had been fraying); 7
had noticed; 8 got. Blank 8 is the trap: "by the time" pulls people toward a past perfect, but that clause is
the narrated line, not a reach-back.

**Levels 6-8, Activity 3 (analysis of a completed essay):** read "The Pair I Replaced," printed in the packet
now with its introduction and conclusion in place around the body paragraphs. Each Level does a different thing
with the same text.

- **Level 6:** name the hook type, count the sentences between the hook and the thesis, copy the thesis out, and
  name the conclusion's closing move.
- **Level 7 (a different shape):** the hook here is allowed at your Level but is not the strongest one available
  to you. Rewrite it as a quote hook or a statistic hook, then say in one line what your version makes a reader
  expect that the original does not.
- **Level 8 (a different shape):** this essay's bodies are one continuous group. Say in one line what the
  introduction would have to do differently if the bodies were in two groups written for two readers, and mark
  the sentence in the existing introduction that would have to carry that work.

**Answer note:** the hook is a scenario (a single wet Tuesday, in progress). Two connecting sentences sit
between it and the thesis. The thesis is the last sentence of the introduction and is direct, naming padding
and cable. The conclusion's closing move is a suggestion (press the padding with your thumb). Level 7's rewrite
is open; a quote hook on a line about buying cheap twice, or a statistic on return rates, both work. Level 8's
answer: the third sentence, "Three years later I can say exactly what the money bought," is the one that would
have to open two questions rather than one.

**Level 5 bridge (receptive, 5 minutes inside Activity 2's block):** on the same essay, underline the sentence
in the conclusion that says the thesis again in different words, then write one sentence saying what the very
last sentence does. Level 5's own output stays two paragraphs across the whole Pair; this is exposure, not a
task Level.

**Levels 6-8, Activity 4 (hook types, 6 candidates):** the packet prints six openings written for the same
essay.

1. The old pair finally gave up on a Tuesday, in the rain, halfway between the station and my front door.
2. "Buy cheap, buy twice," my father said, every time anything in our house broke.
3. About a third of the headphones sold at this price are returned inside two years.
4. There is a rubber ring on the left hinge that I have now replaced three times.
5. Are expensive headphones actually worth the money?
6. This essay will explain why my headphones are better than the ones I had before.

- **Level 6:** label 1 to 6 with their type, and cross out the one that is not a hook at all.
- **Level 7 (a different shape):** name the two that are not available at your Level and say why in one line
  each. Then take one of the remaining and write the connecting sentence that would follow it.
- **Level 8 (a different shape):** choose the one opening that could set up two readers at once, and write the
  second sentence so that both readers' questions stay open.

**Answer note:** 1 scenario; 2 quote; 3 statistic; 4 withheld detail; 5 plain question; 6 not a hook, a thesis
announcement, and the one to cross out. Level 7's two: 5 (a plain question is not available above Level 6) and
6 (not a hook at any Level). Level 8's best candidate is 4, since a part that has been replaced three times is
simultaneously a reason to buy and a reason to stop repairing.

**Model essay, "The Pair I Replaced," complete** (introduction, the three body paragraphs from Module 1
reprinted, and conclusion; 338 words; printed in the packet for Activities 3 and 4 and the Level 5 bridge;
a model for analysis, not a Mentor Essay):

> The old pair finally gave up on a Tuesday, in the rain, halfway between the station and my front door. I had
> been promising myself a replacement since the spring, and I had done nothing about it, so the decision got
> made for me. Three years later I can say exactly what the money bought. The pair I use now is better than the
> pair it replaced for two reasons: the padding has lasted, and the cable finally stays out of the way.
>
> The first difference is the padding. The old cups were covered in a soft foam that flattened inside a year, so
> by the end the metal band underneath pressed on the top of my head after about forty minutes. The pair I have
> now uses a firmer foam under a fabric that has not gone shiny, and I have worn them through a whole shift
> without noticing them. That is the test that matters on a long day.
>
> The second difference is the cable. The old one ran from the left cup and caught on every coat button between
> the door and the platform. This pair keeps its cable in a channel along the headband, and the plug sits flush
> against the cup. Nothing catches.
>
> You asked whether they are worth the money, Tomas. They cost about twice what the old pair did. I have had
> them three years, and the only part I have replaced is a rubber ring that costs almost nothing. I still keep
> the old pair in the drawer.
>
> Three years and one rubber ring later, the case is simple enough: firm padding and a cable with nowhere to
> catch are the two things that decide whether a pair survives being worn every day, and this pair has both. If
> you are ever standing in a shop with two pairs at the same price, ignore the sound for a moment and press the
> padding with your thumb. That is the part that will still be deciding things three years from now.

### Phase 1: Editing a paragraph on Focus A (20 min)

One narrative paragraph about a bag, printed in the packet with **8 verified errors**, all of them about which
line a verb belongs to. **Levels 5-7:** find and correct the eight. **Level 8 (a heavier tier, a different
shape):** the same eight, then rewrite the two sentences marked with an asterisk so that each one signals its
reach-back with a cue word from the grammar box as well as with the verb form.

> I bought the bag in a hurry, because the shop was closing and it was the only one in my size. For the first
> year nothing happened to it, and then the strap gave way on a wet Tuesday in November. It frayed quietly for
> months before that, and I noticed nothing.* Until that winter I never used it for anything heavier than a
> book. By then the lining split as well. When I looked inside that evening, I saw that someone left the receipt
> in the front pocket. The zip already stuck twice, and I had it repaired the week after.* Ever since then, I
> had checked the strap every few months. When I finally replaced it, I had realized how much I had been
> carrying in it.

The eight errors: (1) "It frayed quietly for months" to "It had frayed"; (2) "I noticed nothing" to "I had
noticed nothing"; (3) "Until that winter I never used it" to "I had never used it"; (4) "By then the lining
split" to "the lining had split"; (5) "someone left the receipt" to "someone had left"; (6) "The zip already
stuck twice" to "The zip had already stuck twice"; (7) "Ever since then, I had checked" to "I checked", a past
perfect where the narrated line belongs; (8) "I had realized" to "I realized", the same reverse error on the
narrated line. Count checked: 8, in 9 sentences.

**Answer note:** errors 7 and 8 are the reverse of 1 to 6 and are the point of the paragraph: six students will
find the first six quickly and then stop, because the last two look like more of what they have been doing
right. Level 8's rewrites are open; any cue word from the bank that fits ("by then," "before that," "up to that
point") is accepted, as is a cue placed at the front of the sentence.

### Phase 2: Original sentences and openings with Focus A (25 min)

**Level 5, Activity 5:** write six sentences about your own bag. Each one pairs a later event with an earlier one
and uses the past perfect for the earlier. Use a different cue word from the Draft Word Bank in three of them.
Then underline the one sentence a reader could use to guess how you feel about the bag, with no feeling word in
it.

**Level 6, Activity 6 (a different shape):** write the first three sentences of an anecdote hook about your own
bag. One of the three reaches back with the past perfect to something that happened before the incident opens.
Then write one line saying what a reader knows after those three sentences that they did not know before.

**Level 7, Activity 7 (a different shape):** write the same three-sentence opening about your own bag twice:
once with the outcome held back to the end, once with the outcome given in the first sentence. Then write one
line saying what each version makes a reader do.

**Level 8, Activity 8 (a different shape):** write a three-sentence opening about your own bag in which the past
perfect creates a gap: something happened before the incident, and you name that it happened without saying
what it was. Then write one line naming the gap, and one line saying what a reader is likely to assume in the
meantime.

**Answer note:** no key; all four are original work on the student's own bag. Circulation check: Level 5's
underlined sentence contains no feeling word; Level 6's reach-back is genuinely earlier than the opening
incident rather than a restatement of it; Level 7's two versions differ in order only, not in content; Level 8's
named gap is something the writer actually knows and is choosing to withhold, not something vague.

### Phase 3: Mentor Ladder second look (30 min, teacher-presented, never printed)

The same four models from Module 1's Lesson 1, in Level order, on the same object (the writer's headphones,
written for Tomas), each now completed by its Module 2 part, so the class sees once what a finished piece looks
like. **A before-and-after framing, not a new ladder:** for each Level, present Module 1's model first, then the
part Module 2 adds, and ask what the new part tells a reader that the old one could not. The ladder is the
teacher's material and is not printed in the packet.

**Level 5 Mentor Text, completed (second paragraph, 6 sentences, 126 words):**

> I had been using the pair before these for four years when the left cup finally stopped working. It went on a
> Sunday, in the middle of a call, and I spent the rest of that call holding the cup against my ear with one
> hand. I ordered these the same evening, without reading a single review, because I had already decided months
> earlier that I would buy whatever the shop had in stock. They came on the Wednesday. I wore them straight out
> of the box on the walk to the station, and by the time I got there I had stopped noticing them, which had
> never once happened with the old pair. That is still the first thing I would tell anyone about them.

One thing that happened, told in order. Why it mattered: the last sentence. The reach-backs are "had been
using," "had already decided," and "had never once happened." The implied feeling sits in "which had never once
happened with the old pair," with no feeling word anywhere.

**Level 6 Mentor Essay, completed (introduction 93 words, conclusion 64 words):**

> _Introduction._ The left cup of my old pair died in the middle of a call, on a Sunday, and I finished that
> call holding it against my ear with one hand. I had been putting off a replacement since the spring, so the
> decision got made for me that evening. Three years later I can say exactly what the money bought, which is
> what you asked me. For the way you use headphones, Tomas, this pair is worth the money for two reasons: the
> padding holds its shape, and the microphone is usable outdoors.
>
> _Conclusion._ Three years and one rubber ring later, the answer has not changed: firm padding and a microphone
> out of the wind decide whether a pair survives daily wear, and this pair has both. If you are ever choosing
> between two pairs at the same price, press the padding with your thumb. That is the part that will still be
> deciding things in three years.

Scenario hook; two connecting sentences narrowing to the thesis; the direct thesis last, unchanged from the
working thesis line. The conclusion restates it in new words and closes on a suggestion, with no new
information.

**Level 7 Mentor Essay, completed (introduction 77 words, conclusion 64 words):**

> _Introduction._ "You will hear them," a colleague said, the first week I wore these into the office, and she
> was not talking about the music. Up to that point I had not thought about what a pair of headphones announces
> about the person wearing them. Since then I have been unable to unhear it, and it has changed what I think I
> bought. These have earned their place for reasons that have very little to do with sound.
>
> _Conclusion._ So the case for them is not the one I would have made three years ago, and it is not really a
> case about sound at all. What I bought, without knowing it, was a way of being unavailable that nobody could
> take personally. Whether that is worth the money is a question I am not sure I am the right person to answer.

A quote hook, which Level 6 could not use. The indirect thesis stays indirect. The reach-back ("had not
thought") is what lets the introduction hold two times at once. The closing move is a question, and it declines
to settle the implication the bodies built.

**Level 8 Mentor Essay, completed (introduction 120 words, conclusion 92 words):**

> _Introduction._ The rubber ring on the left hinge costs two pounds, and I have bought three of them. That is
> most of the history of this pair in one line, and it reads differently depending on which of you is asking.
> Tomas wants to know whether to buy a pair. Nadia wants to know whether to stop repairing the one she already
> has. I had assumed those were opposite questions, and I had assumed the answers would come out opposite too,
> until I sat down to write both of them. Whether you are deciding to buy this pair, Tomas, or deciding whether
> to keep the one you already own, Nadia, the answer comes out the same, though the reasons do not.
>
> _Conclusion._ Both of you should do the same thing, which is the part I did not expect when I started writing
> this down. Buy them, Tomas. Keep repairing them, Nadia. What I cannot tell either of you is whether the reason
> I can recommend both is that the pair is genuinely that good, or that a fault you can name and fix for two
> pounds is a fault you never quite decide to stop paying for. Three rings in, I have stopped being able to tell
> the difference from where I am standing.

A withheld-detail hook (the three rings, unexplained) that names both readers and both questions without
revealing that the second answer complicates the first. The reach-back ("I had assumed ... and I had assumed")
is the sentence that sets the complication up. The conclusion refuses to resolve it: two short imperative
sentences, then a long one that takes the recommendation back apart, then a last line that shifts distance
without breaking voice.

**Walkthrough (teacher-led, from the board):** one line per Level naming what the added part does. Level 5: the
story tells us why the description was worth writing. Level 6: the beginning gets Tomas in and the ending sends
him out with something to do. Level 7: the beginning holds two times at once, and the ending refuses to answer.
Level 8: the beginning opens two questions and the ending admits it cannot separate them. Students find their
own Level's model and the one directly above it, and say one thing to a partner about the difference. Nothing
here is printed.

**Board-dependent moment, co-constructed live:** a "what Module 2 adds at each Level" board built from student
observations, in their own words ("now we know why he bothered," "she starts with somebody else talking," "he
tells you the answer and then takes it back"). The teacher writes what is said, not a prepared list. It stays up
for Lesson 2.
