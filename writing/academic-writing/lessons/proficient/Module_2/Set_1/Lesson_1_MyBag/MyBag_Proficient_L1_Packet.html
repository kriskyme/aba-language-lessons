Grammar: Reaching Back Without Losing the Thread

The two lines a story runs on

The events you are telling, in the order they happened, are in the past simple. An event that happened before the one you are telling goes in the past perfect: had plus the past participle.

The line of events (past simple)Reaching back (past perfect)
The strap gave way on the platform.It had frayed quietly for months.
I bought the bag in my first week.I had carried a plastic one until then.
The zip stuck for the last time in November.By then I had already had it repaired twice.
I looked inside that evening.Someone had left the receipt in the front pocket.

You only need it once

Once the reach-back is set up, the sentences inside it can go back to the past simple without confusing anyone: I had bought it in a hurry. The shop was closing, I had twenty minutes, and it was the only one in my size.

Cue words

These signal a reach-back, so the verb form is not doing all the work on its own: already, by then, until then, up to that point, before that, the year before, earlier that autumn.

The error to hunt

A past perfect used for the line of events itself stalls the story. "Ever since then, I had checked the strap every few months" should be "I checked." Reach back only for what is genuinely earlier.

The Two Parts You Have Not Written Yet

Where the beginning and the ending sit

1. The shape. An essay is an introduction, the body paragraphs, and a conclusion. You already have the bodies. The working thesis line at the top of them is a note to yourself, and the introduction will absorb it.

2. The introduction runs hook, then two or three connecting sentences, then the thesis last. The connecting sentences carry a reader from one moment down to the whole subject, narrowing as they go.

3. The conclusion says the thesis again in new words and then makes one closing move: a suggestion, a prediction, a question, or an opinion. It adds no new information.

4. Hook types, told as a story:

Model essay: The Pair I Replaced

The old pair finally gave up on a Tuesday, in the rain, halfway between the station and my front door. I had been promising myself a replacement since the spring, and I had done nothing about it, so the decision got made for me. Three years later I can say exactly what the money bought. The pair I use now is better than the pair it replaced for two reasons: the padding has lasted, and the cable finally stays out of the way.

The first difference is the padding. The old cups were covered in a soft foam that flattened inside a year, so by the end the metal band underneath pressed on the top of my head after about forty minutes. The pair I have now uses a firmer foam under a fabric that has not gone shiny, and I have worn them through a whole shift without noticing them. That is the test that matters on a long day.

The second difference is the cable. The old one ran from the left cup and caught on every coat button between the door and the platform. This pair keeps its cable in a channel along the headband, and the plug sits flush against the cup. Nothing catches.

You asked whether they are worth the money, Tomas. They cost about twice what the old pair did. I have had them three years, and the only part I have replaced is a rubber ring that costs almost nothing. I still keep the old pair in the drawer.

Three years and one rubber ring later, the case is simple enough: firm padding and a cable with nowhere to catch are the two things that decide whether a pair survives being worn every day, and this pair has both. If you are ever standing in a shop with two pairs at the same price, ignore the sound for a moment and press the padding with your thumb. That is the part that will still be deciding things three years from now.

Find the Mistakes

A paragraph about a bag

I bought the bag in a hurry, because the shop was closing and it was the only one in my size. For the first year nothing happened to it, and then the strap gave way on a wet Tuesday in November. It frayed quietly for months before that, and I noticed nothing.* Until that winter I never used it for anything heavier than a book. By then the lining split as well. When I looked inside that evening, I saw that someone left the receipt in the front pocket. The zip already stuck twice, and I had it repaired the week after.* Ever since then, I had checked the strap every few months. When I finally replaced it, I had realized how much I had been carrying in it.
