## What this lesson carries forward

**The piece so far.** Every student's folder holds Module 1's kept part: at Level 5 one extended descriptive
paragraph about their bag, at Levels 6 to 8 the body paragraphs of a comparison essay under a working thesis
line. "The bag" means whichever bag that student circled; it is a subject, not a prop (Quality Standards §D8).

**This lesson writes the second part,** in Module 2's form:

- **Level 5:** a second paragraph telling one real thing that happened with the bag, in order, with why it
  mattered and one detail that implies a feeling.
- **Levels 6 to 8:** the **introduction and the conclusion** of the essay, written around the bodies that already
  exist. The working thesis line becomes the last sentence of the introduction, unchanged.

Neither part is a complete piece on its own; Lesson 4 assembles the two.

**Grammar already taught (Module 2's Lessons 1-2), used here without re-teaching.** Focus A: the narrated line is
past simple, an earlier event is past perfect, the reach-back is stated once, and cue words carry part of the
signal. Focus B: fronting, with inversion only after a negative or restrictive adverbial; the it-cleft and the
wh-cleft, one per paragraph at most; used to for repeated actions and states, would for actions only. Essay
Focus A: hook types, the connecting sentences that narrow to the thesis, the thesis last, and the four closing
moves. Module 1's tools stay in force for the kept part and are named again in Day 2's merged checklist.

**Nothing carried from Lesson 2 (Quality Standards §D9).** Lesson 2 Day 2's planning was rehearsal. Every plan
this lesson uses is built in Day 1 Phase 1 inside this packet, and the reminders it needs are reprinted there.
The kept part is the one carried piece.

**Draft Word Bank, Module 2** (reprinted; posted on the board and printed in both units of the packet):

- Time and sequence: that winter, the week after, by then, until then, ever since, the first month
- Reaching back: already, up to that point, the year before, earlier that autumn, before that, since the spring
- What happened: split, snapped, gave way, soaked through, held, survived
- How it turned out: worth it, a nuisance, no trouble, the last straw, fine in the end, still going
- Parts to name exactly: the strap, the base, the zip, the lining, the buckle, the front pocket

### Phase 1: Quick plan (10 min)

Take the kept part out of your folder and keep it beside you. Then, on this page:

- **Level 5, Task A:** circle one: **the day I got it** / **one day I carried it that mattered.** Then write four
  events in the order they happened, one line each, and put a star beside the one detail that will let a reader
  guess how you feel.
- **Level 6, Task B:** name your hook type, write its first line, and copy your working thesis out. Then name
  your conclusion's closing move.
- **Level 7, Task C:** name your hook type, write its first line, and write one line saying what it withholds.
  Copy your indirect working thesis out. Then name your closing move.
- **Level 8, Task D:** name your hook type and write the sentence that opens both readers' questions. Copy your
  working thesis out. Then write one line naming what your conclusion will refuse to settle.

**Reminders printed with the plan** (nothing here is recalled from an earlier lesson):

| Hook type | What it does | Available at |
|---|---|---|
| Scenario | drops the reader into a single moment, in progress | 6, 7, 8 |
| Anecdote | tells one short true incident and stops | 6, 7, 8 |
| Withheld detail | names something without explaining it yet | 6, 7, 8 |
| Quote | opens on someone's words | 7, 8 |
| Statistic | opens on a number that reframes the subject | 7, 8 |
| Plain question | asks the reader something | 6 only |

The introduction runs hook, then two or three connecting sentences that narrow, then the thesis **last**. The
conclusion restates the thesis in new words and then makes one closing move: a suggestion, a prediction, a
question, or an opinion. It adds no new information. **Your working thesis is copied out unchanged; it is not
rewritten.**

_Teacher circulation, not printed:_ does each plan already carry that Level's required feature? A Level 6 plan
whose hook is a one-line summary of the essay has not got a hook. A Level 8 plan whose opening sentence names
only one reader has not yet split. A Level 5 plan with a star on an adjective rather than an event needs one
more minute.

### Phase 2: Independent drafting, complete today (50 min)

Every Level writes Module 2's part in full today. Nobody rewrites the part they already have.

- **Level 5, Task E:** write your second paragraph. Tell the events in the order they happened. Use the past
  perfect where you reach back to something earlier, and at least two cue words from the Draft Word Bank. One
  sentence says why it mattered. One detail lets a reader guess how you feel, and no feeling word appears
  anywhere. Count your sentences at the end and write the number in the box.
- **Level 6, Task F:** write your introduction, then your conclusion. The introduction opens on your hook, then
  two connecting sentences that narrow toward your subject without previewing your two points, then your working
  thesis as the last sentence, copied out unchanged. The conclusion restates that thesis in different words and
  closes on a suggestion. Add no fact that is not already in your body paragraphs. Count the sentences in your
  introduction and write the number in the box.
- **Level 7, Task G:** the same two parts, with your hook a quote, a statistic, or an anecdote, and your indirect
  thesis last. Your conclusion closes on a question or an opinion rather than a suggestion, and does not settle
  the thing your bodies made a reader infer. When you change something as you write, cross it out and write the
  new version beside it. Do not erase. Count the sentences in your introduction and write the number in the box.
- **Level 8, Task H:** the same two parts. Your hook names something that belongs to both readers' questions at
  once, without explaining it. Your connecting sentences keep both questions open, and your thesis is copied out
  unchanged as the last sentence. Your conclusion gives both readers the same answer and then declines to settle
  which reading of the bag is the right one. When you change something as you write, cross it out and write the
  new version beside it. Count the sentences in your introduction and write the number in the box.

**Board-dependent moment:** a running "strong sentence" board. As students volunteer a line from the draft in
progress, write it up unedited and underline the part that does the work: a reach-back, a fronted adverbial, a
cleft, a closing move that lands. The board grows through the block and is not prepared in advance.

_Teacher circulation, not printed:_ check the required feature is appearing while the part is being written, not
only at the end. The two to catch early: an introduction whose connecting sentences have turned into a summary of
the body paragraphs, and a conclusion that has introduced a fact the bodies never mentioned. At Level 8, check by
the halfway point that the hook is genuinely holding two questions rather than addressing one reader and
mentioning the other.

### Phase 3: Mid-draft share-out (15 min)

Three or four students, one from each Level where possible, read one sentence from their draft: an opening line,
a closing move, or an event. The class says what the sentence does, not whether they like it. Add the sentences
to the strong-sentence board.

### Phases 1 and 2 combined into one Task per Level: check both parts, then improve (55 min)

**Level 5, Task A.**

_First paragraph (Module 1's part)_
1. Circle one shortened clause. Does it have its commas right: none if it says which one you mean, two if it
   adds information about a thing already named?
2. Box the detail that lets a reader guess how you feel about the bag. Is it still a fact rather than an
   adjective about you?

_Second paragraph (Module 2's part)_
3. Underline every verb. Is the line of events in the past simple, and does every reach-back use had plus the
   past participle?
4. Circle each cue word you used. Are there at least two?
5. Underline the sentence that says why it mattered.
6. Box the detail that lets a reader guess how you feel here. Is there a feeling word anywhere in the
   paragraph? If there is, it has to go.

_Both paragraphs_
7. Read them in order. Do they describe the same bag without contradicting each other, and does the second
   paragraph have 6 to 9 sentences?
8. Now improve them. Replace one flat word with a more exact one from the Draft Word Bank. If a reach-back is
   doing its work with the verb form alone, add a cue word in front of it. If your two boxed details imply the
   same thing, change one of them.

**Level 6, Task B.**

_Module 1's part (your body paragraphs)_
1. Underline the first sentence of each body paragraph. Does each one still state that paragraph's single point?
2. Circle the sentence that says why a feature matters to Priya. Is it still there and still about what she will
   be doing with the bag?

_Module 2's part (your introduction and conclusion)_
3. Box your hook. Is it one of the allowed types, and is it a moment rather than a summary of the essay?
4. Number your connecting sentences. Do they narrow from the hook toward your subject without previewing your
   two points?
5. Is your thesis the last sentence of the introduction, and is it the working thesis copied out unchanged?
6. Underline the sentence in your conclusion that says the thesis again in different words.
7. Does your conclusion add any fact that is not in your body paragraphs? If it does, cross it out.

_Both parts_
8. Read the whole essay in order. Does the introduction promise what the bodies deliver?
9. Now improve it. Rewrite any connecting sentence that previews your points. If your hook is a summary, replace
   it with one moment. Add to the circled Priya sentence one thing about her year of commuting that the feature
   answers.

**Level 7, Task C.**

_Module 1's part (your body paragraphs)_
1. Underline the three or four details you are relying on to carry your implication. Are they still facts about
   the bag?
2. Circle each cohesion word you used: admittedly, what's more, this suggests, in fairness. Is each still doing
   the job it names?

_Module 2's part (your introduction and conclusion)_
3. Box your hook. Is it a quote, a statistic, or an anecdote, and does it point at something without explaining
   it?
4. Number your connecting sentences. Do they narrow without giving away what the hook withholds?
5. Is your indirect thesis the last sentence of the introduction, and is it copied out unchanged?
6. Underline your closing move. Is it a question or an opinion, and does it leave your implication standing
   rather than explaining it?
7. Are your crossings-out still legible?

_Both parts_
8. Hand the whole essay to a partner. Ask them to write in the margin what they think you are not saying. Write
   what they wrote here: ______________________
9. Now improve it. If your partner reached the implication only from the conclusion, the bodies are doing too
   little and the conclusion too much: move one detail earlier. If they missed it entirely, do not add a
   sentence stating it; change or add one detail. Leave every crossing-out visible.

**Level 8, Task D.**

_Module 1's part (your two groups)_
1. Draw a line across the page where the Priya group ends and the Marcus group begins. Are the two still built
   differently in sentence length, order, and level of abstraction?
2. Mark with a T each place where the tone or the level of abstraction changes. You still need at least two.

_Module 2's part (your introduction and conclusion)_
3. Box your hook. Does it name one thing that belongs to both readers' questions, without explaining it?
4. Number your connecting sentences. Do both readers' questions stay open through all of them?
5. Is your thesis the last sentence of the introduction, and is it copied out unchanged?
6. Underline the sentence in your conclusion that gives both readers the same answer.
7. Underline your closing move. Does it decline to settle which reading of the bag is right, without
   contradicting the answer you just gave?
8. Are your crossings-out still legible?

_Both parts_
9. Hand the whole essay to a partner. Ask them to write down the answer each group reaches and whether the
   ending settles anything. Write what they wrote here: ______________________
10. Now improve it. If your partner said the ending settled it, cut whichever sentence did the settling. If your
    hook turned out to belong to only one reader, replace the thing it names with a part of the bag both readers
    would ask about. Leave every crossing-out visible.

**Answer note:** none of these has a key; they are checks on the student's own piece. Circulation priorities, in
order: a Level 6 introduction whose connecting sentences preview the two points, which is this position's most
common failure; a conclusion at any Level carrying a new fact; a Level 7 conclusion that explains the
implication instead of leaving it; a Level 8 ending that resolves cleanly because it is easier to write that
way; and, at Level 5, a feeling word that has crept into the new paragraph.

### Phase 3: Final line check (20 min)

Each student reads their last sentence to a partner, then reads the partner's. The only question asked is
whether that sentence is the one the whole piece should end on. Two or three pairs are cold-called to read their
closing lines aloud from their seats; add them to the strong-sentence board.

Nothing is collected. Both parts stay with the student for Lesson 4.

**What this lesson does not do.** There is no Peer Editing exchange, no publishing, and no Closing Transfer
Check at this position; the piece has not been assembled yet. Every student leaves Day 2 with both parts checked
once, together, and the new part improved. Lesson 4 assembles the whole, runs Peer Editing on both Modules'
features, revises for effect, and publishes.
