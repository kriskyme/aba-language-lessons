## Task Levels for this plan (Proficient band)

Proficient's task Levels are **5, 6, 7, 8** (Conventions §B): two extension-down Levels borrowed from Advanced
(5, 6) and the band's two native Levels (7, 8), with no extension-up, since Level 8 is the ceiling of the
scale. Four task Levels, so every lesson carries four differentiated task-Level sections. Quoted verbatim from
`learningobjectives.csv` (Module 1: Describing), **Reading modality only**:

| Level | Role | Reading Can-do |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | extension-down | "Can read an extended description, summarize how the writer organizes details, and identify at least one clearly evaluative word choice that signals the writer's attitude even though the writer never states the opinion directly, decoding unfamiliar words as needed." |
| 6 | extension-down | "Can read an extended description and identify specific word choices that create a positive, negative, or neutral impression, separating explicitly stated description from clearly-signaled evaluative comments, and locate at least one place where the writer's language visibly shifts partway through (e.g. from confident claims to a hedge like 'results may vary')." |
| 7 | native (anchor Level) | "Can read two extended descriptions of the same subject, critically assess how each writer's rhetorical choices shape a different impression, and identify at least one unstated interest or assumption behind one writer's framing that isn't explicitly acknowledged, using sophisticated inference and register awareness." |
| 8 | native | "Can read a stylistically dense extended description - layered with irony, understatement, or deliberate ambiguity - and (a) map shifts in tone, register, and abstraction to their rhetorical function, (b) identify at least one place where the literal description and the writer's real attitude diverge, and (c) assess how a specific alternate audience would likely misread that divergence." |

**The anchor is a paired text set, not a single passage.** The anchor is calibrated to Level 7 (Conventions §A),
and Level 7's Can-do requires *two* extended descriptions of the same subject. Lesson Prompt 0.2's Level 7 row
allows exactly this: 600-700 words total, roughly 300-350 per text, 7-11 paragraphs across the pair, average
sentence length 20-25 words, full academic and idiomatic register. Both texts are lettered as one continuous
sequence per 0.9 (Text 1 `[A]`-`[D]`, Text 2 `[E]`-`[H]`), so every item can cite a paragraph letter
unambiguously.

This differs from the Advanced plan, where the second text was a Level-7-only add-on printed inside that task.
Here the pair *is* the shared centerpiece every Level works from:

- **Level 5 and Level 6** work from **Text 1 only** (the promotional/insider description in each lesson below),
  which is the more rhetorically loaded of the two and carries the evaluative word choices and the visible
  language shift those two Levels need. Neither Level is asked to compare across texts - that is Level 7's own
  ceiling and must not be pulled down into theirs (Quality Standards §A3).
- **Level 7** compares Text 1 against Text 2 and names one unstated interest or assumption behind Text 1's
  framing.
- **Level 8** works the same pair but at its own ceiling: mapping tone/register/abstraction shifts to their
  rhetorical function, locating where Text 1's literal description and its writer's real attitude diverge, and
  assessing how a **named** alternate audience (specified per lesson below) would misread that divergence.

**Level 8 and the anchor ceiling.** Level 8's Can-do needs a stylistically dense, layered description. That
layering lives in **Text 1** of each pair - a text whose surface is descriptive and whose framing is doing
promotional or self-justifying work - while the pair as a whole stays inside Level 7's 0.2 ceiling. The
difficulty Level 8 adds is task demand on the same text, not a harder text, which is what keeps the
band-distance invariant (Conventions §B) intact for the Level 5 student reading the same page.

**No fixed-output Levels.** Levels 1-2 are not task Levels in this band, so Lesson Prompt 0.11 / Quality
Standards §D10 slot-to-shape assignment does not apply to this Set.

## Topic grounding

Every topic names a real, documented, lookup-able subject, as the Module Lesson-Plan prompt's band-conditioned
rule requires (and requires most strictly at this band). The *frame* around each text is invented - a staff
writer, a period visitor, a podcast guest, a novelist's narrator - and **no statement is attributed to a real,
named individual**. Lesson 2 in particular writes an original period-style visitor entry by a fictional
visitor: it does not put words into Queen Victoria's or Henry Cole's real diaries, both of which survive and
are quotable. Hashima's tour mechanics and the Great Exhibition's building and catalogue were confirmed by live
search this pass; the Eames lounge chair and Trans-Siberian platskart carriage are grounded in stable,
well-established knowledge and will be checked again at generation time, when their concrete details are
actually written.

### Per-lesson task-Level basis

Each Level's task below is built to satisfy the Can-do quoted above for that Level, and nothing beyond it.

**Lesson 1 - Hashima Island.** Text 1: the operator's feature ("what you will see on the route"). Text 2: the
reporter's account of the same 45-60 minutes.
- **L5:** summarize how Text 1 organizes its details (the crossing, the circuit of the island, the three
  permitted viewing points) and identify one clearly evaluative word choice signalling the writer's attitude to
  the ruins, where no opinion is stated outright.
- **L6:** separate Text 1's stated description (distances, dates, what is visible from each viewing point) from
  its clearly-signalled evaluative comments, and locate the point where its language visibly shifts from
  confident claims into hedging about what the route does not reach.
- **L7:** compare Texts 1 and 2 on the same three subjects (the seawall, the apartment blocks, the parts of the
  island closed to visitors) and name one unstated interest behind the operator's framing that the text never
  admits to.
- **L8:** map Text 1's shifts in tone and register to their rhetorical function; identify one place where its
  literal description and its writer's real attitude diverge; assess how **a traveller booking the tour from
  the operator's own page** would likely misread that divergence.

**Lesson 2 - The Crystal Palace.** Text 1: the official catalogue-style description. Text 2: the fictional
visitor's diary entry, dated the same summer.
- **L5:** summarize how the catalogue text organizes its description (dimensions, then materials, then the
  route through the transept) and identify one evaluative word choice signalling official pride.
- **L6:** separate stated fact (span, acreage, quantity of glass) from clearly-signalled evaluative comment,
  and locate the shift where the catalogue's confident register gives way to a hedge about crowding or heat.
- **L7:** compare the catalogue and the diary on the same three subjects (the glazing and light, the crowds,
  the exhibits themselves) and name one unstated assumption behind the catalogue's framing.
- **L8:** map the catalogue's shifts in register and abstraction to their function; identify where its literal
  description of the building and its real attitude to the visiting public diverge; assess how **a reader in
  1851 who had not visited and could not afford the entrance fee** would likely misread that divergence.

**Lesson 3 - The Eames Lounge Chair.** Text 1: the design historian's turns. Text 2: the restorer's turns,
same episode, same chair.
- **L5:** summarize how the historian organizes their description (silhouette, then materials, then how it sits
  in a room) and identify one evaluative word choice signalling their attitude to the chair.
- **L6:** separate the historian's stated description (plywood shells, leather cushions, the angle of the back)
  from their clearly-signalled evaluative comment, and locate the shift where confident description turns
  hedged about how the chair has aged.
- **L7:** compare the two speakers on the same three subjects (the plywood shell, the leather, the chair's
  proportions) and name one unstated interest behind the historian's framing.
- **L8:** map the historian's shifts in tone and abstraction to their function; identify where their literal
  description of wear and their real attitude to it diverge; assess how **a listener about to buy a
  second-hand chair on the strength of the episode** would likely misread that divergence.

**Lesson 4 - Trans-Siberian platskart carriage.** Text 1: the first narrator's excerpt. Text 2: the second
narrator's excerpt, same carriage, same route.
- **L5:** summarize how Narrator 1 organizes their description of the carriage (the bunk, then the corridor,
  then the window) and identify one evaluative word choice signalling their attitude to the space.
- **L6:** separate Narrator 1's stated description (bunk dimensions, timetable, temperature) from
  clearly-signalled evaluative comment, and locate where their language visibly shifts as the journey lengthens.
- **L7:** compare the two narrators on the same three subjects (the bunks, the shared corridor, the night
  hours) and name one unstated assumption behind Narrator 1's framing.
- **L8:** map Narrator 1's shifts in tone, register, and narrative distance to their function; identify where
  the literal description of the carriage and the narrator's real attitude to their fellow passengers diverge;
  assess how **a reader who has only travelled the route in a two-berth compartment** would likely misread that
  divergence.

### Interactivity notes

- **Lesson 1 (Panel Round):** 4-6 speakers in turn from their seats; every listener carries a running tally of
  which of the two texts each claim came from, plus one phrase they judge well put. Board moment sits in Day 1
  Phase 1 (Visual Inquiry predictions collected and checked against the texts) and Day 2 Phase 2 (a
  two-column impression tracker built live as groups report).
- **Lesson 2 (Jigsaw Expert Panels):** three prompts split across mixed groups, each group holding one shared
  subject (light, crowds, exhibits) across both texts before the report-back. Board moment moves to Day 1
  Phase 1 (Mystery Quote guesses checked off) and Day 2 Phase 3 (claims recorded as each group reports).
- **Lesson 3 (Rotating Partners):** everyone speaks at once in pairs, a new partner for each of the three
  prompts, so no listener task is needed. Reader's Theater on Day 1 gives the two podcast voices real speaker
  roles. Board moment moves to Day 1 Phase 2 (a live tone-shift map marked against paragraph letters as the
  transcript is read aloud) and Day 2 Phase 2.
- **Lesson 4 (Town Hall Role-Play):** three prompts split across tables; every student holds a role tied to one
  narrator. Board moment sits in Day 1 Phase 1 (the K-W-L chart's K and W columns built live, L column
  completed in Day 2 Phase 1) and Day 2 Phase 2.
- Formats across the Set run oral, collaborative, and visual: annotation marking (L1), expert-group synthesis
  (L2), scripted read-aloud in role (L3), and a live chart persisting across both days (L4). Writing appears
  only as a recording format, never as the ground of a task.
