**Vocabulary Theme:** Furniture, materials and wear (shell, veneer, patina, shock mount, delaminate, upholstery)
**Topic:** The Eames Lounge Chair (Herman Miller, 1956), described by a design historian and by a furniture restorer in the same podcast episode

**Fictional-subject note:** the chair is real and so is everything technical said about it. Models 670 and 671
were designed by Charles and Ray Eames for Herman Miller in 1956; the shells are moulded plywood with a face
veneer (Brazilian rosewood on the early production chairs, discontinued in the early 1990s); the cushions were
leather over down until foam was added from 1960; and the shells are joined to the frame with rubber shock
mounts, whose failure is the classic restoration job. **The podcast, its host, and both guests are invented.**
Dr. Ayala Voss and Marta Kellner are fictional, and nothing here is attributed to Charles or Ray Eames, to
Herman Miller, or to any other real, named individual or company spokesperson.

**Note on the anchor:** the anchor is a **paired text set**, per this band's Level 7 objective and the approved
plan. Paragraphs run [A]-[D] in Text 1 and [E]-[H] in Text 2 as one continuous lettered sequence. Levels 5 and 6
work from Text 1 only; Levels 7 and 8 work the pair.

### Phase 1: Pre-Reading & Activation (15 min)

**Take a Side hook (5 min):** this statement is printed in the packet with two boxes under it. Every student
marks one box on their own page, then turns to somebody who marked the other box and gives their reason. Nobody
moves to a corner or a side of the room; the choice lives on the page (Quality Standards §D11).

> **A chair that famous has stopped being furniture. It is an object now.**
>
> ☐ Agree  ☐ Disagree

Take two or three reasons from the floor without settling the question. It is settled, if at all, at the end of
Day 2.

**Skill Spotlight (2 min):** "Today we're practicing describing one thing from two different sets of interests,
and hearing how the describer's job changes what gets described. By the end you'll be able to describe something
of your own twice, once for someone admiring it and once for someone about to rely on it."

**Target vocabulary (6 min):** Pre-teach these 6 words using in-context paragraph matching. Show each word's own
sentence from the transcript, cold, and let students propose a meaning before you confirm it.

| Word | Paragraph | Student-friendly definition |
|---|---|---|
| shell | [A] | a single curved, moulded piece forming part of the chair's body |
| veneer | [B] | a very thin layer of fine wood glued over a cheaper or stronger material |
| patina | [C] | the surface a thing takes on through years of ordinary use, valued rather than repaired |
| shock mount | [D] | the small rubber block that joins a shell to the frame and lets it flex |
| delaminate | [F] | to come apart in layers, as glued sheets of wood separate |
| upholstery | [G] | the padding and covering of a seat, and the work of fitting it |

**Idiom and register spotlight (2 min):** Four expressions, on top of the vocabulary budget. Students may guess
first and then confirm against the gloss; the gloss never disappears (0.4).

- **"stands the test of time"** [C] - transparent. Stays good, or stays in use, over many years.
- **"cut corners"** [F] - **opaque, glossed:** to do a job cheaply or quickly by leaving out steps that should
  have been done. Nothing to do with actual corners.
- **"on its last legs"** [G] - **opaque, glossed:** almost worn out, close to failing. Ask students why a
  furniture restorer might enjoy using it.
- **"past its best"** [H] - transparent. No longer in the condition it once was.

**ANCHOR TEXT SET (Level 7, 695 words total, 8 turn-blocks)**

**TEXT 1 - "The Long Sit," episode 112, first excerpt. Guest: Dr. Ayala Voss, design historian.**

> [A] **HOST:** Why do people who have never sat in one still know the silhouette?
> **VOSS:** Because it is three curves and nothing else: two moulded shells for the seat and the back, a third
> for the headrest, and a cast aluminium base underneath that lets the whole thing turn. Charles and Ray Eames
> designed it for Herman Miller in 1956¹, and nobody has altered the shape since, which is rare enough in
> furniture to be worth saying out loud. You can put it in a room with anything at all and it will not argue
> with the other furniture.
>
> [B] **HOST:** What is it actually made of, underneath the reputation?
> **VOSS:** Layers of veneer, pressed and glued in a mould under heat and pressure, with the expensive wood only
> on the face, which is the part almost everyone misses. The earliest production chairs were Brazilian
> rosewood², and the cushions were leather over down, down alone, until 1960, when foam went in alongside it. It
> is a chair built by aircraft methods and then upholstered like something out of a gentlemen's club, and that
> contradiction is most of why it looks the way it does.
>
> [C] **HOST:** It has a reputation for lasting.
> **VOSS:** It stands the test of time in a way that almost nothing else from that decade manages, and the
> proportions are why: the back leans further than you expect, the seat sits low and deep, and the arms are
> already where your elbows are. Sixty years on, an original that has been sat in every evening looks exactly
> like a chair that has been sat in every evening. That is not damage; that is patina.
>
> [D] **HOST:** No reservations at all?
> **VOSS:** The rubber shock mounts holding the shells to the frame do perish in the end, and a chair that has
> stood in strong light will tell you so in the veneer. Whether any of that matters rather depends on what you
> want from the thing. If you want an object, it is perfect; if you want a chair, it is still, I would say,
> perfect enough.

**TEXT 2 - the same episode, second excerpt. Guest: Marta Kellner, furniture restorer.**

> [E] **HOST:** What actually comes through your workshop?
> **KELLNER:** Chairs that have stood in the sun, chairs that have been in a loft for twenty years, and chairs
> somebody bought in a hurry. Nine in ten are here for the shock mounts: the rubber hardens, then it cracks, and
> then the shell works against its fixing until the plywood splits around it. You do not always see that
> happening, and most people hear it first, as a knock when somebody leans back.
>
> [F] **HOST:** And the shells themselves?
> **KELLNER:** Veneer lifts: the face layer is very thin, and if a chair has sat by a window the glue lets go
> and the surface begins to delaminate along an edge, the headrest first, usually. That can be laid back down in
> a day, and nobody will know afterwards. What cannot be put right cheaply is a shell that split at the mount
> and was glued by somebody who cut corners, because then I am rebuilding plywood rather than repairing it.
>
> [G] **HOST:** Do the cushions matter as much as people think?
> **KELLNER:** The upholstery is the part buyers judge and the part I worry about least, because leather worn
> soft and dark is worth keeping and it is the first thing a seller photographs. Down that has flattened can be
> refilled in an afternoon by anybody with patience. I would far rather have tired cushions on sound shells than
> new cushions on a chair that is on its last legs underneath.
>
> [H] **HOST:** So what should somebody look at, before they buy?
> **KELLNER:** Lean the back hard and listen for a knock, then look along the edge of every shell for a lifted
> line, and turn the chair over and look at the mounts. Then sit in it properly: the back should lean further
> than you expect, and a chair that has been rebuilt badly will sit you up straighter than it ought to. A chair
> that is past its best is not always the one that looks tired; very often it is the one that has been made to
> look new.

---

> ¹ Herman Miller catalogues the pair as models 670 (the chair) and 671 (the ottoman). Both are still in
> production, which is why a second-hand chair may be sixty years old or six.
>
> ² Brazilian rosewood was withdrawn from production in the early 1990s under international trade restrictions.
> Later chairs use walnut, cherry, and other veneers instead.

> **FROM THE CLUB NEWSLETTER: BRINGING BACK A 1974 TOURING CARAVAN**
>
> [A] Ken bought it for two hundred pounds with a tree growing through the awning rail, and he has spent
> eighteen months on it since. The outer body ______ is aluminium over a timber frame, and the aluminium was
> the easy part: a wash, a polish, and it keeps the dull grey patina the club rather likes, which is more
> honest than a respray.
>
> [B] Inside was the real work. Damp had got into the roof panels and they had begun to delaminate, so the

>
> [C] The bench upholstery went out to a trimmer in the next village, who matched the original orange as nearly

> [D] It goes to its first rally in May, on the original wheels, with the awning Ken found folded in a box

3. Which would you rather buy, tired upholstery on a sound frame, or new upholstery on a weak one? Say why in

**Collaborative Evidence Matrix.** Mixed-ability groups of 4, one student per task Level. Each student completes

| Task Level | Cell prompt |
|---|---|
| 5 | Voss answers four questions in a fixed order. Write what each of the four is about, in four short phrases. Then write one word Voss uses that shows admiration, although Voss never says "I admire it." |

**Answer note (Level 5):** [A] the shape and why it is recognisable, [B] the materials, [C] why it lasts and how
it ages, [D] what is wrong with it. Admiring words include "perfect," "patina," and "does not argue."
**Answer note (Level 6):** [B] plain: "Layers of veneer, pressed and glued in a mould"; signalled: "upholstered
like something out of a gentlemen's club." [D] plain: "The rubber shock mounts holding the shells to the frame
do perish"; signalled: "perfect enough." The hedge turns at "Whether any of that matters."
**Answer note (Level 7):** shells: [D] "will tell you so in the veneer" against [F] "begins to delaminate along
an edge ... rebuilding plywood rather than repairing it." Leather: [B]/[C] leather over down and the evening

about least ... the first thing a seller photographs." Proportions: [C] the back, seat and arms as the reason it
lasts against [H] the same lean used as a test for a bad rebuild. Unstated interest: Voss is describing a
canonical object whose reputation is the subject of the episode, so a failure has to arrive as a reservation
rather than as a fault.
**Answer note (Level 8):** [A] plain answer, [B] technical run, [C] reverence, [D] defence. The divergence is in
[C]'s "That is not damage; that is patina," a sentence that describes an ageing chair while ruling out the word
for it; [D] then concedes the mounts perish. A listener about to buy second-hand hears "patina" and "perfect

### Phase 3: Structured Oral Output Debate & Synthesis (30 min) - Rotating Partners

**Format.** Everyone talks at once, in pairs, so no listener task is needed. Three prompts, roughly eight
minutes each, and a new partner for every prompt, found from where students sit. Nobody forms a circle or
crosses the room in a body (Quality Standards §D11).

**Rotated prompts:**
1. Describe the chair to your partner as Voss would, then as Kellner would. Your partner says which was which.
2. Your partner has money for one of the two: a chair with tired cushions and sound shells, or one with new
   cushions and a bad rebuild. Describe both so they can choose.
3. Go back to the statement you marked yesterday. Has either guest changed your mark? Describe the sentence
   that did or did not.

**Sentence stems by task Level:**
- **Level 5:** "Voss describes the chair as ___." / "The word ___ shows that ___."
- **Level 6:** "This part is description and this part is opinion, because ___." / "The language changes at ___,
  from ___ to ___."
- **Level 7:** "Both describe ___, but Voss makes it sound ___ and Kellner makes it sound ___." / "Voss needs the
  chair to be ___, which is why ___."
- **Level 8:** "The surface of this is ___; the work it is doing is ___." / "A buyer who heard only ___ would
  check ___, and would miss ___."

**Differentiated participation (Level 5, per 0.8):** before the first rotation, the Level 5 student rehearses
their opening sentence with a neighbour for thirty seconds, using their stem and one quotation they choose from
Text 1. They begin each rotation from that rehearsed sentence.

**Foundation Support (per Quality Standards §B, if a student is working below Level 5):** they match printed
phrases from the packet to the guest who said each, and hold up the phrase their partner is describing rather
than speaking a full turn. Nothing here needs the teacher to prepare anything.

**Group Synthesis and Closing Transfer Check (5 min).** Read the board's two rows aloud and ask which guest the
class would take with them to look at a chair, and which they would rather read.

Then the Closing Transfer Check, oral, in pairs, nothing collected: **describe the last room you slept in,
twice. First the way somebody letting it out would describe it. Then the way you would describe it to a friend
who has to sleep there tomorrow.** Same room, same facts, two sets of interests. Cold-call two or three pairs.
Students describe from memory; nothing is brought to class and nobody needs the room in front of them.
