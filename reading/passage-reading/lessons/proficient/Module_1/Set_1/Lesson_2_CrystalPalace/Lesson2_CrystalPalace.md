### Phase 1: Pre-Reading & Activation (15 min)

**Mystery Quote hook (board-dependent, 5 min):** Write this sentence on the board with no source and no date:

> "The arrangements for the admission of the shilling public have answered every expectation."

Ask: "Who wrote this, and what were they worried about?" Collect four or five guesses on the board exactly as
students give them, including the disagreements. Do not confirm anything. After Phase 2's reading, return to the
board and check each guess against Paragraph [D], where the sentence sits, and against Paragraph [G], where
someone who was actually there that day describes the same arrangements. The check only works because the
guesses went up first.

**Skill Spotlight (2 min):** "Today we're practicing describing a place in a chosen register, and noticing how
much of a reader's impression comes from register rather than from facts. By the end you'll be able to describe
one thing twice, in two registers, with the facts unchanged both times."

**Target vocabulary (6 min):** Pre-teach these 6 words using in-context paragraph matching. Show each word's own
sentence from the text, cold, and let students propose a meaning before you confirm it.

| Word | Paragraph | Student-friendly definition |
|---|---|---|
| girder | [B] | a long, heavy beam of iron or steel that carries the weight of a structure |
| glazing | [B] | the glass fitted into a building, and the work of fitting it |
| transept | [C] | the tall part of a long building that crosses it, making the shape of a cross |
| vault | [C] | a high curved ceiling or roof |
| throng | [G] | a large, tightly packed crowd |
| ventilation | [H] | the movement of fresh air through a building |

**Idiom and register spotlight (2 min):** Four expressions, on top of the vocabulary budget. Students may guess
first and then confirm against the gloss; the gloss never disappears (0.4).

- **"in no small measure"** [B] - transparent, formal register. To a large degree. Note that the writer chooses
  a double negative where "greatly" would do, and ask what that choice signals about the document.
- **"leaves nothing to be desired"** [D] - **opaque, glossed:** it is perfect, nothing about it could be
  improved. A fixed formal phrase; it does not mean nothing is wanted.
- **"packed to the rafters"** [G] - **opaque, glossed:** completely full of people, right up to the roof beams.
  Ask students what is odd about using it inside a building made of glass.
- **"at close quarters"** [G] - transparent, formal. Very near to other people, with little space between.

### Phase 2: Text Engagement & Strategy Execution (35 min)

**Strategy: Reciprocal Teaching.** Groups of four, one role each, working both texts in two passes: Text 1 (15
min), then Text 2 (12 min), then a whole-class comparison of what the four roles produced (8 min). Roles rotate
between the two passes, so nobody holds the same role twice.

| Role | What this student does, in their own words, out loud to the group |
|---|---|
| Summarizer | After each paragraph, give the paragraph in one sentence. Use the paragraph letter. |
| Questioner | Ask the group one question the paragraph answers and one it does not. |
| Clarifier | Name any word, number, or reference the group cannot decode, and say how the group settled it (context, footnote, or the vocabulary list). |
| Predictor | Before the next paragraph, say what you expect it to describe and why. Check yourself afterwards. |

Organizer for each group, filled as they go: one row per paragraph, four columns headed Summary, Questions,
Clarified, Prediction. No round-robin reading; the texts are read silently within each group's turn-taking.

**ANCHOR TEXT SET (Level 7, 686 words total, 8 paragraphs)**

**TEXT 1 (352 words) - from the official catalogue of the Exhibition¹, in the section describing the building itself**

> **THE EXHIBITION BUILDING IN HYDE PARK**
>
> [A] The Building erected for the Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations occupies a site in Hyde
> Park of some nineteen acres, and measures one thousand eight hundred and fifty-one feet in length, a figure
> chosen to answer to the year. It was raised in under nine months, upon a design by Mr. Joseph Paxton, whose
> occupation until lately was the care of a great garden. The Visitor approaching from the south will observe
> that the whole presents, from every side, one uninterrupted surface of glass.
>
> [B] The materials are iron, timber, and glass, and nothing else of consequence. Some three thousand columns
> and two thousand three hundred girders carry the structure, every one cast to a common pattern, so that no
> member is unlike its neighbour. The glazing, of near nine hundred thousand square feet, was set in a ridged
> and furrowed system of the designer's own devising, which admits the light in no small measure while carrying
> the rain away in gutters formed within the sash-bars themselves. The economy of the arrangement is not the
> least of its merits.
>
> [C] At the centre the Building is crossed by a transept, whose vault rises to one hundred and eight feet, and
> which encloses entire three elms of the Park that it was thought improper to fell. Beneath them stands the
> crystal fountain. It is here that the Visitor first apprehends the whole extent of the interior, the eye being
> carried down the nave in either direction until the further columns are lost in a bluish haze.
>
> [D] Of the arrangements for the public, little need be said. The Exhibition opened on the first of May and
> will close in October, and the reduced admission of one shilling² has brought a class of visitor whose
> conduct leaves nothing to be desired. The refreshment courts are commodious. On certain days the pressure
> within the transept has been considerable, and the temperature under so much glass is, in warm weather, a
> subject to which attention has been given. It is believed that the arrangements for the admission of the
> shilling public have answered every expectation.

**TEXT 2 (334 words) - a private diary, Saturday 12 July 1851, by a visitor up from Manchester for the week**

> [E] To the Exhibition at last, on a shilling day. I had read the descriptions and thought them overdone. They
> are not overdone. You come up out of the park and the whole side of it is glass, no stone, nothing to tell you
> where the building begins. J. says it is a greenhouse and he is right, and it is the finest thing I ever stood in front of.
>
> [F] Inside, the light is like nothing indoors. It comes from every direction at once and casts no proper
> shadow, and everything under it, the machines, the shawls, the people, looks slightly unlike itself. The
> girders overhead are painted in blue and red and yellow, which I did not expect and did not care for at first. By eleven the transept was packed to the rafters, and the crystal fountain was a rumour rather
> than a sight, being three deep in bonnets on every side.
>
> [G] What the catalogue does not tell you is how it is to be in a throng of that size at close quarters, under
> glass, in July. The heat by noon was very great, and the canvas they have stretched outside the roof helps
> less than they suppose. I saw two women taken out faint at the north end, and a good deal of quiet
> determination on the faces of the rest, who had paid their shilling and were not going to be got
> out before they had seen the Koh-i-Noor.
>
> [H] Of the exhibits I saw perhaps a fifth, and understood a third of those. The machinery in motion at the
> west end is the thing I shall remember: shafts and belts running the length of a room bigger than our chapel,
> and printing and spinning going forward in front of you. The ventilation there was better, the crowd thinner,
> and nobody hurrying you along. I came out at four with my feet aching and my head full, and stood a while
> looking back at it before I went for the omnibus.

---

> ¹ The Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations ran in Hyde Park from 1 May to 15 October 1851
> and drew about six million visits. The building was taken down afterwards and rebuilt, enlarged, at Sydenham
> in south London.
>
> ² Season tickets and the early five-shilling days priced most people out. From late May a shilling admitted a
> visitor on most weekdays, and it was the shilling days that carried the bulk of the six million.

### Phase 1: Text Re-Engagement & Vocab Warm-Up (15 min)

**Partner re-scan (3 min):** in pairs, find the three places where both texts describe the same thing (the
light, the crowd in the transept, the exhibits). Read out one sentence from each text for one of the three and
say which paragraph letters you used.

**Vocabulary Refresher Text (8 min).** A different building entirely, in a different century. Read it, then do
the task under it.

> **RENFIELD STREET STATION: WHAT THE ROOF WORKS INVOLVE**
>
> [A] The station roof has not been fully overhauled since 1961, and the work now beginning will run for
> fourteen months. The three main spans are carried on riveted iron girders that are, by survey, sound; the
> problem is everything fixed to them. Most of the original glazing was replaced with corrugated sheeting after
> a storm in the 1980s, which is why the concourse is darker now than any photograph of it before that date.
>
> [B] Contractors will strip the sheeting span by span and reglaze in laminated panels to the current standard.
> Working at night where possible, they will keep two of the three spans open at all times, so the concourse
> below stays usable even at the evening peak, when the throng between the barriers and the food court is at its
> heaviest.
>
> [C] The curved vault over the eastern entrance is a separate case. It is listed, its ironwork is original, and
> every replacement pane must match the tint of the surviving glass, which is being made to order. That work
> alone accounts for four months of the programme and rather more than a third of its cost.
>
> [D] Passengers should expect noise, hoardings, and a reduced seating area near platform 4. Ventilation at
> concourse level will be improved as part of the same contract, which those who use the station in August will
> appreciate. Full details are posted at the travel centre and on the operator's website.

**Task (3-4 min, light):** find and circle the five target words that appear in this notice. Then tell your
partner, in one sentence, what the notice says will be better afterwards and what it will cost passengers in the
meantime.
**Answer note:** girders [A], glazing and reglaze [A]/[B], throng [B], vault [C], ventilation [D]. Transept does
not appear, which is the point: it belongs to a cross-shaped building. The one-sentence answer is some version
of "more light and better air afterwards, fourteen months of noise and less seating in the meantime."

**Spoken Vocabulary Mastery (4 min):** answer out loud, in a full sentence, to a partner.
1. Describe a building you know that has a lot of glazing. What does the light inside it feel like?
2. Where have you been in a throng of people? What did you notice first, and what did you stop noticing?
3. A room with bad ventilation: describe it without using the words "hot" or "stuffy."

### Phase 2: Collaborative Multi-Level Investigation (30 min)

**Collaborative Evidence Matrix.** Mixed-ability groups of 4, one student per task Level. Each student completes
their own cell alone first (12 min), then the group shares orally and combines (10 min), then agrees the one
line it will carry into Phase 3 (8 min).

| Task Level | Cell prompt |
|---|---|
| 5 | Text 1 describes the building in a fixed order. Write its four paragraphs as four short phrases in that order. Then write one word from Text 1 that shows the writer is proud of the building, although the writer never says so. |
| 6 | Take Paragraph [B] and Paragraph [D] of Text 1. For each, write one phrase that is plain description and one that is the writer signalling an opinion. Then write the exact phrase where [D] first turns hedged. |
| 7 | Both texts describe the same three things: the light, the crowd in the transept, and the exhibits. Fill one row per subject with each text's own words. Then write one sentence naming something Text 1 wants its reader to believe that it never states. |
| 8 | Track Text 1's register from [A] to [D]: where does it move from measurement to celebration, and where from celebration to defence? Name the place where its literal description and its real attitude pull apart, then say in one sentence how a reader in 1851 who could not afford to go would take that passage. |

**Answer note (Level 5):** [A] the site and the size, [B] the materials, [C] the transept and the view down the
nave, [D] the public arrangements. Proud words include "uninterrupted," "not the least of its merits," and
"apprehends the whole extent."
**Answer note (Level 6):** [B] plain: "Some three thousand columns and two thousand three hundred girders";
signalled: "The economy of the arrangement is not the least of its merits." [D] plain: "The Exhibition opened on
the first of May"; signalled: "leaves nothing to be desired." The hedge turns at "On certain days."
**Answer note (Level 7):** light: [B] "admits the light in no small measure" against [F] "casts no proper shadow
... everything looks slightly unlike itself." Crowd: [D] "the pressure within the transept has been
considerable" against [F]/[G] "packed to the rafters," "three deep in bonnets," two women "taken out faint."
Exhibits: [C] the fountain and the view down the nave against [H] "perhaps a fifth, and understood a third of
those." Unstated: that the building is a success on every count, so crowding and heat can be reported as
matters that have received attention rather than as experiences anyone had.
**Answer note (Level 8):** [A] measurement, [B]-[C] celebration, [D] defence. The divergence is in [D]: a
paragraph that says "little need be said" says a great deal, and its praise of shilling visitors' "conduct"
admits the doubt it is denying. A reader in 1851 who could not afford to go reads it as reassurance that the
Exhibition is orderly and open to all, and would not hear the anxiety underneath, nor learn that on a hot
Saturday the transept was impassable.

### Phase 3: Structured Oral Output Debate & Synthesis (30 min) - Jigsaw Expert Panels

**Format.** Three expert groups (8 min), then remixed panels of three, one expert from each group, reporting to
each other and to the board (17 min), then synthesis (5 min). Every student is active throughout; nobody
listens without a job.

**Expert groups, one shared subject each, both texts:**
- **Group 1, the light:** Paragraphs [B], [C], [F].
- **Group 2, the crowd in the transept:** Paragraphs [D], [F], [G].
- **Group 3, the exhibits:** Paragraphs [C], [D], [H].

**Rotated prompts, one per group (Quality Standards §D7):**
1. (Group 1) Which text made you see the light better, and which sentence did it?
2. (Group 2) Describe the transept at noon so that both texts would accept your description.
3. (Group 3) Text 2 saw a fifth of the exhibits. Does that make it a worse description of the Exhibition, or a
   better one?

In the remixed panels, each expert takes their prompt to the new group in turn, so all three prompts are worked
by every student, and each panel sends one line to the board.

**Sentence stems by task Level:**
- **Level 5:** "Text 1 describes ___ first, then ___." / "The word ___ shows the writer ___."
- **Level 6:** "This part is description and this part is opinion, because ___." / "The writing changes at ___,
  from ___ to ___."
- **Level 7:** "Both describe ___, but Text 1 makes it sound ___ and Text 2 makes it sound ___." / "Text 1 needs
  its reader to believe ___, which is why it ___."
- **Level 8:** "The surface here is ___; the work it is doing is ___." / "A reader who ___ would take this as
  ___, and would miss ___."

**Differentiated participation (Level 5, per 0.8):** in the expert group the Level 5 student is the recorder,
writing the group's two chosen quotations, and rehearses one sentence with a neighbour before the remix. They
speak from that rehearsed sentence in the panel. This is their version of the group task, not an extra
requirement.

**Foundation Support (per Quality Standards §B, if a student is working below Level 5):** they match printed
phrases from the packet to the text each came from and hold up the one their panel reaches, rather than taking
a spoken turn. Nothing here needs the teacher to prepare anything.

**Board-dependent moment (Phase 3, satisfies Section 0.7):** as each remixed panel finishes, it reports one line
to the board under its subject heading (LIGHT, CROWD, EXHIBITS). The board ends holding a class description of
the building that no single panel produced, built from three separate conversations.

**Group Synthesis and Closing Transfer Check (5 min).** Ask which of the three board headings the two texts
disagreed about most, and why that is the one.

Then the Closing Transfer Check, oral, in pairs, nothing collected: **describe a drink you buy regularly, twice.
First the way the people who sell it would describe it. Then the way you would describe it to a friend who is
deciding whether to order one.** Same drink, same facts, two registers. Cold-call two or three pairs. Students
describe from what they know; nothing is brought to class.
