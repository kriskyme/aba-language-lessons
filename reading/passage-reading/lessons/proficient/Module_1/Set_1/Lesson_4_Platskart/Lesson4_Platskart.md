**Genre:** Literary short-story excerpts | **Reading Strategy:** Think-Aloud Modeling

**Vocabulary Theme:** Rail travel, confinement and motion (berth, aisle, jolt, threadbare, stale, boiler)
**Topic:** A third-class platskart carriage on the Trans-Siberian Railway, described by two narrators making the same journey

**Fictional-subject note:** the carriage is real and so is its arrangement. Platskart is open third class: 54

are invented**, as are the two stories these excerpts come from; no statement here is attributed to a real,
named individual, and no real traveller's published account has been drawn on.

**Note on the anchor:** the anchor is a **paired text set**, per this band's Level 7 objective and the approved
plan. Paragraphs run [A]-[D] in Text 1 and [E]-[H] in Text 2 as one continuous lettered sequence. Levels 5 and 6

Take reports round the class and build both columns on the board. The L column stays empty until Day 2 Phase 1,
which is where it is closed.

**Skill Spotlight (2 min):** "Today we're practicing describing a place by describing the people in it, and
noticing what a describer's own position does to what they see. By the end you'll be able to describe something
everybody around you is experiencing at the same time, in a way that says where you are standing."

**Target vocabulary (6 min):** Pre-teach these 6 words using in-context paragraph matching. Show each word's own
sentence from the text, cold, and let students propose a meaning before you confirm it.

| Word | Paragraph | Student-friendly definition |
|---|---|---|
| berth | [A] | a bed or bunk on a train or ship |
| aisle | [B] | the long open passage between seats or bunks that people walk down |
| jolt | [C] | a sudden rough movement or shake |
| threadbare | [C] | worn so thin with use that the threads show |
| stale | [C] | of air, used up and unpleasant from having been breathed too long in one space |
| boiler | [B] | the fixed water heater at the end of a carriage, giving hot water at any hour |

**Idiom and register spotlight (2 min):** Four expressions, on top of the vocabulary budget. Students may guess
first and then confirm against the gloss; the gloss never disappears (0.4).

- **"the small hours"** [C] - **opaque, glossed:** the hours just after midnight, one and two and three in the
  morning. Nothing to do with size.
- **"cheek by jowl"** [D] - **opaque, glossed:** packed very close together, physically almost touching. A
  "jowl" is the lower part of the cheek, which is the whole of the image.
- **"keep myself to myself"** [D] - transparent, register-marked. To stay private and not seek company. Ask
  what it tells you about the speaker that they say it approvingly.
- **"make the best of it"** [G] - transparent. To accept a situation you cannot change and get what you can
  from it.

**Strategy: Think-Aloud Modeling.** Read both excerpts aloud to the class. At each `[THINK-ALOUD: ...]` marker,

whether students followed. Students listen and jot one word or phrase in the margin capturing your reasoning.
Read Text 1 (15 min), then Text 2 (13 min), then take margin notes from the class and compare them (7 min).

**ANCHOR TEXT SET (Level 7, 683 words total, 8 paragraphs)**

**TEXT 1 - from "The Fourth Morning," a short story. First narrator, travelling east from Moscow.**

> [A] The carriage was fifty-four berths in one undivided space, bays of four down one side and single bunks
> beneath the windows on the other, and I had been given an upper. [THINK-ALOUD: the narrator gives me the
> layout before anything else, like a floor plan, and I notice they say "given," as though the berth happened to
> them.] From up there you could see the whole length of it at once: boots and plastic bags and a child's coat
> hung on a hook. It was a hundred small arrangements of other people's lives, laid end to end. I had wanted
> exactly this, and had said so in the booking office when the woman asked twice whether I would prefer a
> compartment.
>
> [B] By the second evening I had the geography of the place. The boiler at the far end gave hot water at any
> hour, and a queue formed there around nine with cups and paper packets. The aisle ran the length of the train
> as a kind of public street, and everybody used it. The woman opposite folded her bedding along it, two boys
> ran it end to end until somebody stopped them, and the attendant came through twice a night to check tickets
> and say something short to whoever was still awake.
>
> [C] Sleeping was a question of getting used to the jolt. The train took it up through the joins in the rail
> and passed it into the mattress, which was thin, and the blanket, which was threadbare and smelled of the
> depot. [THINK-ALOUD: three things are being described here, but every one of them is a surface between the
> narrator and other people, and I want to see whether that keeps happening.] In the small hours the air turned
> stale and warm, forty people breathing inside one tube of steel. I lay awake listening to a man two bays down
> who talked in his sleep in a language I could not follow.
>
> [D] I would recommend it to anybody. You are cheek by jowl with strangers for four days and you learn the
> manners of it quickly: you do not look at what people are eating, you fold your bedding in the morning, you
> take your turn at the boiler. I kept myself to myself for most of it, which suited me well enough. I got down
> at Irkutsk with perhaps nine words of Russian and the faces of everybody in my half of the carriage.

**TEXT 2 - from "Tayshet," a short story. Second narrator, on the same class of carriage, going home.**

> [E] I take platskart because it is the cheapest and because you can see what is coming¹. In a compartment you
> are shut in with three people and whoever they turn out to be is what you have for two days. Here there are
> fifty-four of us, and if a bay is bad you go and sit somewhere else for an hour. My berth was a lower side
> one, which folds down into a table in the morning, so I had the table, and so did anybody who wanted it.
>
> [F] The aisle is a street, and everybody is learned from it. There is the man getting off at Tayshet, the two
> sisters with the card game, and the attendant² who has worked this run for nine years and knows which window
> will open. [THINK-ALOUD: this narrator names people. The first one described boots and bags in the same
> space, and I am beginning to think the two carriages are identical and the two narrators are not.] Somebody's
> grandmother had brought food for a week and had given most of it away by the second day, which is ordinary and which nobody remarked on.
> By the third the bay across from mine was eating together and had stopped asking whose bread was whose.
>
> [G] The jolt is the same for everyone, and so is the boiler. What differs is what a person does with the
> night. The berths are hard, the blankets are threadbare, and by two in the morning the air is stale enough to
> wake you. You open the window at the aisle end for a minute and close it before anybody complains. You make
> the best of it, and I have slept badly on this train for eleven years and would not travel any other way.
>
> [H] Foreigners are easy to spot, and I say that without unkindness: they take the upper berths, they
> photograph the boiler, and they keep to themselves in a carriage that does not really allow it. Then somebody
> hands them tea, and by Krasnoyarsk they are eating with the bay and trying out words. What they describe
> afterwards is the carriage; what they will remember is the four people they ate with.

>
> ² Each carriage travels with one or two attendants, who check tickets, hand out bedding, and keep the boiler

**Collaborative Evidence Matrix.** Mixed-ability groups of 4, one student per task Level. Each student completes
their own cell alone first (12 min), then the group shares orally and combines (10 min), then agrees the one
line it takes into the Town Hall (8 min).

| Task Level | Cell prompt |
|---|---|
| 5 | Text 1 describes the carriage in a fixed order. Write what each of the four paragraphs is about, in four short phrases. Then write one word Text 1 uses that shows how the narrator feels about the other passengers, although the narrator never says it. |
| 6 | Take Paragraph [B] and Paragraph [D] of Text 1. For each, write one phrase that is plain description and one that is the narrator signalling an attitude. Then write the exact phrase where [D] turns from description to verdict. |
| 7 | Both texts describe the same three things: the bunks, the aisle, and the night hours. Fill one row per subject with each narrator's own words. Then write one sentence naming an assumption Text 1's narrator makes that Text 1 never states. |
| 8 | Track Text 1's narrative distance from [A] to [D]: where is the narrator above the carriage, where inside it, and where addressing the reader? **Answer note (Level 5):** [A] the layout and the berth, [B] the boiler and the aisle, [C] the night, [D] the
verdict. Words showing feeling include "arrangements," "strangers," and "faces."
**Answer note (Level 6):** [B] plain: "The boiler at the far end gave hot water at any hour"; signalled: "a kind
of public street." [D] plain: "you fold your bedding in the morning"; signalled: "which suited me well enough."
The turn is at "I would recommend it to anybody."
**Answer note (Level 7):** bunks: [A] "I had been given an upper," a vantage point, against [E] "My berth was a
lower side one, which folds down into a table," a thing shared with others. Aisle: [B] "a kind of public street"
watched from above against [F] "a street, and everybody is learned from it." Nights: [C] "forty people breathing
inside one tube of steel" against [G] "What differs is what a person does with the night." Unstated assumption:
that being in the carriage is the same as being among the people in it, so four days of proximity can be
reported as four days of company.
**Answer note (Level 8):** [A] above it, looking down the length; [B]-[C] inside it, lying awake; [D] turned
outward to the reader. The divergence is in [D], where every manner named is a way of not engaging and the
narrator's own summary of success is "I kept myself to myself." A reader who has only made this journey in a
two-berth compartment reads [D] as proof that the open carriage is the sociable choice, and hears the word
"strangers" as warmth rather than as an accurate description of who those people still were on the fourth day.

**Board-dependent moment (Phase 2, satisfies Section 0.7):** build one three-row organizer on the board, headed
THE BUNKS, THE AISLE, THE NIGHT, with a column per narrator, filled as each group reports its row. No group has

### Phase 3: Structured Oral Output Debate & Synthesis (30 min) - Town Hall Role-Play

**Format.** The class is a public meeting about what a rail company should put in its description of platskart
travel. Tables of four, every student holding a role, three prompts split across the tables so that
simultaneous conversations are not identical; each table reports one claim to the board when its window closes.

**Roles (every seat has one):**
- **Two "first narrator" seats per table:** argue from Text 1's experience, using Text 1's own phrases.
- **Two "second narrator" seats per table:** argue from Text 2's, using Text 2's own phrases.
- Each table also nominates a **recorder**, which is the Level 5 student's role (see below), who reads the
  table's agreed line to the board.

**Prompts, split across tables:**
1. (Tables 1-2) Describe the night hours honestly enough that nobody is surprised, and attractively enough that
   somebody still books it.
2. (Tables 3-4) The company wants one photograph. Describe what should be in it, and say which narrator would
   agree with you.
3. (Any table finishing early, and used for the whole-class round) Text 2 says foreigners describe the carriage
   afterwards. Describe what the other thing is, in one sentence.

**Sentence stems by task Level:**
- **Level 5:** "Text ___ describes the ___ as ___." / "The word ___ shows the narrator ___."
- **Level 6:** "This is description and this is attitude, because ___." / "The writing turns at ___, from ___ to
  ___."
- **Level 7:** "Both describe ___, but for one it is ___ and for the other it is ___." / "Text 1 assumes ___,
  which it never says."
- **Level 8:** "The paragraph says ___ while describing ___." / "A reader who ___ would take that as ___, and
  would miss ___."

**Differentiated participation (Level 5, per 0.8):** the Level 5 student is their table's recorder, which is a
listening and tracking role for the discussion itself, and rehearses the table's reporting line with a neighbour
before reading it out. They contribute one prepared sentence at a natural pause rather than holding a live
argumentative turn.

**Foundation Support (per Quality Standards §B, if a student is working below Level 5):** they match printed
phrases from the packet to the narrator who said each, and hold up the phrase their table is discussing rather
than taking a spoken turn. Nothing here needs the teacher to prepare anything.

**Group Synthesis and Closing Transfer Check (5 min).** Read the board's three rows and ask which row a
prospective passenger would most want to have read beforehand.

Then the Closing Transfer Check, oral, in pairs, nothing collected: **describe the weather outside right now,
twice. First as somebody who has been out in it all morning. Then as somebody who has been indoors and is about
to go out.** Same weather, same facts, two positions. Cold-call two or three pairs. Nobody goes outside and
nothing is brought to class.
