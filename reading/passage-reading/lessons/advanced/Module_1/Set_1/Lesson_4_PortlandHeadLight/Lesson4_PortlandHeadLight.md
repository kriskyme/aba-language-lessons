# Module 1 (Describing) - Advanced Band - Lesson 4: Keeping the Light

**Module:** 1, Describing | **Band:** Advanced (Levels 5-6, B2-B2+) | **Anchor text calibrated to:** Level 5
**Task Levels this lesson:** 4 (extension-down), 5 and 6 (native), 7 (extension-up)
**Genre:** Interview-profile piece | **Reading Strategy:** Reader's Theater
**Phase 1 Hook:** Four-Corner Debate | **Phase 3 Protocol:** Concentric Circles
**Vocabulary Theme:** Coastal-lighthouse life (beacon, keeper, foghorn, shoal, isolated, vigil)
**Topic:** A keeper's account of Portland Head Light, Maine
**Version:** 1.1.4.0

Generated per `Generate_Lesson_Prompt_v2.8.md`, following the approved
`Module1_Advanced_Lesson_Plan.md`, Lesson 4 row.

**Fictional-subject note:** the interview and the specific keeper quoted are fictional. Portland Head Light is a
real, historic lighthouse in Cape Elizabeth, Maine; this piece portrays a composite account of what its civilian
keepers' daily duties were like before the light was automated and its live-in keeper position ended, matching
well-documented historical practice. No quote here is attributed to a real, identifiable individual.

### Phase 1: Pre-Reading & Activation (15 min)

**Four-Corner Debate hook (board-dependent, 5 min):** Post this statement where all four corners of the room can
see it: "A machine can do a lighthouse keeper's job just as well as a person." Label the four corners Strongly
Agree, Agree, Disagree, Strongly Disagree. Students move to the corner matching their view. Build a live tally on
the board as each corner is counted (do not pre-fill any numbers). After reading, return to the board and check
which corner's reasoning comes closest to what the keeper in the interview actually says.

**Skill Spotlight (2 min):** "Today we're practicing describing a job by comparing how it used to be done to how
it's done now, and giving a clear reason for the comparison, the way the keeper compares her nightly routine to
what a machine does today. We're also going to practice spotting the exact words the keeper uses to reveal her
feelings, even when she never states them directly."

**Target vocabulary (6 min):** Pre-teach these 6 words using in-context paragraph matching (show each word's
sentence from the text, cold, and have students guess the meaning from context before confirming):

| Word | Paragraph | Student-friendly definition |
|---|---|---|
| beacon | [A] | a light used to guide or warn ships |
| foghorn | [B] | a loud horn that warns ships when fog blocks a lighthouse's light |
| keeper | [B] | the person responsible for operating and maintaining a lighthouse |
| shoal | [B] | a shallow, rocky area of water that is dangerous for ships |
| isolated | [C] | far away from other people, alone for long periods |
| vigil | [C] | a period of staying alert and watchful, especially at night |

**Idiom/Slang Spotlight (2 min):** The text uses two expressions worth flagging before reading:
- "kept watch" [B] - a transparent chunk: gloss it lightly. "If you 'keep watch,' you stay alert and pay close
  attention, especially to keep something safe."
- "in the dead of night" [C] - an opaque idiom: gloss it directly rather than asking students to infer it. "'In
  the dead of night' means in the middle of the night, when everything is dark and quiet."

### Phase 2: Text Engagement & Strategy Execution (35 min)

**Strategy: Reader's Theater.** Pair students up and assign one partner the INTERVIEWER role and the other the
KEEPER role. At each `[REHEARSAL CUE: ...]` marker, pause and practice that line's delivery quietly together
before continuing, performing the stage directions in parentheses as brief actions rather than reading them
aloud as dialogue. After one full silent read-through and one paired rehearsal, 2-3 pairs perform the script for
the class.

**Anchor Text (Level 5, 454 words of dialogue, 6 paragraphs)** - word count counts only the keeper's and
interviewer's spoken lines, matching how a script's length is conventionally measured; speaker labels, stage
directions, and rehearsal cues are formatting overhead, not prose content, the same way paragraph letters aren't
counted.

> **Keeping the Light: An Evening with Portland Head Light's Keeper**
>
> [A] *(The interviewer stands on the rocky point at Cape Elizabeth, Maine, as the tower's lamp begins to glow
> against the darkening sky.)*
>
> **INTERVIEWER:** Before the light was automated, what did a typical evening look like for the keeper on duty?
>
> **KEEPER:** *(She glances up at the tower.)* Most evenings began the same way. An hour before sunset, I climbed
> the tower stairs to trim the wick and polish the lens, since even a little soot could dim the beacon enough to
> matter to a ship ten miles out.
>
> [B] **INTERVIEWER:** That sounds like a lot of responsibility for one evening.
>
> **KEEPER:** It never really ended, either. Once the lamp was lit, I kept watch* through the night, checking the
> flame every few hours, since a keeper who fell asleep put every ship near the shoal in danger. On foggy nights,
> I also sounded the foghorn myself, every few seconds, sometimes for a full day at a stretch.
>
> [C] **INTERVIEWER:** Did being isolated out here ever bother you?
>
> **KEEPER:** *(She pauses, choosing her words carefully.)* [REHEARSAL CUE: pause briefly, as if deciding how much
> to share.] People ask that a lot, and the honest answer is both yes and no. The nearest town was close enough
> to see, but the job still meant long stretches alone, especially in the dead of night†, when the only company
> was the horn and the waves against the rocks. Still, the light needed a nightly vigil, and I liked being the
> person who kept it burning.
>
> [D] **INTERVIEWER:** Portland Head Light has stood here since the 1790s.¹ What do you think has stayed the same,
> now that a machine runs the light?
>
> **KEEPER:** The purpose hasn't changed. A ship far out on the water still needs a fixed point of light to steer
> by. What changed is who provides it: a sensor now turns the lamp on and off, and a machine sounds the horn when
> the fog rolls in, faster than I ever could by hand.
>
> [E] **INTERVIEWER:** Do you think something was lost when the keeper's job here ended?
>
> **KEEPER:** I think so, though I understand why the change made sense. A machine doesn't get tired and never
> misses a night by accident, but it also doesn't notice the small things a person notices: a strange color in the
> water, or a boat drifting somewhere it shouldn't be. The light still shines reliably, but nobody stands at the
> top of the tower anymore, watching the water the way a keeper once did.
>
> [F] **INTERVIEWER:** If you could say one thing to someone visiting the tower today, what would it be?
>
> **KEEPER:** *(She looks out toward the water.)* [REHEARSAL CUE: slow down for the last sentence.] I'd tell them
> to notice how steady the light looks from shore, then imagine someone climbing those stairs every evening to
> keep it that way. The light isn't just a machine now, and it wasn't just a job before either. For over two
> hundred years, someone or something has kept a promise to every ship passing this point: you will not be alone
> out there in the dark.
>
> *kept watch: stayed alert and paid close attention, especially to keep something safe
>
> †in the dead of night: in the middle of the night, when everything is dark and quiet
>
> ¹ Portland Head Light was commissioned by George Washington in 1791, making it the oldest lighthouse in Maine;
> it is now automated and sits inside Fort Williams Park in Cape Elizabeth.

**Board-dependent moment (Phase 1, satisfies Section 0.7):** After reading, return to the Four-Corner Debate tally
collected on the board during the hook. As a class, check each corner's reasoning against Paragraphs [D]-[E]:
whose original reasoning comes closest to what the keeper actually says a machine can and cannot do? This only
exists once the class's own tally is on the board to check.

### Phase 3: Day 1 Literal Comprehension & Vocab Context Check (25 min)

**Fact Finders (for the lowest task Level, Level 4, 10 min):** Answer using the text. Point to the paragraph letter
that gives you the answer.
1. What two tasks does the keeper do an hour before sunset? (Paragraph ___)
2. What does the keeper do herself on foggy nights? (Paragraph ___)
3. What does a sensor do now instead of the keeper? (Paragraph ___)
4. What are two things a machine does not notice, according to the keeper? (Paragraph ___)

**Cause & Effect set (for the native task Levels, 5 and 6, 10 min):**
1. Why did even a little soot on the lens matter to the keeper? Point to the paragraph and quote the reason.
2. Why does the keeper say a keeper who fell asleep put ships in danger?
3. What effect did automation have on the keeper's job, according to Paragraph [D]?

**Vocabulary context completion (all task Levels, 5 min):** Fill in the blank using a target word from Phase 1.
1. A ship far from shore needs to see the ______ to know where the rocks are.
2. On a foggy night, the ______ warns ships that cannot see the light.
3. Working at the lighthouse could feel ______, since the nearest town was still a walk away.
4. Every night, the keeper kept a ______, checking the flame again and again.

### Phase 1: Text Re-Engagement & Vocab Warm-Up (15 min)

**3-Minute Partner Re-Scan:** Pairs re-scan "Keeping the Light" for key details and paragraph markers (no new
reading, just relocating).

**Vocabulary Refresher Text (8-9 min, unrelated topic, recycles Day 1 vocabulary):**

> **The Bridge Keeper Who Works Alone at Night**
>
> On the river just outside town, a small drawbridge rises to let tall boats pass underneath. Few people know
> that a keeper still operates it by hand every night, alone in a narrow control room above the water. The job
> can feel isolated, since barely any boats pass after midnight, but the keeper still keeps a steady vigil,
> checking the warning beacon on each tower every hour to make sure it hasn't gone dark. When fog rolls in off
> the river, an old foghorn built into the bridge sounds every few seconds, warning boats away from the shallow
> shoal near the eastern piling. The keeper says the hardest part isn't staying awake, it's staying ready, since a
> boat can appear on the radio with almost no warning. Still, after ten years on the job, the keeper says there's
> something satisfying about being the one person a captain can count on at three in the morning.

Task (light-touch, 3-4 min): Circle the 6 target words (beacon, keeper, foghorn, shoal, isolated, vigil) wherever
they appear, then tell a partner in one sentence what the bridge keeper and the lighthouse keeper from Day 1 have
in common.

**Spoken Vocabulary Mastery (3-4 min):** Oral round-robin: each student picks one target word and uses it as
either a noun or a verb where possible ("The captain kept watch through the fog" vs. "She stood watch at the
lighthouse.").

### Phase 2: Collaborative Multi-Level Investigation (30 min)

Form groups of 4 (one student per task Level: 4, 5, 6, 7). Each student completes their own task Level below as a
prep tool (10 min), then the group combines findings into a shared Collaborative Evidence Matrix on chart paper
or a shared doc (10 min), then reports out to build the board-dependent moment below.

**Task Level 4 (extension-down, Level 4 ceiling: familiar-object description, one feature, one comparison, one
stated reason):**
Using Paragraphs [A]-[B] only: (1) Name one task the keeper does before sunset. (2) The text compares the
keeper's old job to something that runs it today. What does that comparison point to? (3) What reason does the
keeper give for climbing the tower stairs every evening?
Word bank provided: *soot, beacon, sensor, ship.* Sentence starter: "One task the keeper does is ___. The text
compares the keeper's job to ___ because ___."

This task also carries a genuine, simplified interpretive component (Respectful Tiers check): (4) Which part of
the keeper's evening job sounds more difficult to you, climbing the stairs or staying awake all night? Circle one
and complete: "I think ___ sounds more difficult because the text describes it as ___."

**Task Level 5 (native, anchor level ceiling: summarize organization, identify one evaluative word):**
(1) In 2-3 sentences, summarize how the interview is organized. (Hint: does it move from the keeper's evening
routine, to the isolation of the job, to what has changed, to a closing reflection, or some other way?) (2) Find
one word or phrase in Paragraph [E] that clearly signals the keeper's attitude toward the job ending, even though
the keeper never says "I miss it" directly. Explain what that word suggests.

**Task Level 6 (native, Level 6 ceiling: separate stated fact from evaluative language, locate a register shift):**
(1) List two facts stated directly in Paragraph [D] (no opinion words). (2) List one clearly evaluative phrase
from Paragraph [E] that shows judgment rather than fact. (3) Locate the place in Paragraph [E] where the keeper's
tone shifts from a confident, understanding claim ("I think so, though I understand why the change made sense")
to a more personal complaint about what the machine misses. Quote both halves of the shift.

**Task Level 7 (extension-up, Level 7 ceiling: compare two texts, identify an unstated interest):** See the
second text below. (1) List one detail the interview includes that the visitor placard leaves out, and one
detail the placard includes that the interview leaves out. (2) Both texts describe the same lighthouse but create
a different impression. In 2-3 sentences, describe that difference. (3) Identify one unstated interest behind the
placard's framing: what does whoever wrote it gain by describing the lighthouse the way it does, and where in
the text can you see that interest working even though it's never stated directly?

**Second comparison text for Task Level 7 only (Level 7 ceiling, promotional/informational framing, ~135 words):**

> **Welcome to Portland Head Light**
>
> Built in 1791 on the orders of President George Washington, Portland Head Light is the oldest lighthouse in
> Maine and one of the most photographed lighthouses in the country. Its automated beacon still guides ships
> safely into Casco Bay today, operated without a resident keeper. Visitors can explore the surrounding grounds at
> Fort Williams Park year-round, free of charge, and tour the keeper's former quarters, now home to a small
> museum of maritime history. The tower's dramatic setting, framed by rocky cliffs and crashing waves, has drawn
> painters, photographers, and tourists for generations. Whether you visit at sunrise or sunset, Portland Head
> Light offers one of the most memorable views on the entire Maine coastline. Plan your visit today and discover
> why this landmark remains a must-see destination.

**Board-dependent moment (Phase 2, satisfies Section 0.7):** As each group reports its task Level's findings,
build a shared "Then (Keeper) / Now (Machine)" T-chart on the board, adding one entry per group. No single
group's own notes contain the complete picture; it exists only once every task Level has reported in.

### Phase 3: Structured Oral Output Debate & Synthesis (30 min) - Concentric Circles

Form two circles of equal size, facing partners. Each pair discusses the current prompt for 2-3 minutes, then the
outer circle rotates one seat to form new pairs for the next prompt. Both circles talk at once, so every student
is active throughout; advance to the next prompt at each rotation (roughly every 8-10 minutes across the 25-30
minute window) rather than discussing one prompt the whole time:

1. Describe the keeper's job. How does it compare to what happens at the lighthouse today? Use one specific
   detail from the text to support your description.
2. Describe how the keeper talks about isolation and the nightly vigil. What specific words does she use, and
   what impression do those words create?
3. Describe what has changed and what has stayed the same about keeping ships safe. What is one reason the
   keeper gives for why the change happened?

**Sentence stems by task Level (register capped at each Level's own ceiling):**
- Level 4: "One task the keeper used to do is ___, because the text says ___."
- Level 5: "The keeper's word choice, like ___, shows her feelings because ___."
- Level 6: "The keeper states a fact when she says ___, but shifts to a more personal reflection when she says
  ___."
- Level 7: "Comparing the two texts, I noticed the [visitor placard/interview] frames this as ___, which serves
  the unstated interest of ___."

**Differentiated participation (Section 0.8, Part B):** The Level 4 student does not take an on-demand turn with
a new partner at every rotation alongside the higher task Levels. Instead: before the first rotation, they
rehearse their stem once, quietly, with a same-circle partner, and get a thumbs-up, so their first pairing is
already a repetition, not a cold performance. Alternatively, if the teacher judges the student needs more support
that day: for the first rotation, they take a listening/tracking role - a tally sheet marking how many times
their partner says "alone" versus "machine" as a reason for how the job changed - with one fully-prepared
sentence to contribute once they do rotate in.

**Foundation Support (for a student functioning below Level 4's own floor):** Provide a picture card set showing
a lighthouse tower, a keeper figure, and a sensor/machine icon. The student's Phase 3 role is physical, not
spoken: they hold up the picture card that matches whatever is currently being discussed by their partner, and,
during the Four-Corner Debate from Day 1, they can move to whichever corner a partner points them to rather than
explaining their reasoning aloud. Pair this student with a peer buddy throughout Phase 2's matrix work as well.

**Closing Transfer Check (5 min, ends Phase 3):** In pairs, every student produces one new instance of the Day 1
Skill Spotlight skill, applied to something other than the lighthouse. Sentence frame: "I would describe [something
you can see out the window] by comparing it to ___, because ___," or for Levels 5-7: "Notice how I just used the
word ___ to show how I feel about this, without saying it directly." Say it out loud to a partner. The teacher
cold-calls 2-3 pairs to share aloud. Nothing is collected or graded.

**Board-Dependent Moment Rotation note:** this lesson places its two board-dependent moments in Phase 1 (Day 1,
the Four-Corner Debate tally-and-check) and Phase 2 (Day 2, the Then/Now T-chart built from group report-backs),
rotating the required slot away from Lesson 3's Phase 1 + Phase 3 pairing and keeping the slot genuinely varied
across this Set's four cycles.
