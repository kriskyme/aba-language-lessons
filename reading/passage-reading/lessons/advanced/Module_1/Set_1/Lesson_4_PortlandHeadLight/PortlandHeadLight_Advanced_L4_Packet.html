[A] (The interviewer stands on the rocky point at Cape Elizabeth, Maine, as the tower's lamp begins to glow against the darkening sky.)

Interviewer: Before the light was automated, what did a typical evening look like for the keeper on duty?

Keeper: (She glances up at the tower.) Most evenings began the same way. An hour before sunset, I climbed the tower stairs to trim the wick and polish the lens, since even a little soot could dim the beacon enough to matter to a ship ten miles out.

[B] Interviewer: That sounds like a lot of responsibility for one evening.

Keeper: It never really ended, either. Once the lamp was lit, I kept watch through the night, checking the flame every few hours, since a keeper who fell asleep put every ship near the shoal in danger. On foggy nights, I also sounded the foghorn myself, every few seconds, sometimes for a full day at a stretch.

[C] Interviewer: Did being isolated out here ever bother you?

Keeper: (She pauses, choosing her words carefully.) People ask that a lot, and the honest answer is both yes and no. The nearest town was close enough to see, but the job still meant long stretches alone, especially in the dead of night, when the only company was the horn and the waves against the rocks. Still, the light needed a nightly vigil, and I liked being the person who kept it burning.

[D] Interviewer: Portland Head Light has stood here since the 1790s.¹ What do you think has stayed the same, now that a machine runs the light?

Keeper: The purpose hasn't changed. A ship far out on the water still needs a fixed point of light to steer by. What changed is who provides it: a sensor now turns the lamp on and off, and a machine sounds the horn when the fog rolls in, faster than I ever could by hand.

[E] Interviewer: Do you think something was lost when the keeper's job here ended?

Keeper: I think so, though I understand why the change made sense. A machine doesn't get tired and never misses a night by accident, but it also doesn't notice the small things a person notices: a strange color in the water, or a boat drifting somewhere it shouldn't be. The light still shines reliably, but nobody stands at the top of the tower anymore, watching the water the way a keeper once did.

[F] Interviewer: If you could say one thing to someone visiting the tower today, what would it be?

Keeper: (She looks out toward the water.) I'd tell them to notice how steady the light looks from shore, then imagine someone climbing those stairs every evening to keep it that way. The light isn't just a machine now, and it wasn't just a job before either. For over two hundred years, someone or something has kept a promise to every ship passing this point: you will not be alone out there in the dark.

¹ Portland Head Light was commissioned by George Washington in 1791, making it the oldest lighthouse in Maine; it is now automated and sits inside Fort Williams Park in Cape Elizabeth.

Focus on the Objective

Feature and comparison. In Paragraph [D], the keeper says "the purpose hasn't changed," but "what changed is who provides it." The feature is the light itself. The comparison is between the keeper's old routine and the sensor's new one. The reason follows right after: the machine works "faster" than a hand-lit lamp ever could.

Evaluative language. In Paragraph [E], the keeper says a machine "doesn't notice the small things a person notices." She never says "I think the change is a loss," but pointing out exactly what the machine misses is doing that work. It's a judgment, not a fact you could check with a ruler.

The Bridge Keeper Who Works Alone at Night

On the river just outside town, a small drawbridge rises to let tall boats pass underneath. Few people know that a keeper still operates it by hand every night, alone in a narrow control room above the water. The job can feel isolated, since barely any boats pass after midnight, but the keeper still keeps a steady vigil, checking the warning beacon on each tower every hour to make sure it hasn't gone dark. When fog rolls in off the river, an old foghorn built into the bridge sounds every few seconds, warning boats away from the shallow shoal near the eastern piling. The keeper says the hardest part isn't staying awake, it's staying ready, since a boat can appear on the radio with almost no warning. Still, after ten years on the job, the keeper says there's something satisfying about being the one person a captain can count on at three in the morning.

Welcome to Portland Head Light

Built in 1791 on the orders of President George Washington, Portland Head Light is the oldest lighthouse in Maine and one of the most photographed lighthouses in the country. Its automated beacon still guides ships safely into Casco Bay today, operated without a resident keeper. Visitors can explore the surrounding grounds at Fort Williams Park year-round, free of charge, and tour the keeper's former quarters, now home to a small museum of maritime history. The tower's dramatic setting, framed by rocky cliffs and crashing waves, has drawn painters, photographers, and tourists for generations. Whether you visit at sunrise or sunset, Portland Head Light offers one of the most memorable views on the entire Maine coastline.
