[A] More than a hundred years after construction began, the Basílica de la Sagrada Família still rises above Barcelona, unfinished and unmistakable. Antoni Gaudí spent over forty years on the project and never lived to see it completed. Visitors arriving from Gothic cathedrals often expect something familiar: grey stone, matching towers, a facade built to impress through scale. What they find instead is stranger, and to most eyes, far more alive.

[B] The building's three main facades tell three different stories. The Nativity Facade, the oldest, is crowded with carved doves and twisting vines, so densely arranged that some visitors call it a stone forest. Gaudí worked on this facade himself, choosing organic, flowing shapes over the straight lines of a typical Gothic cathedral. The Passion Facade, finished decades after his death, is the opposite: stark, angular figures meant to convey suffering rather than joy. The Glory Facade, still unfinished, is planned as the largest of the three.

[C] Inside, stone columns rise like actual trees, branching near the ceiling into narrower supports that spread the roof's weight outward. Gaudí based this design on trees he had studied near his childhood home. A forest canopy, he reasoned, already solves the problem a roof needs to solve: holding enormous weight using the least material. Sunlight enters through stained glass arranged by color, blue and green on one side, orange and red on the other, so the light inside shifts through the day.

[D] Construction has always moved slowly, funded mainly by ticket sales and private donations rather than government money. For decades, the building's completion date was a running joke among Barcelona residents. Engineers now use computer modeling and stone-cutting robots that Gaudí never had, which has sped up the work considerably. Even so, project leaders stay careful about naming a firm finish date, since structural challenges have delayed the basilica before and could do so again.

[E] What makes the building remarkable, according to architects who study it, is not just its size but how it fuses natural forms with engineering decades ahead of its time. Gaudí spent years building small hanging models¹ made of weighted string to calculate his stone arches. This let him design shapes a drafting table alone could never have produced. Critics who tour the site often use words like extraordinary and awe-inspiring to describe standing beneath the towers as light pours through the glass.

[F] The basilica became a UNESCO World Heritage Site² in 2005, alongside several other Gaudí works in the city. Architecture students now travel from around the world to study its structural innovations, and local guides say many visitors arrive for a quick photo and end up staying for hours, completely blown away. Whether the building will ever truly be finished, Barcelona seems, for now, content to wonder.

¹ Gaudí calculated his arches using upside-down hanging models: a string weighted at points would naturally curve into the exact shape a stone arch needed to stand without extra support, so he built the model, then flipped a photo of it right-side up to get his design.
² UNESCO is the United Nations agency that names and protects sites of major cultural importance around the world.

Focus on the Objective

Feature and comparison. In Paragraph [B], the article says the Nativity Facade is "so densely arranged that some visitors call it a stone forest." The feature is how crowded the carving is. The comparison is to a stone forest. The reason follows right after: Gaudí chose "organic, flowing shapes over the straight lines of a typical Gothic cathedral."

Evaluative language. In Paragraph [A], the article says what visitors find "is stranger, and to most eyes, far more alive." The writer never says "I like this building," but "far more alive" is doing that work. It's a judgment, not a fact you could check with a ruler.

A Neighborhood Bakery Gets a New Look

When the Ferreira family renovated their bakery last spring, they wanted the new facade to feel different from every other shop on the street. Instead of a flat, straight-lined storefront, they chose an organic design with curved wooden shelving visible through the window and a small mosaic of colored tile above the door. A local artist added light ornamentation around the sign, small carved leaves that customers now stop to look at before going in. The renovation wasn't simple. It required real structural changes to the old building, including a new support beam that had been a running joke among the family for years before anyone finally paid for it. Regulars say the new look completely blew them away.
