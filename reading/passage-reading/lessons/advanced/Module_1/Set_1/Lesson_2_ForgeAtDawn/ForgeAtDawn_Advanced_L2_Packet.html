Words to Know

1. forgea workshop with a fire, used for heating and shaping metal
2. anvila heavy iron block that metal is hammered against
3. hammera tool used to strike and shape metal; also, to strike metal with it
4. quenchto cool hot metal quickly, usually in water
5. temperthe balance of hardness and flexibility in a piece of metal
6. bladethe flat, sharp part of a knife or sword
Phrase Spotlight
trial by fire: a difficult first experience that tests whether someone can handle something hard.
get the hang of it: to start doing something more easily after practicing it.

[A] Every morning before sunrise, Kenta walks the narrow mountain path to his master's forge, a low stone building with a thatched roof and a hearth of packed clay. Inside, the air already smells of charcoal smoke and hot iron. His master, a swordsmith named Watanabe, has worked at this same anvil for over thirty years, and Kenta has been his apprentice for barely six months.

[B] Today, Watanabe begins folding a block of tamahagane¹, the traditional steel used for Japanese swords. He heats it in the coals, hammers it flat, folds it in half, and heats it again, over and over. A modern steel factory rolls sheet metal in one smooth pass, uniform from edge to edge. Watanabe's steel is different: each fold traps a thin layer of air and impurity, and each hammer strike drives it back out, so the finished blade ends up stronger, not weaker, for having been broken down so many times.

[C] Kenta's own arms ache just from watching. His master's hammer falls in a steady rhythm, never rushed, never wasted. When Kenta tries it himself, his first few strikes land unevenly, and Watanabe laughs and tells him every apprentice's trial by fire looks exactly the same. Little by little, Kenta begins to get the hang of it, though his rhythm is nowhere near his master's.

[D] By afternoon, the folded steel has become a rough blade shape, glowing orange-white. Watanabe checks its color against the daylight one final time, then plunges it into a trough of water to quench it. The forge fills with a sharp hiss and a cloud of steam. "Every quench is the same motion," he says, wiping his hands, "though no two blades ever come out quite identical." Afterward, he reheats the blade gently over low coals. This second, slower step gives it temper, the balance of hardness and flexibility that keeps a blade from shattering. A hairline crack can still appear in the coldest half-second, and then there is nothing to do but start again.

[E] Kenta watches the blade cool, its surface now a dull grey where it had glowed only minutes before. What strikes him most is not the blade itself but how carefully his master moves around it, the way someone might handle something remarkable, not something merely useful. Watanabe has never called the blade beautiful out loud, but the way he holds it, turning it slowly under the light, says more than the word ever could.

[F] Kenta still has years of practice ahead before he folds his own steel from start to finish. For now, he sweeps the ash from the hearth and banks the coals for tomorrow, already looking forward to the next early walk up the mountain.

¹ Tamahagane is a type of steel made from iron sand, smelted in a traditional Japanese furnace and prized for making sword blades.

Focus on the Objective

Feature and comparison. In Paragraph [B], the story says a modern steel factory "rolls sheet metal in one smooth pass, uniform from edge to edge." The feature is Watanabe's folding process. The comparison is to factory steel. The reason follows right after: each fold "traps a thin layer of air and impurity," and each hammer strike "drives it back out," so the blade ends up stronger.

Evaluative language. In Paragraph [E], the story says Watanabe handles the blade "the way someone might handle something remarkable, not something merely useful." The writer never says "this blade is amazing," but "remarkable" is doing that work. It's a judgment, not a fact you could check with a ruler.

A Ring for a Wedding

Every Saturday, Mei works at her grandmother's small jewelry shop, learning how to make silver rings by hand. She heats a bar of silver over a small forge until it glows soft orange, then places it on a tiny anvil and taps it gently with a jeweler's hammer. Unlike a factory machine that presses out a hundred identical rings in a minute, Mei's grandmother shapes each ring slowly, checking its curve against her finger after every few strikes. Once the ring is close to its final shape, she quenches it in a small dish of cool water, and the metal hisses softly. Afterward comes the temper, a gentle second heating that keeps the silver strong without making it brittle. Mei has learned that a blade for cutting and a ring for a finger are shaped by almost the same steps, just at very different sizes. This week, for the first time, her grandmother let her hold the hammer herself.
