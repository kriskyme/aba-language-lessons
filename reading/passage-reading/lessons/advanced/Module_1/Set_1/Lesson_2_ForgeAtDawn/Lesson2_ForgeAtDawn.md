### Phase 1: Pre-Reading & Activation (15 min)

**K-W-L Walk hook (board-dependent, 5 min):** Build a K-W-L chart live on the board. Ask: "What do you already
know about how a sword is made?" (fill the K column from student call-outs - do not pre-fill it). Then: "What do
you want to find out?" (fill the W column). Both columns exist only once students supply them. The L column is
completed after reading, at the end of Phase 3 or the start of Day 2 Phase 1.

**Skill Spotlight (2 min):** "Today we're practicing describing a craft by noticing specific steps and comparing
them to a more familiar, modern process, the way this story compares a swordsmith's hand-folded steel to a
factory's machine-rolled steel. We're also going to practice spotting the exact words a writer uses to show
respect or admiration, even when the writer never says 'this is amazing' directly."

**Target vocabulary (6 min):** Pre-teach these 6 words using in-context paragraph matching (show each word's
sentence from the text, cold, and have students guess the meaning from context before confirming):

| Word | Paragraph | Student-friendly definition |
|---|---|---|
| forge | [A] | a workshop with a fire, used for heating and shaping metal |
| anvil | [A] | a heavy iron block that metal is hammered against |
| hammer | [B] | a tool used to strike and shape metal; also, to strike metal with it |
| quench | [D] | to cool hot metal quickly, usually in water |
| temper | [D] | the balance of hardness and flexibility in a piece of metal |
| blade | [D] | the flat, sharp part of a knife or sword |

**Idiom/Slang Spotlight (2 min):** The text uses two expressions worth flagging before reading:
- "trial by fire" [C] - an opaque idiom: gloss it directly rather than asking students to infer it. "A 'trial by
  fire' is a difficult first experience that tests whether someone can handle something hard."
- "get the hang of it" [C] - a transparent chunk: something becomes easier the more you practice it. Gloss:
  "if you 'get the hang of' something, you start to do it more easily after trying it a few times."

### Phase 2: Text Engagement & Strategy Execution (35 min)

**Strategy: Reciprocal Teaching.** Groups of 4 take one role each: **Summarizer** (states the main point of each
paragraph in one sentence), **Questioner** (writes one question about each paragraph), **Clarifier** (finds any
word or phrase the group found confusing and explains it), **Predictor** (guesses what the next paragraph will
say before reading it). Provide each role its own graphic-organizer box (six boxes per paragraph, one per role)
to fill in as the group reads together.

**Anchor Text (Level 5, 450 words, 6 paragraphs)**

> **The Forge at Dawn**
>
> [A] Every morning before sunrise, Kenta walks the narrow mountain path to his master's forge, a low stone
> building with a thatched roof and a hearth of packed clay. Inside, the air already smells of charcoal smoke and
> hot iron. His master, a swordsmith named Watanabe, has worked at this same anvil for over thirty years, and
> Kenta has been his apprentice for barely six months.
>
> [B] Today, Watanabe begins folding a block of tamahagane¹, the traditional steel used for Japanese swords. He
> heats it in the coals, hammers it flat, folds it in half, and heats it again, over and over. A modern steel factory
> rolls sheet metal in one smooth pass, uniform from edge to edge. Watanabe's steel is different: each fold traps
> a thin layer of air and impurity, and each hammer strike drives it back out, so the finished blade ends up
> stronger, not weaker, for having been broken down so many times.
>
> [C] Kenta's own arms ache just from watching. His master's hammer falls in a steady rhythm, never rushed,
> never wasted. When Kenta tries it himself, his first few strikes land unevenly, and Watanabe laughs and tells
> him every apprentice's trial by fire* looks exactly the same. Little by little, Kenta begins to get the hang of
> it†, though his rhythm is nowhere near his master's.
>
> [D] By afternoon, the folded steel has become a rough blade shape, glowing orange-white. Watanabe checks
> its color against the daylight one final time, then plunges it into a trough of water to quench it. The forge fills
> with a sharp hiss and a cloud of steam. "Every quench is the same motion," he says, wiping his hands, "though
> no two blades ever come out quite identical." Afterward, he reheats the blade gently over low coals. This
> second, slower step gives it temper, the balance of hardness and flexibility that keeps a blade from
> shattering. A hairline crack can still appear in the coldest half-second, and then there is nothing to do but
> start again.
>
> [E] Kenta watches the blade cool, its surface now a dull grey where it had glowed only minutes before. What
> strikes him most is not the blade itself but how carefully his master moves around it, the way someone might
> handle something remarkable, not something merely useful. Watanabe has never called the blade beautiful out
> loud, but the way he holds it, turning it slowly under the light, says more than the word ever could.
>
> [F] Kenta still has years of practice ahead before he folds his own steel from start to finish. For now, he
> sweeps the ash from the hearth and banks the coals for tomorrow, already looking forward to the next early
> walk up the mountain.
>
> *trial by fire: a difficult first experience that tests whether someone can handle something hard
>
> †get the hang of it: to start doing something more easily after practicing it
>
> ¹ Tamahagane is a type of steel made from iron sand, smelted in a traditional Japanese furnace and prized for
> making sword blades.

**Board-dependent moment (Phase 1, satisfies Section 0.7):** After reading, return to the K-W-L chart built live
during the hook. As a class, complete the L column together: what did we learn that we did not know before
reading? This content exists only from what the class produces together, on the board, not on any handout.

### Phase 3: Day 1 Literal Comprehension & Vocab Context Check (25 min)

**Fact Finders (for the lowest task Level, Level 4, 10 min):** Answer using the text. Point to the paragraph letter
that gives you the answer.
1. What is tamahagane, and what is it used for? (Paragraph ___)
2. What does Watanabe do right before he plunges the blade into the water? (Paragraph ___)
3. What does the second, slower reheating step give the blade? (Paragraph ___)
4. What is Kenta's job for the rest of the day, after the blade cools? (Paragraph ___)

**Cause & Effect set (for the native task Levels, 5 and 6, 10 min):**
1. Why does folded steel end up stronger than factory-rolled steel? Point to the paragraph and quote the reason.
2. Why can a hairline crack appear during the quench? What effect does that have on the process?
3. Kenta's rhythm improves over the course of the day. What causes that change?

**Vocabulary context completion (all task Levels, 5 min):** Fill in the blank using a target word from Phase 1.
1. Watanabe has worked at the same ______ for over thirty years.
2. A ______ is a workshop with a fire, used for shaping metal.
3. The blade needs to be cooled quickly, so Watanabe plans to ______ it in water.
4. The gentle second heating gives the blade its ______, the balance between hardness and flexibility.

### Phase 1: Text Re-Engagement & Vocab Warm-Up (15 min)

**3-Minute Partner Re-Scan:** Pairs re-scan "The Forge at Dawn" for key details and paragraph markers (no new
reading, just relocating).

**Vocabulary Refresher Text (8-9 min, unrelated topic, recycles Day 1 vocabulary):**

> **A Ring for a Wedding**
>
> Every Saturday, Mei works at her grandmother's small jewelry shop, learning how to make silver rings by hand.
> She heats a bar of silver over a small forge until it glows soft orange, then places it on a tiny anvil and taps it
> gently with a jeweler's hammer. Unlike a factory machine that presses out a hundred identical rings in a minute,
> Mei's grandmother shapes each ring slowly, checking its curve against her finger after every few strikes. Once
> the ring is close to its final shape, she quenches it in a small dish of cool water, and the metal hisses softly.
> Afterward comes the temper, a gentle second heating that keeps the silver strong without making it brittle. Mei
> has learned that a blade for cutting and a ring for a finger are shaped by almost the same steps, just at very
> different sizes. This week, for the first time, her grandmother let her hold the hammer herself.

Task (light-touch, 3-4 min): Circle the 6 target words (forge, anvil, hammer, quench, temper, blade) wherever
they appear, then tell a partner in one sentence what a ring-maker and a swordsmith have in common.

**Spoken Vocabulary Mastery (3-4 min):** Oral round-robin: each student picks one target word and uses it as
either a noun or a verb ("Watanabe forges the blade" vs. "The forge was very hot.").

### Phase 2: Collaborative Multi-Level Investigation (30 min)

Form groups of 4 (one student per task Level: 4, 5, 6, 7). Each student completes their own task Level below as a
prep tool (10 min), then the group combines findings into a shared Collaborative Evidence Matrix on chart paper
or a shared doc (10 min), then reports out as the board-dependent moment (10 min, see below).

**Task Level 4 (extension-down, Level 4 ceiling: familiar-object description, one feature, one comparison, one
stated reason):**
Using Paragraph [B] only: (1) Name one feature of Watanabe's folding process. (2) The text compares
Watanabe's steel to something. What is it compared to? (3) What reason does the text give for why the folded
steel ends up stronger? Word bank provided: *factory steel, one smooth pass, air and impurity, hammer strike.*
Sentence starter: "One feature of Watanabe's process is ___. The text compares it to ___ because ___."

This task also carries a genuine, simplified interpretive component (Respectful Tiers check): (4) Which step
sounds more interesting to you, the folding or the quenching? Circle one and complete: "I think the ___ step
sounds more interesting because the text describes it as ___."

**Task Level 5 (native, anchor level ceiling: summarize organization, identify one evaluative word):**
(1) In 2-3 sentences, summarize how the story is organized. (Hint: does it move step by step through the day, or
some other way?) (2) Find one word in Paragraph [E] that clearly signals the writer's positive attitude toward
the blade, even though the writer never says "this is amazing." Explain what that word suggests.

**Task Level 6 (native, Level 6 ceiling: separate stated fact from evaluative language, locate a register shift):**
(1) List two facts stated directly in Paragraph [D] (no opinion words). (2) List one clearly evaluative phrase from
Paragraph [E] that shows judgment rather than fact. (3) Locate the place in Paragraph [C] where Watanabe's
tone shifts from confident reassurance ("every apprentice's trial by fire looks exactly the same") to something
closer to hedging (Kenta's rhythm being "nowhere near his master's"). Quote both halves of the shift.

**Task Level 7 (extension-up, Level 7 ceiling: compare two texts, identify an unstated interest):** See the second
text below. (1) List one detail the story includes that the museum text leaves out, and one detail the museum
text includes that the story leaves out. (2) Both texts describe the same craft but create a different impression.
In 2-3 sentences, describe that difference. (3) Identify one unstated commercial interest behind the museum
text's framing: what does the museum gain by describing the craft the way it does, and where in the text can
you see that interest working even though it's never stated directly?

**Second comparison text for Task Level 7 only (Level 7 ceiling, official/promotional framing, ~150 words):**

> **Traditional Swordsmithing: A Living Heritage Craft**
>
> Visit our newly renovated Metalworking Hall to see traditional Japanese swordsmithing demonstrated by
> certified master artisans, using techniques passed down for over a thousand years. Guests can watch as
> tamahagane steel, the same material once reserved for samurai blades, is folded, hammered, and quenched
> using only fire, water, and hand tools, no modern machinery involved. Our resident swordsmith performs live
> folding demonstrations every weekend, followed by a guided question and answer session included with
> general admission. This priceless craft survives today only through dedicated apprenticeship programs like the
> one supported by this museum's Heritage Preservation Fund. Visitors are warmly encouraged to consider a
> tax-deductible donation to help train the next generation of master swordsmiths before these skills are lost
> forever. Museum members receive early access to limited-edition replica blades crafted on site, available
> exclusively in our gift shop. Group tours and school visits can be booked online.

**Board-dependent moment (Phase 2, preferred anchor slot):** As each group reports out, build a shared
comparison T-chart on the board live: **"Traditional Folding"** on the left, **"Modern Factory Steel"** on the
right, matching Module 1's Describing skill. No single student's own task-Level sheet contains the full picture;
the board only fills in once every task Level in every group has reported.

### Phase 3: Structured Oral Output Debate & Synthesis (30 min) - Jigsaw Expert Panels

Regroup students into four Expert Panels, one per task Level, so each panel has only same-Level members first
(to compare notes), then reshuffle into mixed groups of 4 (one member from each original panel) to share
findings. Split the mixed groups across the three questions below - not every group answering the same one -
so groups compare different answers when the class synthesizes afterward:

1. Which do you think produces a better blade: Watanabe's traditional folding method, or a modern factory's
   one-pass steel? Use details from the story to support your answer.
2. Which moment in the story best shows Watanabe's skill: the folding, the hammering, or the quenching?
   Describe it and explain why you chose it.
3. The story never uses the word "beautiful," but shows Kenta noticing something remarkable about the blade.
   What detail in the story made you think the writer respects this craft? Explain.

**Sentence stems by task Level (register capped at each Level's own ceiling):**
- Level 4: "I think the [folding method/factory method] makes a better blade because the text says ___."
- Level 5: "One reason I'd choose ___ is that the writer describes ___, which shows ___."
- Level 6: "The writer's language shifts when describing ___, which suggests ___ rather than just stating fact."
- Level 7: "Comparing the two texts, I noticed the [museum text/story] frames this as ___, which serves the
  unstated interest of ___."

**Differentiated participation (Section 0.8, Part B):** The Level 4 student does not take an on-demand turn in
the mixed-group round alongside the higher task Levels. Instead: before the mixed-group round, they rehearse
their stem once, quietly, with their same-Level Expert Panel partner, and get a thumbs-up; their first
contribution in the mixed group is then already a repetition, not a cold performance. Alternatively, if the teacher
judges the student needs more support that day: during the mixed-group round, they take a listening/tracking
role - a tally sheet marking which method (traditional or factory) each speaker prefers - with one
fully-prepared sentence to contribute at a natural pause.

**Foundation Support (for a student functioning below Level 4's own floor):** Provide a picture card set showing
the forge, the hammer and anvil, and the finished blade. The student's Phase 3 role is physical, not spoken: they
hold up the picture card that matches whatever step is currently being discussed in the mixed group, and,
during the K-W-L board check from Day 1, they can point to the paragraph letter a partner names rather than
reading it aloud. Pair this student with a peer buddy throughout Phase 2's matrix work as well.

**Closing Transfer Check (5 min, ends Phase 3):** In pairs, every student produces one new instance of the Day
1 Skill Spotlight skill, applied to a piece of furniture in the room, not the forge. Sentence frame: "This
[piece of furniture] is made [by hand/by machine], which is different from [something familiar] because [reason],"
or for Levels 5-7: "Notice how I just used the word ___ to show I think this is [good/well-made], without saying it
directly." Say it out loud to a partner. The teacher cold-calls 2-3 pairs to share aloud. Nothing is collected or
graded.
