# Lesson 4: The Floor Under the Wheat

**Module:** 2, Narrating | **Band:** Advanced | **Task Levels:** 4, 5, 6, 7 | **Version:** 2.1.4.11

**Set:** Module 2, Set 1, Lesson 4 | **Genre:** feature-style newspaper article | **Reading strategy:** Close Reading with Annotation | **Phase 1 hook:** Take a Side | **Phase 3 protocol:** Rotating Partners

**Anchor calibration:** Level 5 (B2). 450 words, 6 paragraphs, average sentence length 22.5 words.

**Subject note:** the find is real and can be looked up: a Roman mosaic showing Achilles and Hector, found under
a wheat field in Rutland, England, after pottery was noticed at the surface in 2020, excavated the following
year, reburied, and given legal protection. Per the plan's guardrail, every person in this article is an unnamed
composite and no real finder, archaeologist, university, or official is named or quoted. The county heritage
notice in Task D is invented for this lesson.

### Phase 1: Pre-Reading and Activation (15 min)

**Hook: Take a Side (6 min).** The four options below are printed in the packet. Students circle one
individually, then turn to somebody who circled a different one and give each other one reason, from their seat.
Take a show of hands on each option and write the four totals on the board.

> Somebody finds something very old and very rare buried under a field they do not own. What should happen to it?
>
> 1. Dig it up and put it in a museum where people can see it.
> 2. Record it, then cover it up again and leave it where it is.
> 3. Leave it completely alone and do not dig at all.
> 4. It belongs to whoever owns the field, and it is their decision.

**Board moment (Day 1).** The four totals, plus one reason per option collected from the room as students give
them, built live on the board. It holds the class's distribution, which no individual packet holds, and it is
returned to at the end of Day 2.

**Skill Spotlight (2 min).** Say, in these words, to the whole class:

> "Today we're practicing telling what happened in order and showing why one moment mattered more than the
> others, without stopping to explain it."

**Pre-teach: Words to Know (5 min).** Five target words, matched in context to the paragraph they appear in.

| Word | Plain meaning | Find it in |
|---|---|---|
| topsoil | the top layer of earth, the part that is ploughed | [A] |
| survey | a careful look over a whole area to record what is there | [C] |
| trench | a long narrow hole dug into the ground | [C] |
| excavate | to dig something out of the ground carefully, to study it | [D] |
| fragment | a small broken piece of something | [D] |

**Idiom and Slang Spotlight (2 min).** On top of the five words above.

- **word got out** - transparent. People found out about something that had been kept quiet.
- **in the first place** - transparent. At the start, before anything else happened.
- **above board** - opaque. Open, honest, and legal, with nothing hidden. Nothing in the words tells you this, so
  gloss it directly before reading.

### Phase 2: Text Engagement and Strategy Execution (35 min)

**Strategy: Close Reading with Annotation.** Students read alone first, with a pen, marking the text in the
margin before anybody discusses anything. The key is printed in the packet and is the standing Advanced key:

| Mark | Where to put it |
|---|---|
| underline | a word you do not know |
| circle | a connector that shows time or order: *then, after, later, by the time* |
| bracket | the sentence that carries the paragraph's main idea |
| star | the moment the article treats as the most important one |
| ! | a word that tells you what the writer thinks, not what happened |

Twelve minutes of silent annotation, then eight minutes in pairs comparing marks, then fifteen minutes of
whole-class discussion working paragraph by paragraph from what students marked. The `!` mark matters
particularly in [F], which is where the article stops reporting.

## ANCHOR TEXT

### The Floor Under the Wheat

**RUTLAND, ENGLAND** - *How a piece of broken pottery in a ploughed field turned into the most unusual Roman
floor recorded in Britain.*

**[A]** The field looks like every other field on this side of the county, and for most of the last seventy years
it has grown wheat. In the summer of 2020 a man walking the edge of it picked a piece of pottery out of the
**topsoil**, turned it over, and put it in his pocket. He went back to the same corner of that field four times
that week before he telephoned anybody.

**[B]** What lay under the crop, about a metre down, was the floor of a Roman dining room, and set into it a
mosaic eleven metres long showing Achilles dragging the body of Hector(1) around the walls of Troy. No floor
telling that story had been recorded anywhere in Britain before. The room was part of a large villa, and the
mosaic was its floor, walked on at dinner.

**[C]** He reported it to the county archaeologist in the end, and a fortnight later somebody came out to look.
The first **trench** went in that autumn, two metres by one, on the strength of the pottery and an aerial
**survey** that had shown a pale rectangle in the standing crop. It reached coloured stone almost at once. The
team filled the trench in again, left the field to the winter, and told hardly anybody.

**[D]** The full dig ran for ten days the following August, in heat, with a line of people on their knees
**excavating** by hand and lifting **fragments** of burnt plaster into trays. The room had been damaged by fire
some time after it was built, and somebody had later cut a grave down through the floor. Photographs were taken
every evening, and the trays went up to the farm track at dusk. "Nothing about this
place is tidy," one of the site supervisors said. "People went on using the room long after it stopped being
grand, and that is the part I cannot stop thinking about."

**[E]** Word got out in November, when the site was given legal protection(2), which makes digging there without
permission a criminal offence and has a great deal to do with people who do not dig above board. Two questions
came back more than any others: can it be seen, and who owns it? It cannot be seen. It was recorded,
photographed, and buried again, which is what usually happens, because a mosaic is safer under a field than under
a roof nobody has yet paid for.

**[F]** The family still farm the field. They plough around a rectangle now, and the wheat above the dining room
comes up exactly the same colour as the wheat either side of it, which is how nobody noticed for seventeen
hundred years in the first place.

---

(1) Achilles and Hector: two warriors on opposite sides in the Trojan War, a story told in Greek poetry and
known all over the Roman world. Achilles kills Hector and drags his body behind a chariot.

(2) Legal protection: in Britain, an important site can be put on a national list that makes it a crime to dig
or build there without permission. The land can still be farmed.

### Phase 3: Day 1 Literal Comprehension and Vocabulary Context Check (25 min)

Four Level blocks. Every student answers only their own Level's block.

#### Task A (Level 4) - Fact Finder

*Objective: read a short narrative text and identify the sequence of events, one outcome, and the brief reason
given for that outcome.*

1. Number these five events 1 to 5 in the order the article gives them.
   - The site is given legal protection.
   - A man picks up a piece of pottery.
   - The first trench is dug.
   - The mosaic is buried again.
   - The ten-day dig takes place.

   **Answer note:** 4 [E], 1 [A], 2 [C], 5 [E], 3 [D].


2. In which season and year was the pottery found, and which paragraph tells you?

   **Answer note:** Summer 2020, Paragraph [A].

3. The mosaic was covered over again instead of being put on show. Write in one sentence the reason the article
   gives.

   **Answer note:** It is safer under a field than under a roof nobody has paid for yet.

4. Circle the three things the article says were found in or under the field, and cross out the two that were
   not: **a piece of pottery / a mosaic floor / a grave / a coin / a wall painting**

   **Answer note:** Circle pottery, the mosaic floor, the grave. No coin is mentioned; the burnt plaster in [D]
   is the near miss for a wall painting.

#### Task B (Level 5) - Sequence and Significance

*Objective: summarize the sequence of events and why they mattered, and identify at least one clearly telling
action or detail that signals how someone felt, even though the feeling is never named directly.*

Note for the teacher: this is the Module 2 shape of the native-Level set, not a Cause and Effect set, which
belongs to Module 3.

1. Summarise what happened in four sentences, from the walk in the field to the wheat growing back, using the
   paragraph letters as your guide.

   **Answer note:** [A] the pottery and the four visits. [B]-[C] what was under it, the trench, the field left
   for winter. [D] the ten days, the fire, the grave. [E]-[F] protection, reburial, the family ploughing round.

2. Paragraph [A] describes something the finder did four times. Name it, and say in one sentence what it tells
   you about how he was feeling. The article never names the feeling.

   **Answer note:** Going back to the same corner of the field before telephoning anybody. It signals doubt, or
   not trusting himself, or not wanting to be wrong in public.

3. Find one other action in the article that shows a feeling nobody names. Quote it and say whose feeling it is.

   **Answer note:** "told hardly anybody" [C], the team's caution; or "They plough around a rectangle now" [F],
   the family's. Accept the supervisor's "I cannot stop thinking about" only if the student notices it is the one
   place a feeling is named, which makes it the opposite of a signal.

4. Which single moment does the article treat as the one that mattered most? Give the paragraph letter and one
   phrase that shows you.

   **Answer note:** Accept [A], the four visits, on the grounds that the article opens there and closes on the
   same field; or [D], the grave cut through the floor, on the grounds that it is the only thing the article
   quotes anybody about. Both need a phrase.

#### Task C (Level 6) - Pacing, Reactions, and the Tone Shift

*Objective: identify specific pacing choices as heightening or minimizing significance, separate stated events
from clearly-signaled reactions, and locate at least one place where the narrative's tone visibly shifts.*

1. Find the longest span of time the article covers in the fewest words, and the shortest span it gives the most
   words to. then say in two sentences what that pairing makes the reader weigh more heavily, and why an article
   about a Roman floor might want that.

   **Answer note:** Seventeen hundred years in one clause at the end of [F]; one week in a field across three
   sentences at the start of [A]. It makes the man in the field outweigh the Romans: an article that opened on
   Achilles would be about the ancient world, and this one is about noticing. The item must not name either
   span (§C9).

2. Beside each line write E if it only reports something that happened, R if it only shows somebody's
   reaction without naming a feeling, or B if it does both at once.
   - "The first trench went in that autumn, two metres by one."
   - "told hardly anybody"
   - "The full dig ran for ten days the following August"
   - "Two questions came back more than any others."
   - "Photographs were taken every evening, and the trays went up to the farm track at dusk."
   - "It was recorded, photographed, and buried again."

   **Answer note:** E, R, E, B, E, E. Line 4 is both: the questions were asked, and the asking is how the article
   shows the public reaction. Line 2 reports no event, only what the team chose not to do, so it is the one pure R
   (§C12).

3. The article's tone shifts once, visibly. Name the paragraph where it shifts and quote the first line that
   shows the new tone.

   **Answer note:** [F]. "The family still farm the field." Up to the end of [E] the register is that of a news
   report; [F] drops the reporting voice entirely. Accept [D]'s quotation as a second candidate if the student
   argues the supervisor's voice breaks the register first, which it does, inside quotation marks.

4. The article gives the mosaic's subject, Achilles and Hector, in half of one sentence in [B] and never returns
   to it. Say in one sentence what that choice tells you about what this article is about.

   **Answer note:** It is not about the mosaic. Accept any answer that names the real subject as the finding, the
   field, or the people.

#### Task D (Level 7) - Two Accounts of the Same Discovery

*Objective: read two extended narratives covering the same or related events, critically assess how each
writer's structural choices shape what the reader is meant to conclude, and identify at least one unstated
motive or interest behind one writer's structural choices.*

Read the county heritage service's public note on the same site, printed below in full.

> ### County Heritage Service: Note on the Protected Site
> *Invented for this lesson.*
>
> **[G]** A nationally important Roman site in this county was added to the protected list in November following
> a programme of survey and controlled excavation carried out under licence by a professional team.
>
> **[H]** The site came to the service's attention through a reported surface find. Members of the public who
> notice archaeological material are asked to report it and to leave it in place. Removing or digging material
> without permission damages the record irreparably and is a criminal offence.
>
> **[I]** Following recording, the excavated area was backfilled to conserve it. Reburial is standard practice
> and is the option that best protects fragile floors of this type for the future.
>
> **[J]** The service thanks the landowners for their co-operation throughout. The location of protected sites
> is not published in detail, and the service does not respond to requests for access.

1. Both texts describe the same discovery. Find two things that both of them describe. For each one, write the
   words the article uses and the words the heritage note uses, then say in one sentence what each writer's wording
   is built to make the reader conclude.

   **Answer note:** Three are easy to find, and the student needs only two: how it was found, the excavation, and what happens to the site now;
   accept any shared subject genuinely covered by both. The note turns a man with a pocket into a procedure with a
   rule attached, ten days of hands and knees into a licensed programme, and a decision about money into standard
   practice. Look for students noticing that the note's version of the finding quietly instructs the reader while
   appearing to narrate. Neither the shared subjects nor the quotations may be printed for the student: both texts are
   on the page in full, so finding and pairing them is the Level 7 work (§C9).


2. The article says the mosaic is reburied because nobody has paid for a roof. The note says reburial is the
   option that best protects the floor. In three to four sentences, say whether these are two accounts of the
   same decision or two different claims, and what each text needs its version for.

   **Answer note:** They are compatible and not the same: one gives a cause, the other gives a justification. The
   article needs the money in view because its subject is what actually happened; the note cannot afford to
   suggest that protection is a budget outcome. The strong answers notice neither text says the other is wrong. This question is also where the task meets the Level 7 row's "identify at least one unstated motive or interest": saying what each text needs its version for names the interest neither states.

#### Vocabulary in Context (all Levels, 5 min)

Complete each sentence with one word from the bank. Two words are not needed.

**Bank: topsoil / survey / trench / excavate / fragment / plaster / floor**

1. The plough turns over only the ________, not the ground below it.
2. They dug a narrow ________ across the field to see what was under it.
3. An aerial ________ of the whole farm showed a shape in the crop.
4. It took ten days to ________ the room by hand.
5. She lifted one ________ of pottery out of the tray and held it up.

**Answer note:** topsoil, trench, survey, excavate, fragment. "Plaster" and "floor" are the padding, both used in
the article and both plausible in items 4 and 5.

### Phase 1: Text Re-Engagement and Vocabulary Warm-Up (15 min)

**Partner re-scan (3 min).** In pairs, find and say aloud: the only paragraph with a month in it, the only
paragraph where somebody speaks, and the two paragraphs that mention the wheat.

**Answer note:** [E], [D], and [A] with [F].

**Vocabulary Refresher Text (8 min).** A short unrelated text that reuses Day 1's words.

> ### Allotment Notice: The New Plots, Numbers 41 to 56
>
> **[A]** The new plots at the top end will be marked out in March. A **survey** of the whole site was done in
> January, and the sixteen plots fit, though numbers 53 and 54 are narrower than the rest and have been priced
> accordingly.
>
> **[B]** Three things to expect. A **trench** for the water pipe will be cut along the top path in the first
> week, so nobody should plant anything in that strip before April. Forty tonnes of **topsoil** are being
> delivered on the 12th, because the ground up there is thin and full of builders' rubble. Anyone who wants to
> **excavate** a pond on their own plot must ask the committee first.
>
> **[C]** Finally, please keep any **fragment** of old glass or china you turn up in the bucket by the gate rather
> than in the hedge. Somebody has been putting it in the hedge, and the committee would rather not have to say who.

**Task (light touch, 3-4 min).** In pairs, find and circle the five target words. Then answer out loud, in one
sentence: which of the three things in the second paragraph would matter most to somebody taking plot 41, and
why?

**Answer note:** Words: survey, trench, topsoil, excavate, fragment. Any answer is acceptable with a reason from
the notice. Note that this refresher carries no idiom, by design, since it is the fourth in a Set and the idiom
work is done; the five target words are the point.

**Spoken Vocabulary Mastery (3-4 min).** Round the room, one question each, answered aloud in a full sentence:
What is the oldest thing you have ever held? If you could survey one place properly, which? What would somebody
excavating this room in a thousand years find first?

### Phase 2: Collaborative Multi-Level Investigation (30 min)

Groups of four, one student per task Level. Each student completes their own cell first, then the group combines
them orally in Level order.

**Collaborative Evidence Matrix: What happened, and what the article is really about**

| Task Level | Cell prompt |
|---|---|
| Level 4 | Choose the moment you would have put in the headline, and give one reason from the text. |
| Level 5 | Name two actions the article describes without saying what anybody felt, and say what each one signals. Quote both. |
| Level 6 | Find every sentence in [F] and say what each one is doing that a news report would not do. Then say why the article saved them for the end. |
| Level 7 | Take one sentence from the heritage note and rewrite it so it keeps every fact but puts a person in it. Then say what the original was buying by leaving the person out. |

**Answer note (Level 4):** any headline choice is acceptable with a reason quoted from the text. The events run
the pottery [A], the trench [C], the winter [C], the ten-day dig [D], the fire and the grave [D], protection [E],
reburial [E], the ploughing [F]; the class fixed that order in Day 1 Phase 3, which is where this Level's
sequence component is met.
**Answer note (Level 5):** "went back to the same corner four times" [A] / doubt; "told hardly anybody" [C] /
caution; "They plough around a rectangle now" [F] / acceptance.
**Answer note (Level 6):** [F]'s three clauses give the family the last word, put the wheat back over the
mosaic, and hand the reader the reason nobody noticed. A news report would not end on the thing that did not
happen for seventeen hundred years.
**Answer note (Level 7):** "The site came to the service's attention through a reported surface find" becomes "A
man reported a piece of pottery he had found in a field." The original buys a service that notices things, and no
individual with a decision to make.

**Board moment (Day 2).** Build a two-column chart live on the board as each group reports: stated events on the
left, clearly-signalled reactions on the right, in the order the article gives them. No group has both columns,
so the chart only exists once every task Level has reported. Finish by returning to Day 1's Take a Side tally
and asking whether anybody's mark would move now.

### Phase 3: Structured Oral Output and Synthesis (30 min)

**Protocol: Rotating Partners (25 min).** Everybody talks at once, in pairs, from wherever they sit. Students
find a new partner for each of the three prompts, so no listener task is needed: every seat is speaking.

- **Prompt 1:** The article spends its first and last paragraphs on a field and its middle on a floor. If you
  were the editor, would you have run it that way?
- **Prompt 2:** The heritage note asks people who find something to leave it in place. The man in the article put
  it in his pocket and nothing was lost. Does the rule survive that?
- **Prompt 3:** Nobody can see this mosaic. Is a thing that has been recorded, photographed, and buried again
  saved or lost?

**Sentence stems by task Level:**

- **Level 4:** "First ______, then ______." / "The moment that mattered was ______ because the article says
  ______."
- **Level 5:** "What happened was ______, and it mattered because ______." / "The article never says ______, but
  ______ shows it."
- **Level 6:** "The article spends longest on ______, which makes ______ feel more important than ______." / "The
  tone changes at ______, from ______ to ______."
- **Level 7:** "The heritage note is structured to make the reader conclude ______." / "The interest behind that
  choice is ______, which the text never states."

**Differentiated participation (Level 4).** Rotating Partners already gives every student a private, low-stakes
turn rather than a public one, which is most of the differentiation. On top of that, the Level 4 student rehearses
one prepared sentence with their first partner in the two minutes before the first rotation, and opens with it
each time they change partner, so the same sentence carries them into three conversations.

**Foundation Support.** For a student working below Level 4: matching the five printed annotation marks in the
packet to five printed descriptions; pointing to the paragraph that answers a Fact Finder instead of writing it;
a two-choice circle in place of items 1 and 4 of Task A; a peer buddy for the matrix cell. In Phase 3 their role
is to hold up, to each new partner, a paragraph letter they have written on their own paper, and let the partner
say what happened there.

**Closing Transfer Check (5 min, oral only, nothing collected).** Every student, in pairs, out loud:

> "In three or four sentences, tell your partner what you did in the five minutes before you sat down today, in
> order, and make one part of it the part that mattered. Do not explain why it mattered."

Cold-call two or three pairs. The scenario is used in no other lesson of this Set.
