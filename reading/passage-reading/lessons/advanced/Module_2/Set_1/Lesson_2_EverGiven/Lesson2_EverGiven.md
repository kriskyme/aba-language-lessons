# Lesson 2: Six Days in the Canal

**Module:** 2, Narrating | **Band:** Advanced | **Task Levels:** 4, 5, 6, 7 | **Version:** 2.1.2.11

**Set:** Module 2, Set 1, Lesson 2 | **Genre:** social media post thread | **Reading strategy:** Teacher Read-Aloud with Interactive Stops | **Phase 1 hook:** Visual Inquiry | **Phase 3 protocol:** Panel Round

**Anchor calibration:** Level 5 (B2). 459 words, 7 posts, average sentence length 18.4 words.

**Subject note:** the grounding of the container ship Ever Given in the Suez Canal on 23 March 2021, the six days
of dredging and tug work, the queue of waiting ships, the vessels that turned for the Cape of Good Hope, and the
refloating on 29 March are real and can be looked up. The account writing the thread is invented, and so is
Meridian Line and its customer update in Task D. No real person, company, or official is quoted anywhere in this
lesson. Post [A] is short, as an opening post is; the follow-ups are long-form, as thread follow-ups now are.

**Image:** `Lesson2_EverGiven_Img_Hook.jpg` is used for the Phase 1 Visual Inquiry hook. Commons file title
"Suez Canal Crisis (ASTER).jpg", NASA/METI/AIST/Japan Space Systems and the U.S./Japan ASTER Science Team,
Public domain, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Suez_Canal_Crisis_(ASTER).jpg . It is a false-colour
satellite image taken on 4 April 2021 of the southern entrance to the canal, in the Gulf of Suez, where more
than a hundred ships are waiting at anchor. Credited in `Set1_Advanced_Image_Credits.md`; the packet prints no
credit.

### Phase 1: Pre-Reading and Activation (15 min)

**Hook: Visual Inquiry (5 min).** The satellite photograph is printed in the packet with this caption only: *A
satellite photograph of a stretch of sea, taken in April 2021. The colours are not the real ones.* Do not say
where it is or what is happening.

Ask, in this order, taking answers from the whole class: What are the small white marks? How many can you count
in one corner? Are they moving or still, and how can you tell? What would make that many stop in one place? Only
after the last question, name the place.

**Skill Spotlight (2 min).** Say, in these words, to the whole class:

> "Today we're practicing telling what happened in order and showing why one moment mattered more than the
> others, without stopping to explain it."

**Pre-teach: Words to Know (6 min).** Five target words, matched in context to the post they appear in.

| Word | Plain meaning | Find it in |
|---|---|---|
| aground | stuck on the bottom, where the water is too shallow for a ship | [A] |
| dredger | a machine or boat that digs sand and mud out from under the water | [C] |
| tug | a small, very powerful boat that pushes or pulls a larger one | [D] |
| convoy | a group of ships travelling through together, in order | [F] |
| backlog | work or traffic that has built up because it could not be dealt with in time | [F] |

**Idiom and Slang Spotlight (2 min).** On top of the five words above.

- **round the clock** - transparent. All day and all night without stopping.
- **hold tight** - transparent. Wait, and do not do anything yet.
- **go pear-shaped** - opaque. To go badly wrong after starting well. Nothing in the words tells you that, so
  gloss it directly before reading.

### Phase 2: Text Engagement and Strategy Execution (35 min)

**Strategy: Teacher Read-Aloud with Interactive Stops.** Read the thread aloud, one post at a time, at the pace
of somebody reading a screen out loud. Stop at each `[STOP & CHECK]`, ask the question in the bracket, take two
or three answers, and move on without confirming which is right until the post after it. The expected answers
below each stop are for the teacher and are not printed in the student packet.

## ANCHOR TEXT

### @boxship_watch, a container-shipping analyst

*A thread posted over six days in March 2021.*

**[A]** **1/7, 23 March, 08:40.** Right. A 400-metre container ship has gone **aground** in the Suez Canal,
sideways. Not alongside the bank. Sideways, bow in one wall and stern in the other. I have never typed that
sentence before. #Suez

`[STOP & CHECK 1: Ask "What does the writer mean by 'I have never typed that sentence before'? What is that
telling you about how unusual this is?"]`
**Answer note:** They have written about shipping for years and have no ready phrase for this. Accept anything
that reads the line as surprise rather than as a fact about typing.

**[B]** **2/7, 23 March, 14:10.** The wind is the first answer everyone reaches for, and there was a sandstorm
that morning, so it is not a silly one. But a ship that size does not turn across a canal because of weather
alone, and the investigation will take months. For today what matters is simpler: nothing can pass in either
direction, and about twelve per cent of the world's trade goes through this ditch. The ships already inside the
canal are stuck inside it, and the ones outside are anchoring where they can.

`[STOP & CHECK 2: Ask "The writer says the wind is 'the first answer everyone reaches for'. Are they agreeing
with it?"]`
**Answer note:** No, but they do not reject it either. They set it aside as incomplete. Take both readings now
and settle it at Post [C].

**[C]** **3/7, 24 March, 19:55.** Two **dredgers** are working on the bow, and the photograph doing the rounds
today is of one small digger on the bank, which is not the whole operation but is not a lie either. The dredgers have pulled some
twenty thousand cubic metres of sand and clay out from around the bow since yesterday. Somebody told
a reporter this would be done by tonight. It will not be done by tonight.

**[D]** **4/7, 26 March, 21:30.** Fourteen **tugs** now, working round the clock, and the queue at both ends has
passed two hundred ships. Some owners have already turned for the Cape of Good Hope(1), which adds about ten days
and a great deal of fuel to a voyage, and that is a decision you cannot take back once you have taken it. Nobody at either end knows whether tomorrow is the day it
opens, which is the worst kind of information to plan a voyage around. Hold tight is getting harder to say.

`[STOP & CHECK 3: Ask "Why does the writer call the Cape decision one 'you cannot take back'?"]`
**Answer note:** A ship that has turned cannot return to its place in the queue if the canal opens the next day.

**[E]** **5/7, 27 March, 23:05.** The spring tide(2) on Sunday night is the best chance anyone has, and if it
goes pear-shaped there we are into refloating plans that involve taking containers off, which is weeks, not days.
I have checked tide tables twice today. I have not checked tide tables twice in a day in eleven years of this
job.

`[STOP & CHECK 4: Ask "The writer tells you how many times they checked the tide tables. Why put that in?"]`
**Answer note:** It is the one place in six days where the thread says something about the writer rather than
the ship. Hold answers; this is the Level 5 task.

**[F]** **6/7, 29 March, 12:20.** She is off. Refloated on the tide this morning, six days after she went on,
and the first **convoy** is already moving north. The **backlog** is over four hundred ships and will take about
a week to clear, which is the part nobody will report, because a queue is not a photograph.

**[G]** **7/7, 29 March, 12:24.** One more thing. There were twenty-five people on that ship for those six days,
and I have read perhaps two hundred posts about her this week, including my own, and I do not think I have seen
one of them mentioned. They were not rescued and they did not go anywhere, and for six days they were simply
aboard, in the middle of the story everybody else was telling.

---

(1) Cape of Good Hope: the southern tip of Africa. Ships that cannot use the Suez Canal sail right around it,
which is a much longer route.

(2) Spring tide: a tide that rises higher than usual, which happens twice a month at the full and new moon. A
higher tide lifts a grounded ship further.

### Phase 3: Day 1 Literal Comprehension and Vocabulary Context Check (25 min)

Four Level blocks. Every student answers only their own Level's block.

#### Task A (Level 4) - Fact Finder

*Objective: read a short narrative text and identify the sequence of events, one outcome, and the brief reason
given for that outcome.*

1. Number these five events 1 to 5 in the order the thread gives them.
   - Fourteen tugs are working.
   - The ship goes aground sideways.
   - The ship is refloated.
   - Two dredgers start work on the bow.
   - Some owners turn for the Cape of Good Hope.

   **Answer note:** 3 [D], 1 [A], 5 [F], 2 [C], 4 [D]. The tugs and the Cape decision are both in [D]; accept
   either order for those two.


2. How many days passed between the ship going aground and being refloated? Which post tells you?

   **Answer note:** Six. Post [F], "six days after she went on."

3. Post [D] says some owners turned for the Cape of Good Hope. Write in one sentence the reason the post gives
   for why that choice is hard to make.

   **Answer note:** It cannot be taken back; a ship that has turned loses its place in the queue.

4. Circle the three things the thread says were used to free the ship, and cross out the two that were not:
   **dredgers / tugs / a high tide / a crane / helicopters**

   **Answer note:** Circle dredgers, tugs, a high tide. A crane is mentioned nowhere; taking containers off in
   [E] is the plan that was not needed, which makes it the strong distractor.

#### Task B (Level 5) - Sequence and Significance

*Objective: summarize the sequence of events and why they mattered, and identify at least one clearly telling
action or detail that signals how someone felt, even though the feeling is never named directly.*

Note for the teacher: this is the Module 2 shape of the native-Level set, not a Cause and Effect set, which
belongs to Module 3.

1. Summarise the six days in four sentences, using the post letters as your guide.

   **Answer note:** [A]-[B] the grounding and what it stopped. [C] the dredgers and the wrong forecast. [D] the
   tugs, the queue, the ships that left. [E]-[F] the tide, the refloating, the backlog.

2. Post [E] describes something the writer did twice in one day. Name it, and say in one sentence what it tells
   you about how they were feeling. The thread never names the feeling.

   **Answer note:** Checking the tide tables. It signals worry or dread. The second sentence, which compares the
   day against eleven years, is what makes it a signal rather than a fact.

3. Which single post does the writer treat as the one that mattered most? Give its letter and one phrase from it
   that shows you.

   **Answer note:** [G]. Accept "One more thing," "for those six days," or "I do not think I have seen one of
   them mentioned." Accept [F] if the student argues the refloating from "She is off," but ask them why [G]
   was written four minutes later.

4. In Post [C] the writer says a photograph "is not the whole operation but is not a lie either." Say in one
   sentence what they are warning the reader about.

   **Answer note:** That one striking image will stand in for six days of work that nobody photographed.

#### Task C (Level 6) - Pacing, Reactions, and the Tone Shift

*Objective: identify specific pacing choices as heightening or minimizing significance, separate stated events
from clearly-signaled reactions, and locate at least one place where the narrative's tone visibly shifts.*

1. The thread covers six days. Find the post that gives the most attention to the shortest span of time, and
   say how long that span actually is. Then say in two sentences what that choice makes the reader weigh more
   heavily, and how the timestamps help it work.

   **Answer note:** [G], four minutes, against the six days of [A] to [F]. The four minutes outweigh the six
   days; the timestamps make [G] read as something the writer could not leave unsaid rather than as a planned
   conclusion. The item must not name the post or the span: finding them is the Level 6 work (§C9).

2. Beside each line write E if it only reports something that happened, R if it only shows somebody's
   reaction without naming a feeling, or B if it does both at once.
   - "Fourteen tugs now, working round the clock"
   - "I have checked tide tables twice today."
   - "Hold tight is getting harder to say."
   - "I have never typed that sentence before."

   **Answer note:** E, B, R, R. Line 2 is both: checking the tables happened, and the checking is what signals the
   dread. Lines 3 and 4 report no event, only what the writer can and cannot bring themselves to say, so they are
   pure R (§C12).

3. The thread's tone shifts once, visibly. Name the post where it shifts and quote the first line that shows the
   new tone.

   **Answer note:** [G]. "One more thing." Everything before it is technical and dry, including the refloating;
   [G] turns to people and drops the shipping register entirely.

4. Post [B] ends on "about twelve per cent of the world's trade goes through this ditch." Say in one sentence
   what the word "ditch" is doing in a post that is otherwise measured.

   **Answer note:** It undercuts the scale it has just given, keeping the writer's authority while refusing the
   grand register. Accept any answer naming the deflation.

#### Task D (Level 7) - Two Accounts of the Same Six Days

*Objective: read two extended narratives covering the same or related events, critically assess how each
writer's structural choices shape what the reader is meant to conclude, and identify at least one unstated
motive or interest behind one writer's structural choices.*

Read the shipping line's update to its customers, printed below in full. It covers the same six days.

> ### Meridian Line: Update to Customers
> *Invented for this lesson. Posted 30 March 2021.*
>
> **[H]** We are pleased to confirm that transits through the Suez Canal resumed yesterday and that Meridian
> vessels are moving north in the first convoys. Customers with cargo on board will receive revised arrival
> windows within forty-eight hours.
>
> **[I]** The interruption followed severe weather in the canal on 23 March, which is outside the control of any
> operator. Our teams worked continuously with partners and agents throughout the period to protect customer
> cargo and to keep schedules under review.
>
> **[J]** Four Meridian vessels were rerouted via southern Africa as a precaution. This decision was taken early
> and has limited the delay our customers now face.
>
> **[K]** We thank our customers for their patience and our crews for their professionalism. Meridian Line
> remains committed to the reliability our customers expect.

1. Both texts describe the same six days. Find two things that both of them describe. For each one, write the
   words the thread uses and the words the update uses, then say in one sentence what each writer's wording
   is built to make the reader conclude.

   **Answer note:** Three are easy to find, and the student needs only two: why it happened, the six days of work, and the people on board;
   accept any shared subject genuinely covered by both. The update makes the cause weather and therefore nobody's, the
   six days a period of company effort with no uncertainty in them, and the crews a line in a thank-you list. Look for
   students naming the grammar: "the interruption followed severe weather" has no agent at all. Neither the shared
   subjects nor the quotations may be printed for the student: both texts are on the page in full, so finding and
   pairing them is the Level 7 work (§C9).


2. The thread and the update both mention the crews, in one sentence each. In three to four sentences, compare
   what each sentence is for, and say which text you would trust more about what the six days were actually
   like, and why.

   **Answer note:** The update's mention closes a list; the thread's opens a gap it admits it helped make. Either
   answer on trust is acceptable if argued: the strong ones note that the thread's own admission ("including my
   own") is also a rhetorical move. This question is also where the task meets the Level 7 row's "identify at least one unstated motive or interest": saying what each text needs its version for names the interest neither states.

#### Vocabulary in Context (all Levels, 5 min)

Complete each sentence with one word from the bank. Two words are not needed.

**Bank: aground / dredger / tug / convoy / backlog / canal / queue**

1. The ship ran ________ in shallow water and could not move.
2. A ________ pulled sand out from under the bow for two days.
3. Ships crossed in a ________, one behind the other in order.
4. Once the route opened there was a ________ of four hundred ships to clear.
5. A single ________ is not strong enough to move a ship that size.

**Answer note:** aground, dredger, convoy, backlog, tug. "Canal" and "queue" are the padding, both used in the
thread and both plausible in items 3 and 4.

### Phase 1: Text Re-Engagement and Vocabulary Warm-Up (15 min)

**Partner re-scan (3 min).** In pairs, find and say aloud: the post with the most numbers in it, the post with
the fewest, and the two posts sent on the same day.

**Answer note:** [D] most, [G] fewest, and [F] with [G] on 29 March.

**Vocabulary Refresher Text (8 min).** A short unrelated text that reuses Day 1's words.

> ### Ferry Aground on the Sandbank Again
> *A short item from an island newspaper. Nothing to do with global trade.*
>
> **[A]** The island ferry went **aground** on the sandbank outside the harbour for the third time this year on
> Tuesday evening, with forty passengers on board and no injuries. A **tug** from the boatyard pulled her clear
> at high water, five hours later.
>
> **[B]** The harbour board has asked for a **dredger** for six years. The sandbank moves every winter, and the
> channel markers have not been moved with it, which is the part nobody argues about and nobody pays for.
>
> **[C]** Tuesday's sailings were cancelled and the **backlog** cleared on Wednesday, when the boat ran a
> **convoy** of three crossings in a row. Passengers waiting on the pier were told to hold tight, which several
> of them had been told last February as well.
>
> **[D]** The board meets again in April. The ferry company says the sandbank is a matter for the harbour, and
> the harbour says the channel markers are a matter for the company, and the ferry will go aground again in
> November.

**Task (light touch, 3-4 min).** In pairs, find and circle the five target words and the one idiom. Then answer
out loud, in one sentence: what does this notice suggest will happen next winter?

**Answer note:** Words: aground, tug, dredger, backlog, convoy. Idiom: hold tight. The notice implies it will
happen again, because the cause is untouched.

**Spoken Vocabulary Mastery (3-4 min).** Round the room, one question each, answered aloud in a full sentence:
When were you last in a queue that turned into a backlog? What in this room would need a tug to move it? Where
have you seen something go aground, on screen or in life?

### Phase 2: Collaborative Multi-Level Investigation (30 min)

Groups of four, one student per task Level. Each student completes their own cell first, then the group combines
them orally in Level order.

**Collaborative Evidence Matrix: Six days, and who each post is really about**

| Task Level | Cell prompt |
|---|---|
| Level 4 | Choose the post you would most want to have read as it happened, and give one reason from the text. |
| Level 5 | For two posts, name one thing the writer states about the ship and one thing the same post lets you know about the writer, which it never states. Quote both. |
| Level 6 | Find the two posts that spend the fewest words, [A] and [G]. Say what each is doing with its shortness, and why the thread puts them at the two ends. |
| Level 7 | Take one sentence from the Meridian update and rewrite it so it keeps every fact but names an agent. Then say what the original sentence was buying by leaving the agent out. |

**Answer note (Level 4):** any post is acceptable with a reason quoted from the text. The posts run [A] aground
sideways, [B] nothing passes, [C] dredgers and the wrong forecast, [D] tugs and the queue, [E] the tide and the
fear, [F] refloated and the backlog, [G] the crew; the class fixed that order in Day 1 Phase 3, which is where
this Level's sequence component is met.
**Answer note (Level 5):** [A] "gone aground... sideways" / "I have never typed that sentence before"; [E]
"the spring tide on Sunday night is the best chance" / "I have checked tide tables twice today"; [G] "twenty-five
people" / "including my own."
**Answer note (Level 6):** [A] is short because the event outruns the language; [G] is short because it is a
correction. Together they frame six days of technical detail as something the writer walked into and out of.
**Answer note (Level 7):** "The interruption followed severe weather" becomes "Severe weather and a 400-metre
ship closed the canal," or similar. The original buys the absence of anyone who could have done otherwise.

**Board moment (Day 2).** Put the seven posts across the board as columns first, from the order the class fixed
in Day 1 Phase 3, then build the chart live as each group reports: one row per task Level. The Level 7 row is
written against the finished chart. Nothing on it is copied from a handout.

### Phase 3: Structured Oral Output and Synthesis (30 min)

**Protocol: Panel Round (25 min).** Four to six students speak in turn from their seats, using their task-Level
stems. Everybody else has the listener task below. Panel and listeners swap at each prompt changeover, at roughly
ten and twenty minutes, so every student speaks once and listens once.

**Listener task, for every seat not currently speaking:** keep a running list of the three moments a speaker
treats as the turning point of the six days. Write the post letter and three or four words. When you join the
panel, your list is the material you speak from. The Level 4 student's version of this list is their
differentiated participation, not an extra requirement.

**Prompts, rotated:**

- **Prompt 1:** Six days, four hundred ships, and the post that mattered is about twenty-five people nobody
  photographed. Was the writer right to put that last, or should it have been first?
- **Prompt 2:** The Meridian update is not false. Is it honest?
- **Prompt 3:** The thread was written as it happened and the update afterwards. How much of the difference
  between them is that, and how much is who wrote them?

**Sentence stems by task Level:**

- **Level 4:** "First ______, then ______." / "The post that mattered was ______ because it says ______."
- **Level 5:** "What happened was ______, and it mattered because ______." / "The writer never says ______, but
  ______ shows it."
- **Level 6:** "The thread spends longest on ______, which makes ______ feel more important than ______." / "The
  tone changes at ______, from ______ to ______."
- **Level 7:** "The update is structured to make the reader conclude ______." / "The interest behind that choice
  is ______, which the text never states."

**Board moment (Day 2, second instance, and the one that feeds the synthesis).** Keep a running board record of
the turning-point claims as they are actually spoken, one line per speaker, since a Panel Round has one voice at
a time and can be transcribed live. The closing synthesis reads the board back.

**Differentiated participation (Level 4).** The Level 4 student takes the listener task for the first round, with
one fully prepared sentence rehearsed with a neighbour beforehand, and contributes it at the first natural pause
rather than being called on cold.

**Foundation Support.** For a student working below Level 4: matching the seven printed post letters in the
packet to seven printed event phrases; pointing to the post that answers a Fact Finder instead of writing it; a
two-choice circle in place of items 1 and 4 of Task A; a peer buddy for the matrix cell. In Phase 3 their role is
to hold up a post letter they have written on their own paper when the panel reaches that moment, rather than
take a spoken turn.

**Closing Transfer Check (5 min, oral only, nothing collected).** Every student, in pairs, out loud:

> "In three or four sentences, tell your partner about the last thing you ate: how it got to you, in order, and
> make one moment in it the moment that mattered. Do not explain why it mattered."

Cold-call two or three pairs. The scenario is used in no other lesson of this Set.
