Words to Know

agroundstuck on the bottom, where the water is too shallow for a ship
dredgera machine or boat that digs sand and mud out from under the water
tuga small, very powerful boat that pushes or pulls a larger one
convoya group of ships travelling through together, in order
backlogwork or traffic that has built up because it could not be dealt with in time
Phrase Spotlight
round the clock: all day and all night without stopping.
hold tight: wait, and do not do anything yet.
go pear-shaped: to go badly wrong after starting well.

[A] 1/7, 23 March, 08:40. Right. A 400-metre container ship has gone aground in the Suez Canal, sideways. Not alongside the bank. Sideways, bow in one wall and stern in the other. I have never typed that sentence before. #Suez

  1. 1.
    What does the writer mean by "I have never typed that sentence before"? What is that telling you about how unusual this is?

[B] 2/7, 23 March, 14:10. The wind is the first answer everyone reaches for, and there was a sandstorm that morning, so it is not a silly one. But a ship that size does not turn across a canal because of weather alone, and the investigation will take months. For today what matters is simpler: nothing can pass in either direction, and about twelve per cent of the world's trade goes through this ditch. The ships already inside the canal are stuck inside it, and the ones outside are anchoring where they can.

  1. 2.
    The writer says the wind is "the first answer everyone reaches for". Are they agreeing with it?

[C] 3/7, 24 March, 19:55. Two dredgers are working on the bow, and the photograph doing the rounds today is of one small digger on the bank, which is not the whole operation but is not a lie either. The dredgers have pulled some twenty thousand cubic metres of sand and clay out from around the bow since yesterday. Somebody told a reporter this would be done by tonight. It will not be done by tonight.

[D] 4/7, 26 March, 21:30. Fourteen tugs now, working round the clock, and the queue at both ends has passed two hundred ships. Some owners have already turned for the Cape of Good Hope1, which adds about ten days and a great deal of fuel to a voyage, and that is a decision you cannot take back once you have taken it. Nobody at either end knows whether tomorrow is the day it opens, which is the worst kind of information to plan a voyage around. Hold tight is getting harder to say.

  1. 3.
    Why does the writer call the Cape decision one "you cannot take back"?

[E] 5/7, 27 March, 23:05. The spring tide2 on Sunday night is the best chance anyone has, and if it goes pear-shaped there we are into refloating plans that involve taking containers off, which is weeks, not days. I have checked tide tables twice today. I have not checked tide tables twice in a day in eleven years of this job.

  1. 4.
    The writer tells you how many times they checked the tide tables. Why put that in?

[F] 6/7, 29 March, 12:20. She is off. Refloated on the tide this morning, six days after she went on, and the first convoy is already moving north. The backlog is over four hundred ships and will take about a week to clear, which is the part nobody will report, because a queue is not a photograph.

[G] 7/7, 29 March, 12:24. One more thing. There were twenty-five people on that ship for those six days, and I have read perhaps two hundred posts about her this week, including my own, and I do not think I have seen one of them mentioned. They were not rescued and they did not go anywhere, and for six days they were simply aboard, in the middle of the story everybody else was telling.

1. Cape of Good Hope: the southern tip of Africa. Ships that cannot use the Suez Canal sail right around it, which is a much longer route.
2. Spring tide: a tide that rises higher than usual, which happens twice a month at the full and new moon. A higher tide lifts a grounded ship further.

    With a partner, find and say aloud: the post with the most numbers in it, the post with the fewest, and the two posts sent on the same day.
Ferry Aground on the Sandbank Again

[A] The island ferry went aground on the sandbank outside the harbour for the third time this year on Tuesday evening, with forty passengers on board and no injuries. A tug from the boatyard pulled her clear at high water, five hours later.

[B] The harbour board has asked for a dredger for six years. The sandbank moves every winter, and the channel markers have not been moved with it, which is the part nobody argues about and nobody pays for.

[C] Tuesday's sailings were cancelled and the backlog cleared on Wednesday, when the boat ran a convoy of three crossings in a row. Passengers waiting on the pier were told to hold tight, which several of them had been told last February as well.

[D] The board meets again in April. The ferry company says the sandbank is a matter for the harbour, and the harbour says the channel markers are a matter for the company, and the ferry will go aground again in November.
