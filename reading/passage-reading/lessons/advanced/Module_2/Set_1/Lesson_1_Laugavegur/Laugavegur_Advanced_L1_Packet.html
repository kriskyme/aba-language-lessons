Before You Read

There are no shops and no roads, and there are rivers to wade across. At the end, the walker compares their own diary against the official description of the same trail.

Four Days on the Laugavegur
From the notebook of a walker who finished the trail in June

[A] The bus left us at Landmannalaugar1 at eleven, and by noon I was climbing through steam. The ground here is warm in places, and thin white vents open beside the path without warning. Our group of six walked in a loose line, stopping whenever somebody wanted a photograph of hills that are orange and green and grey at once. I had told everyone at home that this was an easy trail, and by the first ridge I had stopped saying it.

[B] The last hour was snow. In June there is still snow at that height, packed hard and grey, with marker posts standing out of it every hundred metres. I counted the posts ahead of me. Then I counted them again, and a third time, because the cloud had come down and I could see only four at once. We reached the hut at Hrafntinnusker at seven, the highest and coldest on the trail, and called it a day without argument.

[C] The second day is the one people warn you about, and it was fine. We came down off the snow by ten, crossed two fields of scree, and stood above a green valley with a lake in the middle of it. There was a woman in our group, Marta, who had barely spoken since the bus, and at the top of the descent she sat down on a rock and did not get up for a long time. When I came near she turned her face away and wiped it with her sleeve. I thought she had got cold feet about the rivers ahead. She said she had not expected it to be green.

[D] We forded our first real river before breakfast on the third morning, boots off, poles out, the water somewhere above the knee and cold enough to hurt. After that came the black sand. It goes on for hours, flat and empty, with nothing to look at but the marker posts and the ash moving across the track. Marta walked at the front all day and set a pace the rest of us complained about under our breath. Nobody asked her to slow down.

[E] The last day gives back everything the sand takes: the path drops into a gorge, climbs out of it, and then there are birch trees, the first in four days, small and bent and green. We forded the last river in a line, holding one another's arms, and walked into Thorsmork2 in the rain without hurrying.

[F] I have read the trail association's description of this route three times since I got home. It is accurate. It is also written by someone who has never watched a stranger sit down on a rock and cry about a colour.

1. Landmannalaugar: an area of hot springs in the Icelandic highlands, reached by bus in summer. It is where the trail starts.
2. Thorsmork: a valley of low birch woodland at the end of the trail, sheltered between three glaciers.

The Laugavegur: Four Days, Four Huts
From the trail association's route notes

[G] The Laugavegur is Iceland's best-known hiking route: fifty-five kilometres from the hot springs at Landmannalaugar to the birch woods of Thorsmork, walked in four comfortable days with a hut waiting at the end of each one.

[H] Day one climbs gently through steaming ground and coloured hills to Hrafntinnusker, the highest hut on the route. Snow may lie on this section into July, and marker posts guide walkers across it.

[I] Day two descends to the lake at Alftavatn, where a green valley opens below the path. Many walkers call this the finest hour of the whole trail.

[J] Day three crosses the black sands to Emstrur, and day four fords the last river and finishes among the birch trees. Every river on the route is fordable on foot in normal summer conditions. Book early: the huts fill from February.

Warm-Up
Notice: The Old Quarry Path Reopens Saturday

[A] The path through the old quarry has been closed since the winter storms, and volunteers have spent six weekends putting it back. The loose scree below the east face has been cleared twice, and a new marker post now stands at every turn, because three walkers lost the route in fog last November.

[B] Two changes are worth knowing about. The stream at the bottom no longer has a bridge, so walkers must ford it, which is easy in summer and not advisable after rain. The old air vent from the quarry workings has been fenced off. The ridge path above it is unchanged and still the quickest way back to the car park.

[C] The volunteers worked until dark on the last two Saturdays and then called it a day with the top section still unfinished. It will be done by spring.

[D] Anyone who wants to help with that last section should meet at the car park at nine on the first Saturday of April. Nobody needs experience, but the ground above the old workings is still soft, and the walk in crosses the stream.
