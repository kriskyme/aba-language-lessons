# Lesson 1: Four Days on the Laugavegur

**Module:** 2, Narrating | **Band:** Advanced | **Task Levels:** 4, 5, 6, 7 | **Version:** 2.1.1.18

**Set:** Module 2, Set 1, Lesson 1 | **Genre:** travel / field-journal narrative | **Reading strategy:** Say Something | **Phase 1 hook:** Mystery Quote | **Phase 3 protocol:** Jigsaw Expert Panels

**Anchor calibration:** Level 5 (B2). 455 words, 6 paragraphs, average sentence length 18.2 words.

**Subject note:** the Laugavegur trail, its four huts, the terrain, and the river crossings are real and can be
looked up. The walker, the group, Marta, and the journal itself are invented. No real person is quoted anywhere
in this lesson, and the trail association text in Task D is an invented example of that genre, not an extract
from any real organisation's published material.

### Phase 1: Pre-Reading and Activation (15 min)

**Hook: Mystery Quote (5 min).** The hook orients before it asks anything (Quality Standards §D13, Section
0.12). Say this to the class first, and leave it on the board:

> "Today you are reading four days from the diary of somebody walking the Laugavegur, a 55 kilometre trail
> across the mountains of Iceland. They sleep in a hut at the end of each day. There are no shops and no roads,
> and there are rivers to wade across. At the end, the walker compares their own diary against the official
> description of the same trail."

Then ask the connecting question and collect the answers on the board as a running list, one line each:

> "Have you ever walked somewhere for a whole day, or had a day that turned out much harder than you expected?
> Where was it, and what was the hardest part?"

Take six or seven answers. Every student has one, which is why it is asked first.

Now print the line below, tell the class it comes from near the end of the diary, and ask the second question:

> "The last day gives back everything the sand takes."

> "From that line alone, what do you think the day before was like, and what makes you say so?"

Collect those answers in a second short list beside the first. Do not confirm or correct anything now; the
second list stays on the board through Phase 2 and is checked against the text at the end of it.

**Board moment (Day 1).** The two lists are the day's board-dependent moment: both are co-constructed live from
student call-outs, they hold input no handout contains, and the second is returned to and marked up after
reading.

**Note on the quote choice (teacher-facing).** The line is from Paragraph [E] and is deliberately not the
diary's closing line. That closing line is the answer to Task C item 3, so printing it here would hand a Level 6
item its answer before the reading starts (Quality Standards §C9). The [E] line carries the same invitation and
tests nothing.

**Skill Spotlight (2 min).** Say, in these words, to the whole class:

> "Today we're practicing telling what happened in order and showing why one moment mattered more than the
> others, without stopping to explain it."

**Pre-teach: Words to Know (6 min).** Five target words, each matched in context to the paragraph it appears in.
Read the paragraph aloud, then ask which word in the list the paragraph is using.

| Word | Plain meaning | Find it in |
|---|---|---|
| ridge | the long top edge of a hill or mountain | [A] |
| vent | a small opening where steam or gas comes out of the ground | [A] |
| marker post | a tall post put in the ground to show where a path goes | [B] |
| scree | loose broken stones covering a slope | [C] |
| ford (verb) | to cross a river by walking through it | [D] |

**Idiom and Slang Spotlight (2 min).** On top of the five words above.

- **call it a day** - transparent. To stop doing something for the rest of the day. Say it, give one everyday
  example, move on.
- **under our breath** - transparent. Quietly, so the person cannot hear you.
- **get cold feet** - opaque. To suddenly become too nervous to do something you had planned. This one cannot be
  worked out from the words themselves, so gloss it directly before reading. Note for the teacher: in Paragraph
  [C] the narrator guesses that this is what has happened, and the guess turns out to be wrong. That gap is what
  the Level 5 and Level 6 tasks are built on.

### Phase 2: Text Engagement and Strategy Execution (35 min)

**Strategy: Say Something.** Students read in pairs. At each `[PAUSE: say something to your partner]` both
partners stop. Either partner may react, question, or connect, in one or two sentences, before reading on. There
are no fixed roles and nothing is written down. Circulate and listen for pairs who are only summarising; prompt
them with "what surprised you there?"

**After reading (5 min):** return to the Mystery Quote board list. Tick the guesses the text supports, cross out
the ones it does not, and add any line the class now wants to add. Leave the board as it is for Day 2.

## ANCHOR TEXT

### Four Days on the Laugavegur

*From the notebook of a walker who finished the trail in June.*

**[A]** The bus left us at Landmannalaugar(1) at eleven, and by noon I was climbing through steam. The ground
here is warm in places, and thin white **vents** open beside the path without warning. Our group of
six walked in a loose line, stopping whenever somebody wanted a photograph of hills that are orange and green
and grey at once. I had told everyone at home that this was an easy trail, and by the first **ridge** I had
stopped saying it.

`[PAUSE: say something to your partner]`

**[B]** The last hour was snow. In June there is still snow at that height, packed hard and grey, with
**marker posts** standing out of it every hundred metres. I counted the posts ahead of me. Then I counted them
again, and a third time, because the cloud had come down and I could see only four at once. We reached the hut at
Hrafntinnusker at seven, the highest and coldest on the trail, and called it a day without argument.

`[PAUSE: say something to your partner]`

**[C]** The second day is the one people warn you about, and it was fine. We came down off the snow by ten,
crossed two fields of **scree**, and stood above a green valley with a lake in the middle of it. There was a
woman in our group, Marta, who had barely spoken since the bus, and at the top of the descent she sat down on a
rock and did not get up for a long time. When I came near she turned her face away and wiped it with her sleeve. I
thought she had got cold feet about the rivers ahead. She said she had not expected it to be green.

`[PAUSE: say something to your partner]`

**[D]** We **forded** our first real river before breakfast on the third morning, boots off, poles out, the water
somewhere above the knee and cold enough to hurt. After that came the black sand. It goes on for hours, flat and
empty, with nothing to look at but the marker posts and the ash moving across the track. Marta walked
at the front all day and set a pace the rest of us complained about under our breath. Nobody asked her to slow
down.

`[PAUSE: say something to your partner]`

**[E]** The last day gives back everything the sand takes: the path drops into a gorge, climbs out of it, and
then there are birch trees, the first in four days, small and bent and green. We forded the last river in a line,
holding one another's arms, and walked into Thorsmork(2) in the rain without hurrying.

**[F]** I have read the trail association's description of this route three times since I got home. It is
accurate. It is also written by someone who has never watched a stranger sit down on a rock and cry about a
colour.

---

(1) Landmannalaugar: an area of hot springs in the Icelandic highlands, reached by bus in summer. It is where
the trail starts.

(2) Thorsmork: a valley of low birch woodland at the end of the trail, sheltered between three glaciers.

### Phase 3: Day 1 Literal Comprehension and Vocabulary Context Check (25 min)

Four Level blocks. Every student answers only their own Level's block. Expected answers are on `Answer note:`
lines and are not printed in the student packet.

#### Task A (Level 4) - Fact Finder

*Objective: read a short narrative text and identify the sequence of events, one outcome, and the brief reason
given for that outcome.*

1. Number these four events 1 to 4 in the order the journal gives them.
   - The group walks into Thorsmork in the rain.
   - The bus leaves the group at Landmannalaugar.
   - Marta sits down on a rock at the top of the descent.
   - The group takes off their boots to cross a river.

   **Answer note:** reading down the list as printed, 2, 4, 3, 1; in order the events are the bus, the rock, the
   river, Thorsmork.


2. Before the walk, the writer had told everyone at home one thing about the trail. Write what they had said,
   and say what happened on the first day that changed it.

   **Answer note:** That it was an easy trail; by the first ridge they had stopped saying it, both in [A]. The
   stem must not name what they had said: an earlier draft read "the writer stops telling people the trail is
   easy," which states the very thing the item asks for (Quality Standards §C9).

3. In Paragraph [B], the writer counts the marker posts more than once. The paragraph gives one reason. Write it
   in one sentence.

   **Answer note:** The cloud had come down and only four posts were visible at a time.

4. Circle the two things the writer says are at the end of the trail in Paragraph [E], and cross out the two that
   are not: **birch trees / a lake / rain / snow / a gorge / hot springs**

   **Answer note:** Circle birch trees and rain. The gorge is on the way, not at the end; the lake is Day 2; snow
   and hot springs are Day 1.

#### Task B (Level 5) - Sequence and Significance

*Objective: summarize the sequence of events and why they mattered, and identify at least one clearly telling
action or detail that signals how someone felt, even though the feeling is never named directly.*

Note for the teacher: this is the Module 2 shape of the native-Level set. It is not a Cause and Effect set; that
belongs to Module 3, Explaining.

1. Summarise the four days in four sentences, one per day, using the paragraph letters as your guide.

   **Answer note:** Day 1 [A]-[B], steam, ridge, snow, the hut. Day 2 [C], down off the snow to the green valley.
   Day 3 [D], the river ford and the black sand. Day 4 [E], the gorge, the birch trees, Thorsmork.

2. Of the four days, which one does the writer treat as the one that mattered most? Give the paragraph letter and
   one phrase from it that shows you.

   **Answer note:** [C]. Accept any of "stood above a green valley," "did not get up for a long time," "she had
   not expected it to be green." The day the writer calls "fine" is the day they write about at greatest length.

3. Paragraph [B] describes an action the writer repeats. Name the action, and say in one sentence what it tells
   you about how the writer was feeling. The journal never names the feeling.

   **Answer note:** Counting the marker posts three times. It signals anxiety or fear of losing the path. Accept
   any near equivalent; do not accept "cold," which the text states directly and so is not a signal.

4. Find an action in Paragraph [C] that shows a feeling the journal never names. Quote it and say whose feeling
   it is.

   **Answer note:** Marta's, on either of two acceptable quotations. The stronger is "turned her face away and
   wiped it with her sleeve": she is crying, and the journal never says so. Also accept "sat down on a rock and
   did not get up for a long time." Students who pick the sleeve often read it as fear, because the next
   sentence has the narrator guessing "cold feet"; the sentence after that corrects him ("she had not expected
   it to be green"), so the feeling is being overwhelmed by the view, not afraid. That two-step misdirection is
   the point of the item, and it is worth drawing out in feedback rather than marking a fear reading wrong
   outright.

#### Task C (Level 6) - Pacing, Reactions, and the Tone Shift

*Objective: identify specific pacing choices as heightening or minimizing significance, separate stated events
from clearly-signaled reactions, and locate at least one place where the narrative's tone visibly shifts.*

1. Find the stretch of the four days the journal gives the most space to, and the stretch it passes over fastest.
   Then, in two sentences, say what that pairing makes the reader weigh more heavily.

   **Answer note:** Most space, the few minutes at the top of the descent in [C]; fastest, the whole of Day 3 in
   [D]. The stretched moment on the rock is made to matter more than the hardest day's walking, and the
   compression of [D] into one paragraph of flat repetition ("it goes on for hours") does the opposite. The item
   must not name either stretch: locating them is what the Level 6 row asks the student to do (§C9).

2. Beside each line write E if it only reports something that happened, R if it only shows somebody's
   reaction without naming a feeling, or B if it does both at once.
   - "We reached the hut at Hrafntinnusker at seven"
   - "she sat down on a rock and did not get up for a long time"
   - "Nobody asked her to slow down."
   - "The bus left us at Landmannalaugar at eleven"
   - "Marta walked at the front all day and set a pace the rest of us complained about under our breath."

   **Answer note:** E, B, R, E, B. B is the common case in a narrative and is meant to be: an action can report
   what happened and show how somebody took it in the same clause, so lines 2 and 5 are genuinely both and were
   unanswerable in the earlier two-way version of this item (§C12). R is reserved for a line reporting no event at
   all, which is why line 3, reporting what nobody did, is the only R here.

3. The journal's tone shifts once, visibly, partway through. Name the paragraph where it shifts and quote the
   first line that shows the new tone.

   **Answer note:** [F]. "It is also written by someone who has never watched a stranger sit down on a rock and
   cry about a colour." Up to [E] the register is practical and clipped; [F] turns pointed. Accept [C] as a
   second answer only if the student quotes "she had barely spoken since the bus" and argues the warmth starts
   there; the marked shift is [F].

4. Paragraph [C] opens "The second day is the one people warn you about, and it was fine." Say in one sentence
   what that opening is doing to the reader's expectation of the paragraph.

   **Answer note:** It closes down one kind of significance (danger) so the paragraph can spend itself on a
   different one (Marta). Accept any answer naming the misdirection.

#### Task D (Level 7) - Two Accounts of the Same Route

*Objective: read two extended narratives covering the same or related events, critically assess how each
writer's structural choices shape what the reader is meant to conclude, and identify at least one unstated
motive or interest behind one writer's structural choices.*

Read the trail association's own notes on the same four days, printed below in full.

> ### The Laugavegur: Four Days, Four Huts
> *From a trail association's route notes.*
>
> **[G]** The Laugavegur is Iceland's best-known hiking route: fifty-five kilometres from the hot springs at
> Landmannalaugar to the birch woods of Thorsmork, walked in four comfortable days with a hut waiting at the end
> of each one.
>
> **[H]** Day one climbs gently through steaming ground and coloured hills to Hrafntinnusker, the highest hut on
> the route. Snow may lie on this section into July, and marker posts guide walkers across it.
>
> **[I]** Day two descends to the lake at Alftavatn, where a green valley opens below the path. Many walkers call
> this the finest hour of the whole trail.
>
> **[J]** Day three crosses the black sands to Emstrur, and day four fords the last river and finishes among the
> birch trees. Every river on the route is fordable on foot in normal summer conditions. Book early: the huts
> fill from February.

1. Both texts describe the same four days. Find two things that both of them describe. For each one, write the
   words the journal uses and the words the route notes use, then say in one sentence what each writer's wording
   is built to make the reader conclude.

   **Answer note:** Three are easy to find, and the student needs only two: the snow above Hrafntinnusker, the descent to the lake, and the
   river crossings; accept any shared subject genuinely covered by both. The notes turn each into a managed feature
   (posts guide you, the valley opens, rivers are fordable); the journal turns each into something survived. Look for
   students naming the grammar: the notes put the trail in the subject position and the walker nowhere. Neither the
   shared subjects nor the quotations may be printed for the student: both texts are on the page in full, so finding
   and pairing them is the Level 7 work (§C9).


2. In three to four sentences, say what the journal is claiming the route notes leave out, and whether the notes
   could include it and still do their job.

   **Answer note:** The notes leave out what the trail does to the people on it. There is no obligation to agree
   with the journal here; the strong answers argue that a booking document which included Marta would stop
   functioning as a booking document. That argument is also where this task meets the Level 7 row's "identify at
   least one unstated motive or interest": saying what the notes' job is names the interest (filling the huts)
   the notes never state. Do not restore the earlier framing sentence naming Paragraph [F] and its move; locating
   that is the student's work (§C9).
#### Vocabulary in Context (all Levels, 5 min)

Complete each sentence with one word from the bank. Two words are not needed.

**Bank: ridge / vent / marker post / scree / ford / gorge / hut**

1. Steam came out of a ________ beside the path.
2. We had to ________ the river on foot because there was no bridge.
3. From the ________ we could see the whole valley below us.
4. Loose stones, or ________, made the slope hard to walk on.
5. In thick cloud the next ________ was the only thing to walk towards.

**Answer note:** vent, ford, ridge, scree, marker post. "Gorge" and "hut" are the padding, both taught in the
text and both plausible in items 3 and 5.

### Phase 1: Text Re-Engagement and Vocabulary Warm-Up (15 min)

**Vocabulary Refresher Text (8 min).** A short unrelated text that reuses Day 1's words. Read it once, then do
the task under it.

> ### Notice: The Old Quarry Path Reopens Saturday
>
> **[A]** The path through the old quarry has been closed since the winter storms, and volunteers have spent six
> weekends putting it back. The loose **scree** below the east face has been cleared twice, and a new **marker
> post** now stands at every turn, because three walkers lost the route in fog last November.
>
> **[B]** Two changes are worth knowing about. The stream at the bottom no longer has a bridge, so walkers must
> **ford** it, which is easy in summer and not advisable after rain. The old air **vent** from the quarry
> workings has been fenced off. The **ridge** path above it is unchanged and still the quickest way back to the
> car park.
>
> **[C]** The volunteers worked until dark on the last two Saturdays and then called it a day with the top
> section still unfinished. It will be done by spring.
>
> **[D]** Anyone who wants to help with that last section should meet at the car park at nine on the first
> Saturday of April. Nobody needs experience, but the ground above the old workings is still soft, and the walk
> in crosses the stream.

**Task (light touch, 4 min).** In pairs, find and circle the five target words and the one idiom. Then answer
out loud, one sentence: what is different about this path now compared to before the storms?

**Answer note:** Words: scree, marker post, ford, vent, ridge. Idiom: called it a day. Differences: no bridge,
new posts, the vent fenced off.

**Spoken Vocabulary Mastery (3 min).** Round the room, one question each: When did you last have to ford
anything, even a puddle? What would you use as a marker post if you had none?

### Phase 2: Collaborative Multi-Level Investigation (30 min)

Groups of four, one student per task Level. Each student completes their own cell first as rehearsal prep, then
the group combines them orally in Level order.

**Collaborative Evidence Matrix: What the four days did to the people walking them**

| Task Level | Cell prompt |
|---|---|
| Level 4 | Choose the day you would least like to have walked and say one reason from the text. |
| Level 5 | For two of the four days, name one action the text describes and one feeling that action signals without naming it. Quote the action. |
| Level 6 | Find the two places where the journal tells you what somebody did not say or do. Say what each silence is doing that a stated reaction would not. |
| Level 7 | Take one sentence from the route notes and rewrite it so it keeps every fact but leaves the reader expecting a hard day. Then say what the original was buying by putting it the other way round. |

**Answer note (Level 4):** any day is acceptable with a reason quoted from the text. The days are [A]-[B] the
climb and the snow, [C] the descent, [D] the ford and the black sand, [E] the gorge and the trees; the class
established that order in Day 1 Phase 3, which is where this Level's sequence component is met.
**Answer note (Level 5):** counting the posts [B] / fear; the sleeve [C] / crying; the pace at the front [D] /
something resolved or angry; walking the last kilometre without hurrying [E] / not wanting it to end.
**Answer note (Level 6):** "Nobody asked her to slow down" [D] and "called it a day without argument" [B].
Both report group agreement without anyone speaking, which keeps the narrator from having to claim it.
**Answer note (Level 7):** e.g. "Snow may lie on this section into July, and marker posts guide walkers across
it" becomes "Marker posts are the only guide across snow that may lie on this section into July." The fact set is
identical. What the original was buying: putting the posts second makes them the answer to the snow, so the
sentence closes on a solution and the reader files the section as handled. "Every river on the route is fordable
on foot in normal summer conditions" works the same way and is the other obvious choice. Accept any sentence from
the notes with a rewrite that adds nothing and removes nothing.

**Board moment (Day 2).** Put the four days across the board as columns first, from the order the class fixed in
Day 1 Phase 3, then build the chart live as each group reports: one row per task Level's contribution. No group's
row makes sense without the others; the Level 5 and 6 rows attach to the day columns, and the Level 7 row is
added against the finished chart. This is the day's board-dependent moment, and the whole it makes is on no
handout.

### Phase 3: Structured Oral Output and Synthesis (30 min)

**Protocol: Jigsaw Expert Panels (25 min).** Regroup so that each new group holds one student from each of the
original matrix groups. Each new group takes one prompt from the three below; groups with the same prompt are
seated apart. Everybody runs from their seat; nothing is posted and nothing moves.

- **Prompt 1:** The journal and the route notes are both true. Is one of them more honest?
- **Prompt 2:** The journal spends more words on four minutes at the top of a descent than on a whole day of
  walking. Was that the right choice for a reader who has never walked the trail?
- **Prompt 3:** If the trail association read Paragraph [F], what should they change, if anything?

Rotate the prompts at roughly ten and twenty minutes, so each group works two of the three.

**Sentence stems by task Level** (each inside its own Level's ceiling):

- **Level 4:** "First ______, then ______." / "The part that mattered was ______ because the text says ______."
- **Level 5:** "What happened was ______, and it mattered because ______." / "The writer never says ______, but
  ______ shows it."
- **Level 6:** "The writer spends longest on ______, which makes ______ feel more important than ______." / "The
  tone changes at ______, from ______ to ______."
- **Level 7:** "The route notes are structured to make the reader conclude ______." / "The interest behind that
  choice is ______, which the text never states."

**Differentiated participation (Level 4).** Before their group's round begins, the Level 4 student does a
two-minute rehearsed pair-share of their one prepared sentence with the Level 5 student beside them, then gives
that sentence first when the round opens. They do not improvise a second turn unless they want to.

**Foundation Support.** For a student working below Level 4: matching the four printed day-headings in the
packet to the four printed event phrases; pointing to the paragraph that answers a Fact Finder rather than
writing the answer; a two-choice circle in place of items 1 and 4 of Task A; a peer buddy for the matrix cell.
In Phase 3 their role is to hold up, at the right moment in their group's round, a word they have written on
their own paper (the day the group is discussing), rather than take a spoken turn.

**Closing Transfer Check (5 min, oral only, nothing collected).** Every student, in pairs, out loud:

> "In three or four sentences, tell your partner how you got to class today, in order, and make one moment in
> it the moment that mattered. Do not explain why it mattered. Let the order do it."

Cold-call two or three pairs. This is the spotlighted skill on new material, and the scenario is used in no other
lesson of this Set.
