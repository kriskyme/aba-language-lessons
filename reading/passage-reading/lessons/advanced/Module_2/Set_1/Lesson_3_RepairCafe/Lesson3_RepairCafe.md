# Lesson 3: The Mixer on the Table

**Module:** 2, Narrating | **Band:** Advanced | **Task Levels:** 4, 5, 6, 7 | **Version:** 2.1.3.11

**Set:** Module 2, Set 1, Lesson 3 | **Genre:** advice-column letter with response | **Reading strategy:** Partner / Shared Reading | **Phase 1 hook:** K-W-L Chart | **Phase 3 protocol:** Town Hall Role-Play

**Anchor calibration:** Level 5 (B2). 459 words (letter and reply together), 6 paragraphs, average sentence
length 19.1 words.

**Subject note:** Repair Cafes are real and can be looked up: volunteer-run sessions where people bring broken
household things to be mended for nothing, begun in Amsterdam in 2009 and now running in thousands of towns. The
volunteer, the visitor, the mixer, the evening, the column, and the columnist are all invented, and so is the
review in Task D. No real person or organisation is quoted anywhere in this lesson.

### Phase 1: Pre-Reading and Activation (15 min)

**Hook: K-W-L Chart (6 min).** The chart is printed in the packet with three columns, K, W and L, and the
heading *Repair Cafes: places where volunteers mend people's broken things for free.* Students fill in K and W
individually for two minutes, compare with the person next to them for two, and then report. Build the class K
and W columns live on the board from what they report. Leave the L column empty on the board; it is filled on
Day 2.

**Board moment (Day 1).** The K and W columns are the day's board-dependent moment: co-constructed live from
student call-outs, holding what no single student's chart holds, and deliberately returned to and extended on
Day 2. It stays on the board between the two days.

**Skill Spotlight (2 min).** Say, in these words, to the whole class:

> "Today we're practicing telling what happened in order and showing why one moment mattered more than the
> others, without stopping to explain it."

**Pre-teach: Words to Know (5 min).** Five target words, matched in context to the paragraph they appear in.

| Word | Plain meaning | Find it in |
|---|---|---|
| volunteer | somebody who does work without being paid for it | [A] |
| secondhand | already owned and used by somebody else | [A] |
| guarantee | a promise that something will work, or be put right if it does not | [B] |
| dismantle | to take something apart into its separate pieces | [B] |
| spare part | a new piece bought to replace a broken piece | [C] |

**Idiom and Slang Spotlight (2 min).** On top of the five words above.

- **give it a go** - transparent. To try something without being sure it will work.
- **at the end of the day** - transparent. When everything is considered; what it comes down to.
- **get the wrong end of the stick** - opaque. To misunderstand something completely, usually in a way that makes
  somebody look bad. Nothing in the words tells you this, so gloss it directly before reading.

### Phase 2: Text Engagement and Strategy Execution (35 min)

**Strategy: Partner / Shared Reading.** In pairs, with the turn-taking below. The two voices on the page are a
letter and a reply, so the two readers hold one voice each for the first half and swap for the second.

- **Partner A reads Paragraphs [A]-[B]** aloud. *Turn check:* Partner B says, in one sentence, what the writer
  has promised the visitor.
- **Partner B reads Paragraphs [C]-[D].** *Turn check:* Partner A says what changed between [B] and [C].
- **Partner A reads Paragraph [E].** *Turn check:* Partner B says which of the two things Ruth has just
  separated.
- **Partner B reads Paragraph [F].** *Turn check:* Partner A says what Ruth actually asks the writer to change.

Bracketed capitals are paragraphs; Partner A and Partner B are always spelled out.

## ANCHOR TEXT

### Ask Ruth: "I Took It Apart In Front Of Her"

*A reader's letter and the columnist's reply.*

**[A]** Dear Ruth,

I am a **volunteer** at a Repair Cafe(1) in my town, one Saturday a month, and have been doing it for three
years. I mend small kitchen things, mostly **secondhand** ones people have been given. I am not an engineer; I am
careful, and I read the manuals. Last month something happened that I have not been able to leave alone, and I
want to know whether I was in the wrong.

**[B]** A woman came in at two o'clock with a food mixer that had belonged to her mother. It was forty years old,
and the motor ran, but the bowl would not turn. I told her what I tell everybody, which is that I would give it a
go, that we work for nothing, and that there is no **guarantee** of any kind. She signed the form that says so in
writing. Then I **dismantled** it on the table in front of her.

**[C]** The gear inside had gone, which I had expected. What I had not expected was that the casing would crack
as I opened it, because forty-year-old plastic does what forty-year-old plastic does. I found the **spare part**
online for eleven pounds and wrote the link on a card. She looked at the two halves of the casing lying
on the table and said, quietly, that she had only wanted it to work. Then she put her coat on before I had
finished the sentence I was saying.

**[D]** She has written about the evening online since. My name is not in it, but the town is small, and three
other volunteers have already asked me what happened. Somebody in the comments has got the wrong end
of the stick entirely and thinks we charge people. At the end of the day I did exactly what the form says I do.
I stayed behind that night and swept a floor that did not need sweeping.

Yours, Still Volunteering

**[E]** Dear Still Volunteering,

You did not do anything wrong, and that is not the same as saying nothing went wrong, so let me separate the two. The form covers the casing, but it does not cover the moment when a woman watches the last working thing of
her mother's come apart on a table, and that was never a repair question in the first place.

**[F]** Here is what I would change, and it is small. Say the sentence about old plastic before you open the
casing rather than after it, because a warning that arrives afterwards is a defence. And stop reading her review
for your name: you are not in it because she was not writing about you, but about a mixer, and about a Saturday
afternoon that she does not get to have again.

Ruth

---

(1) Repair Cafe: a free meeting place where volunteers mend visitors' broken belongings. The first one opened in
Amsterdam in 2009, and there are now several thousand around the world.

### Phase 3: Day 1 Literal Comprehension and Vocabulary Context Check (25 min)

Four Level blocks. Every student answers only their own Level's block.

#### Task A (Level 4) - Fact Finder

*Objective: read a short narrative text and identify the sequence of events, one outcome, and the brief reason
given for that outcome.*

1. Number these five events 1 to 5 in the order the letter gives them.
   - The visitor signs the form.
   - The casing cracks.
   - The volunteer sweeps the floor.
   - The visitor arrives with the mixer.
   - The volunteer writes a link on a card.

   **Answer note:** 2 [B], 3 [C], 5 [D], 1 [B], 4 [C].


2. What was wrong with the mixer when the visitor brought it in? Which paragraph says so?

   **Answer note:** The motor ran but the bowl would not turn. Paragraph [B].

3. The casing cracks when the volunteer opens it. Write in one sentence the reason the letter gives.

   **Answer note:** The plastic was forty years old.

4. Circle the three things the volunteer told the visitor before starting, and cross out the two he did not say:
   **that he would try / that the work is free / that there is no guarantee / that the casing might crack / that
   he is an engineer**

   **Answer note:** Circle the first three. He is not an engineer, and the warning about the casing is exactly
   what Ruth says was missing.

#### Task B (Level 5) - Sequence and Significance

*Objective: summarize the sequence of events and why they mattered, and identify at least one clearly telling
action or detail that signals how someone felt, even though the feeling is never named directly.*

Note for the teacher: this is the Module 2 shape of the native-Level set, not a Cause and Effect set, which
belongs to Module 3.

1. Summarise the afternoon in four sentences, using the paragraph letters as your guide.

   **Answer note:** [B] she arrives, is warned, signs, and the mixer is opened. [C] the gear has gone, the casing
   cracks, he finds the part. [C] she says what she wanted and leaves. [D] the review, the questions, the floor.

2. The letter ends on an action, not on an argument. Name the action, and say in one sentence what it tells you
   about how the writer felt. The letter never names the feeling.

   **Answer note:** Sweeping a floor that did not need sweeping. It signals guilt or distress that the writer
   will not state, having just argued that he did nothing wrong.

3. Find one action of the visitor's that shows a feeling the letter never names. Quote it and say whose feeling
   it is.

   **Answer note:** "put her coat on before I had finished the sentence I was saying," the visitor's. Accept
   "looked at the two halves of the casing lying on the table."

4. Of everything in the letter, which single moment does the writer treat as the one that mattered? Give the
   paragraph letter and one phrase that shows you.

   **Answer note:** [C], the moment she speaks and leaves. Accept "she had only wanted it to work" or the coat.
   The cracked casing is given one clause; her leaving is given two sentences.

#### Task C (Level 6) - Pacing, Reactions, and the Tone Shift

*Objective: identify specific pacing choices as heightening or minimizing significance, separate stated events
from clearly-signaled reactions, and locate at least one place where the narrative's tone visibly shifts.*

1. Find the longest span of time the letter covers in the fewest words, and the shortest span it gives the most
   words to. then say in two sentences what that pairing makes the reader weigh more heavily, and what it costs the
   writer.

   **Answer note:** Three years of volunteering in half a sentence in [A]; about four minutes at the table
   across the whole of [C]. It makes the four minutes outweigh the three years, which is against the writer's
   own argument, and the compression of his record is what leaves him with nothing but the form to stand on.
   The item must not name either span (§C9).

2. Beside each line write E if it only reports something that happened, R if it only shows somebody's
   reaction without naming a feeling, or B if it does both at once.
   - "A woman came in at two o'clock with a food mixer that had belonged to her mother."
   - "She signed the form that says so in writing."
   - "three other volunteers have already asked me what happened"
   - "I found the spare part online for eleven pounds."
   - "I stayed behind that night and swept a floor that did not need sweeping."

   **Answer note:** E, E, B, E, B. Lines 3 and 5 are both: the asking and the sweeping each happened, and each is
   how the letter shows a reaction it never names. Three of the five are pure events, so the split is uneven and
   elimination does not solve it (§C11, §C12).

3. The letter's tone shifts once, visibly, partway through. Name the paragraph where it shifts and quote the
   first line that shows the new tone.

   **Answer note:** [D]. "At the end of the day I did exactly what the form says I do." Up to there the letter
   narrates; from there it defends. Accept a student who instead marks the last sentence of [D] as a second
   shift, back out of defence, if they quote it.

4. Ruth's reply opens "You did not do anything wrong, and that is not the same as saying nothing went wrong."
   Say in one sentence what that opening lets her do for the rest of the reply.

   **Answer note:** It settles the question the writer asked so she can spend the reply on the one he did not.

#### Task D (Level 7) - Two Accounts of the Same Afternoon

*Objective: read two extended narratives covering the same or related events, critically assess how each
writer's structural choices shape what the reader is meant to conclude, and identify at least one unstated
motive or interest behind one writer's structural choices.*

Read the visitor's own review of the same afternoon, printed below in full.

> ### Two stars
> *A review of the same Saturday, posted online. Invented for this lesson.*
>
> **[G]** I took my mother's food mixer to the Repair Cafe on Saturday. It is forty years old, and until that
> afternoon it was the last thing of hers in this house that still switched on.
>
> **[H]** The volunteer was kind, and he told me there was no guarantee, and I signed a form that said so. I
> understood that part. What I did not understand was that it would be in pieces on the table about four minutes
> after I sat down.
>
> **[I]** It came apart and it did not go back together. The casing is in two halves in a bag under my stairs,
> with a card telling me where to buy a gear for eleven pounds, which I am not going to do.
>
> **[J]** I am not saying anybody did anything wrong. I am saying that if you are going to open something that
> cannot be closed again, say so first, and give people the minute they need to decide. Two stars. I will not go
> back.

1. Both texts describe the same afternoon. Find two things that both of them describe. For each one, write the
   words the letter uses and the words the review uses, then say in one sentence what each writer's wording
   is built to make the reader conclude.

   **Answer note:** Three are easy to find, and the student needs only two: the warning and the form, the moment the mixer was opened, and how
   it ended; accept any shared subject genuinely covered by both. The letter's structure makes the form the event and
   the woman the aftermath; the review's makes the four minutes the event and the form a thing already agreed and
   beside the point. Both agree on every fact. Look for students naming the tense work in "does what forty-year-old
   plastic does," which turns a particular accident into a general law. Neither the shared subjects nor the quotations
   may be printed for the student: both texts are on the page in full, so finding and pairing them is the Level 7 work
   (§C9).


2. The letter and the review both say that nobody did anything wrong, and both are unhappy. In three to four
   sentences, say what each text needs that sentence for, and whether the two texts are actually in disagreement
   about anything at all.

   **Answer note:** He needs it to be true so the evening is closed; she needs it said so her complaint is heard.
   The strong answers notice that the only disagreement is about when a warning should arrive, which is exactly
   what Ruth identifies, and that a disagreement that small is not what either text is really about. This question is also where the task meets the Level 7 row's "identify at least one unstated motive or interest": saying what each text needs its version for names the interest neither states.

#### Vocabulary in Context (all Levels, 5 min)

Complete each sentence with one word or phrase from the bank. Two are not needed.

**Bank: volunteer / secondhand / guarantee / dismantle / spare part / casing / repair**

1. Nobody is paid here; every person mending something is a ________.
2. The shop had no ________ for the gear, so it had to be ordered online.
3. She bought the machine ________ from a neighbour ten years ago.
4. You have to ________ the whole thing to reach the motor inside.
5. There is no ________ on this work, so it may still fail next week.

**Answer note:** volunteer, spare part, secondhand, dismantle, guarantee. "Casing" and "repair" are the padding,
both used in the text and both plausible in items 2 and 5.

### Phase 1: Text Re-Engagement and Vocabulary Warm-Up (15 min)

**Partner re-scan (3 min).** In pairs, find and say aloud: the paragraph where the visitor speaks, the paragraph
where the writer stops narrating and starts defending himself, and the two paragraphs written by Ruth.

**Answer note:** [C], [D], and [E]-[F].

**Board moment (Day 2).** Fill in the L column of Day 1's K-W-L chart on the board, from what the class now
knows, and mark which of the W questions the text never answered. The chart is the same one from Day 1: it
persists and is built on, which is the property D4 asks for.

**Vocabulary Refresher Text (8 min).** A short unrelated text that reuses Day 1's words.

> ### Club Notice: The Layout Comes Apart On The 14th
>
> **[A]** We **dismantle** the main layout on Saturday the 14th so that it can be rebuilt in the hall for the
> April exhibition. Every **volunteer** who can lift a board is wanted from nine in the morning, and there is tea
> at eleven.
>
> **[B]** Two notes. The new controller has a two-year **guarantee**, so nobody is to open it for any reason, and
> the old one goes in the spares box rather than the bin. We are still short of a **spare part** for the level
> crossing, and Derek has offered a **secondhand** one from a layout he bought in January.
>
> **[C]** The hall is booked from eight on the Sunday. Anyone who has taken a board home to work on should bring
> it back on the 14th and not on the day itself, because at the end of the day we would rather carry it twice
> than find out on the Sunday that it does not fit.

**Task (light touch, 3-4 min).** In pairs, find and circle the five target words and the one idiom. Then answer
out loud, in one sentence: what is the club worried about?

**Answer note:** Words: dismantle, volunteer, guarantee, spare part, secondhand. Idiom: at the end of the day.
The club is worried that a board worked on at home will not fit on the day.

**Spoken Vocabulary Mastery (3-4 min).** Round the room, one question each, answered aloud in a full sentence:
What is the most useful secondhand thing you own? What have you dismantled and failed to put back together? What
would you volunteer to do for free for one Saturday?

### Phase 2: Collaborative Multi-Level Investigation (30 min)

Groups of four, one student per task Level. Each student completes their own cell first, then the group combines
them orally in Level order.

**Collaborative Evidence Matrix: One afternoon, two people, and the minute in the middle of it**

| Task Level | Cell prompt |
|---|---|
| Level 4 | Choose the moment you think the visitor will remember longest, and give one reason from the text. |
| Level 5 | For the letter and for the review, name one action each text describes and one feeling that action signals without naming it. Quote both actions. |
| Level 6 | Find the place in the letter where the writer stops telling the story and starts arguing. Say what he gains and what he loses at exactly that sentence. |
| Level 7 | Ruth says a warning that arrives afterwards is a defence. Test that against the letter: find one sentence that would read differently if it had been said out loud at two o'clock, and say how. |

**Answer note (Level 4):** any moment is acceptable with a reason quoted from the text. The afternoon runs
arrival [B], the warning and the form [B], dismantling [B], the gear and the crack [C], the card [C], her
sentence and the coat [C], the review and the floor [D]; the class fixed that order in Day 1 Phase 3, which is
where this Level's sequence component is met.
**Answer note (Level 5):** letter: the sweeping [D] / guilt; review: "in a bag under my stairs" and the refusal
to buy the gear [I] / grief rather than anger.
**Answer note (Level 6):** at "At the end of the day I did exactly what the form says I do." He gains a clear
position and loses the reader's sense that he is still describing rather than pleading.
**Answer note (Level 7):** "forty-year-old plastic does what forty-year-old plastic does" is the obvious one:
said at two o'clock it is a warning; said in [C] it is an explanation of why nobody is to blame.

### Phase 3: Structured Oral Output and Synthesis (30 min)

**Protocol: Town Hall Role-Play (25 min).** The class sits as it is, in groups of four or five where they sit.
This is a town hall about how the Repair Cafe should run next month. Within each group, students take one role
each and keep it for the whole window: a volunteer repairer, a visitor who has brought something, the person who
organises the Saturdays, and a neighbour who has only read the review. Every role speaks in every round.

Groups take one prompt each per round, so simultaneous conversations are not identical. Rotate at roughly ten and
twenty minutes.

- **Prompt 1:** Should the Repair Cafe change the form, change what is said out loud, or change nothing?
- **Prompt 2:** The review is two stars and the service is free. Does free change what a fair review is?
- **Prompt 3:** Ruth answers a question the writer did not ask. Was that the right thing to do?

**Sentence stems by task Level:**

- **Level 4:** "First ______, then ______." / "The moment that mattered was ______ because the text says ______."
- **Level 5:** "What happened was ______, and it mattered because ______." / "The text never says ______, but
  ______ shows it."
- **Level 6:** "The letter spends longest on ______, which makes ______ feel more important than ______." / "The
  tone changes at ______, from ______ to ______."
- **Level 7:** "The review is structured to make the reader conclude ______." / "The interest behind that choice
  is ______, which the text never states."

**Differentiated participation (Level 4).** The Level 4 student takes the role of the visitor, rehearses one
prepared sentence with a neighbour in the two minutes before the first round, and speaks it first when the round
opens. Their role gives them something to say in every round without improvising an argument.

**Foundation Support.** For a student working below Level 4: matching the four printed role names in the packet
to four printed one-line descriptions; pointing to the paragraph that answers a Fact Finder instead of writing
it; a two-choice circle in place of items 1 and 4 of Task A; a peer buddy for the matrix cell. In Phase 3 their
role is to hold up a word they have written on their own paper (the role currently speaking) rather than take a
spoken turn.

**Closing Transfer Check (5 min, oral only, nothing collected).** Every student, in pairs, out loud:

> "In three or four sentences, tell your partner about a time something you own stopped working: what happened,
> in order, and make one moment in it the moment that mattered. Do not explain why it mattered."

Cold-call two or three pairs. The scenario is used in no other lesson of this Set.
