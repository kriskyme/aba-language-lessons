[A] Dear Ruth, I am a volunteer at a Repair Cafe1 in my town, one Saturday a month, and have been doing it for three years. I mend small kitchen things, mostly secondhand ones people have been given. I am not an engineer; I am careful, and I read the manuals. Last month something happened that I have not been able to leave alone, and I want to know whether I was in the wrong.

[B] A woman came in at two o'clock with a food mixer that had belonged to her mother. It was forty years old, and the motor ran, but the bowl would not turn. I told her what I tell everybody, which is that I would give it a go, that we work for nothing, and that there is no guarantee of any kind. She signed the form that says so in writing. Then I dismantled it on the table in front of her.

[C] The gear inside had gone, which I had expected. What I had not expected was that the casing would crack as I opened it, because forty-year-old plastic does what forty-year-old plastic does. I found the spare part online for eleven pounds and wrote the link on a card. She looked at the two halves of the casing lying on the table and said, quietly, that she had only wanted it to work. Then she put her coat on before I had finished the sentence I was saying.

[D] She has written about the evening online since. My name is not in it, but the town is small, and three other volunteers have already asked me what happened. Somebody in the comments has got the wrong end of the stick entirely and thinks we charge people. At the end of the day I did exactly what the form says I do. I stayed behind that night and swept a floor that did not need sweeping.

Yours, Still Volunteering

Ruth answers

[E] Dear Still Volunteering, You did not do anything wrong, and that is not the same as saying nothing went wrong, so let me separate the two. The form covers the casing, but it does not cover the moment when a woman watches the last working thing of her mother's come apart on a table, and that was never a repair question in the first place.

[F] Here is what I would change, and it is small. Say the sentence about old plastic before you open the casing rather than after it, because a warning that arrives afterwards is a defence. And stop reading her review for your name: you are not in it because she was not writing about you, but about a mixer, and about a Saturday afternoon that she does not get to have again.

Ruth

1. Repair Cafe: a free meeting place where volunteers mend visitors' broken belongings. The first one opened in Amsterdam in 2009, and there are now several thousand around the world.

Two stars: a review of the same Saturday
Posted online by the visitor

[G] I took my mother's food mixer to the Repair Cafe on Saturday. It is forty years old, and until that afternoon it was the last thing of hers in this house that still switched on.

[H] The volunteer was kind, and he told me there was no guarantee, and I signed a form that said so. I understood that part. What I did not understand was that it would be in pieces on the table about four minutes after I sat down.

[I] It came apart and it did not go back together. The casing is in two halves in a bag under my stairs, with a card telling me where to buy a gear for eleven pounds, which I am not going to do.

[J] I am not saying anybody did anything wrong. I am saying that if you are going to open something that cannot be closed again, say so first, and give people the minute they need to decide. Two stars. I will not go back.

What I learned

Club Notice: The Layout Comes Apart On The 14th

[A] We dismantle the main layout on Saturday the 14th so that it can be rebuilt in the hall for the April exhibition. Every volunteer who can lift a board is wanted from nine in the morning, and there is tea at eleven.

[B] Two notes. The new controller has a two-year guarantee, so nobody is to open it for any reason, and the old one goes in the spares box rather than the bin. We are still short of a spare part for the level crossing, and Derek has offered a secondhand one from a layout he bought in January.

[C] The hall is booked from eight on the Sunday. Anyone who has taken a board home to work on should bring it back on the 14th and not on the day itself, because at the end of the day we would rather carry it twice than find out on the Sunday that it does not fit.
