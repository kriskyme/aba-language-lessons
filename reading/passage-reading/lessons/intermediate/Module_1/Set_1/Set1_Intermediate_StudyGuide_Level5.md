# Set 1 Intermediate Study Guide — Task Level 5 (★★★★, highest)

Companion to `Set1_Intermediate_Assessment.md`. Review this guide for about 30 minutes right before the test.
This guide does not use the test's new passages or repeat any test item — it reviews the words and skill you
will need, using different examples.

## Vocabulary & Idiom Review

**Lesson 1: A Grandmother's Kitchen**
- **warmth** — beyond physical heat, this word can also suggest comfort, welcome, or closeness. Example: in
  "At the Market," Maria's candles "bring warmth to any room, even without a flame" — here "warmth" means a
  cozy, welcoming feeling, not literal heat.
*(Lesson 1: A Grandmother's Kitchen)*

**Lesson 2: New Corner of Yoyogi Park Opens to Visitors**
- **welcoming** — a word choice that reveals the writer's positive attitude, not just a neutral description.
  Example: calling the library entrance "more welcoming to visitors" tells the reader the writer approves of the
  change, without saying "I like this" directly.
*(Lesson 2: New Corner of Yoyogi Park Opens to Visitors)*

**Lesson 4: Painting Wynwood Walls: An Interview**
- **bright** — can describe color, but also suggest energy or a positive mood. Example: *"Many students say the
  bright colors are the first thing they notice"* — here "bright" signals something eye-catching and positive,
  not just a literal light color.
- **"jump out"** — to stand out or be very easy to notice. Example: orange and blue "make the bird jump out
  from the gray wall behind it" — the artist chose colors specifically so the mural would not blend into its
  background.
*(Lesson 4: Painting Wynwood Walls: An Interview)*

## Skill Review

The skill every lesson in this Set practiced, now at its deepest level: **summarizing how a writer organizes
their ideas, and identifying a word or phrase that reveals the writer's attitude without stating an opinion
directly.**

- **Lesson 1:** the article moves from describing the kitchen, to the grandmother's method, to why Elena loves
  it. Evaluative language: describing the kitchen as "the warmest part of the whole house" shows Elena's
  attitude without her saying "I love this kitchen."
- **Lesson 2:** the article moves from the announcement, to what was built, to how visitors feel about it.
  Evaluative language: the word "welcoming" (spoken by a quoted resident) shows approval without a direct
  opinion statement.
- **Lesson 3:** the guide is organized as four ordered steps, ending with the writer's own priority statement
  ("comfort matters more than style"). Notice how the order itself is an argument — foot type first, since
  everything else depends on it, and replacement last, since it is a maintenance step, not a purchase step.
- **Lesson 4:** the interview moves from describing the mural, to the artist's color choice, to comparing it with
  past work, to why outdoor art matters to her. Evaluative language: the word "alive" (used to describe this
  year's mural, versus the "slow and round" feeling of last year's) reveals the artist's preference.

## Sample Items (worked examples — not on the test)

**Organization-summary example:** *In 2-3 sentences, summarize how "At the Market" is organized, from
Maria's stall to her candles to what customers say about them.* Strong answer: the text first introduces Maria
and her stall, then explains how she makes the candles and what she adds to them, then ends with how
customers react — moving from the maker, to the product, to the audience.

**Cross-text synthesis example:** *Compare how "At the Market" and "A School Art Show" each describe someone
making something by hand. Which passage gives a stronger sense of tradition, and why?* Strong answer:
compares specific details from both passages (Maria learned her method from her own grandmother; the school
show is a yearly student tradition but focuses more on display than method) and states a clear, supported
judgment.

## Self-Check

- Can I summarize, in 2-3 sentences, how a passage moves from its beginning to its middle to its end?
- Can I find a word or phrase that shows the writer's attitude, without the writer stating an opinion directly,
  and explain why it counts as evaluative?
- Can I compare two different passages and explain which one makes a stronger case, using evidence from
  both?
- Do I understand "warmth," "welcoming," "bright," and "jump out" well enough to explain the feeling or
  attitude each one adds, not just its literal meaning?
