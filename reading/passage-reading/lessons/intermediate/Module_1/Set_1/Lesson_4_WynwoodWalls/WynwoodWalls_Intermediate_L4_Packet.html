[A] Reporter: Thanks for talking with me today. Can you tell me about the mural behind us? (gestures toward the wall)

Artist (Jade Rivera): Of course. This wall is in Wynwood Walls1, and I painted it last spring using spray paint and a few brushes for the fine lines. It shows a large bird made of bright orange and blue shapes.

[B] Reporter: Why did you choose those two colors?

Artist: Orange and blue sit across from each other on the color wheel, so they make the bird jump out from the gray wall behind it. I always start with color before I think about the exact shape. Sometimes I test a color on a scrap piece of wood before I ever touch the wall. The color decides the mood first.

[C] Reporter: How is this mural different from the one you painted last year, the one with the ocean waves? (points to a photo on her phone)

Artist: The wave mural used soft blues and greens, and the shapes felt slow and round. This bird uses sharp shapes and bright colors, so it feels fast and alive. I wanted this wall to feel like motion, not calm.

[D] Reporter: What do you like most about painting outdoors, in a place like this?

Artist: Anyone can walk by and see the work for free. In a gallery, only some people come inside and pay for a ticket. Here, the whole neighborhood becomes part of the art, in a way, and that changes how I paint. Kids often stop and ask me questions while I paint, which never happens in a quiet gallery.

[E] Reporter: What do you hope people feel when they see this mural?

Artist: I hope they slow down for a second and really look, not just walk past it. Even one bright wall can change how a street feels, and that is worth more to me than any prize.

1 Wynwood Walls is an outdoor area in Miami, Florida, known for large, colorful murals painted by street artists from around the world.

Focus on the Objective

Feature and comparison. In Paragraph [C], the artist compares this year's bird mural to last year's wave mural: "The wave mural used soft blues and greens, and the shapes felt slow and round. This bird uses sharp shapes and bright colors, so it feels fast and alive." The reason follows right after: "I wanted this wall to feel like motion, not calm." Naming the two murals' features and giving the reason for the difference is exactly what makes this a description, not just a list of facts.

Evaluative word choice. In that same sentence, the word "alive" is not a plain fact about the mural's colors or shapes; it shows how the artist feels about it. Finding a word like this tells you the writer's attitude even when she never says "I like this mural" directly.

Unit 4B: Painting Wynwood Walls: An Interview

Warm-Up

Circle the 5 words to know from before in the short piece below. Then tell a partner, in one sentence, how the school hallway is like Wynwood Walls.

A School Art Show

Every spring, the school hallway becomes a small gallery for one week. Students hang their paintings and drawings along the walls, and teachers add small cards next to each one. This year, one student painted a bright mural on a large piece of cardboard instead of using a canvas. She used simple shapes, mostly circles and triangles, to build a picture of the neighborhood around the school. Visitors from other classes walk through the hallway during lunch to look at the work. Many students say the bright colors are the first thing they notice. A few visitors even take photos of their favorite pieces before leaving. Teachers say the show helps students feel proud of their work.
