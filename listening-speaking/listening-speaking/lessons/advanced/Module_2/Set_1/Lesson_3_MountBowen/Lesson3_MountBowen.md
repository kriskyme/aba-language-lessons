## Target vocabulary (grounded in the verified text)

1. **pinning** (someone down) - holding them in place so they cannot move at all.
2. **broke loose** - came away suddenly from what was holding it.
3. **raise the alarm** - tell people that something is badly wrong so help can start.
4. **winched** - lowered or lifted by a motor-driven cable.
5. **toxins** - harmful substances that build up in the body.
6. **survivor guilt** - the feeling of having done wrong by coming through something others did not,
   or by being the reason someone else was harmed.

Context sentences (paraphrased from the source, not quoted verbatim):

1. A one-tonne boulder landed on his legs, pinning him to the creek bed.
2. A piece of rock the size of a refrigerator broke loose from the wall above him.
3. His companion walked for a day and a half to raise the alarm.
4. The crew were winched down into the creek bed from the helicopter.
5. Toxins had built up in his legs while he was trapped, and lifting the rock would release them.

**Figurative chunks to gloss (Advanced band):**

- **"hit reset"** - start again from the beginning after things have got out of hand.
- **"you're on"** - I accept; I will do it with you.
- **"take me out"** - kill me, or finish me off.

**Background Note 1:** Hinchinbrook Island is a mountainous national park island off the coast of Far

taking a boat.

**Background Note 2:** The accident happened in April 1997, before mobile phones were common and
before emergency beacons were easy to get. That is why raising the alarm took as long as it did.

## DAY 1: Unit A - Listening

```
DAY 1 (Unit A - Listening)  |--Hook, Vocab & Purpose (20)--|--Listen & Notes (15)--|--Skill Instr. (10)--|--Task (20)--|--Transfer Check (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

*Take a Side hook (5 min).* The choice is printed in the packet and marked there, then discussed from
where students sit. Print this:

> You are two days into a hike with someone you met yesterday. They want to keep going to the summit.
> You think the path has become unsafe. Circle one:
> **A.** You owe it to them to keep going, because you agreed to.
> **B.** You owe it to both of you to turn back, whatever you agreed.

Each student circles one, then finds someone who circled the other and gives one reason. Take two or
three reasons aloud from each side. Leave a tally on the board: the class's split gets revisited in
Phase 4, because both men in this story chose, and one of them spent years deciding whether he had
chosen wrongly.

*Listening Skill Spotlight (3 min).* Say to the class, in these words:

> "Today we are practicing telling the difference between an opinion a speaker states outright and one
> they only let you hear underneath what they say."

That is the whole Spotlight. It names the skill, not this story, and it is the same sentence for every
task Level. Day 2's closing check comes back to this same skill.

*Good to Know (3 min).* Read out Background Notes 1 and 2 above. Informational only.

*Vocabulary (9 min).* Pre-teach the six target words and the three figurative chunks using the context
sentences above. Take the three chunks explicitly at this band rather than leaving them to context.

**Phase 2: Listen & Listening Notes (15 min)**

Say in one sentence what Segment 1 covers (a painter from Airlie Beach took a trip north, got to
Hinchinbrook Island, met a Dutch hiker at the beach, and agreed to climb the mountain with him the
next morning), then play **Segments 2 through 4** straight through, without stopping.

Build this **sequence chain** on the board as a shared organizer, and have students fill the same
chain in their packets while they listen:

```
The climb  ->  The rock  ->  The decision  ->  The walk out  ->  Getting the message through  ->  The rescue
```

Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback:

- **Level 4:** listen for the order of what happened after the rock fell, and for how long it took.
- **Level 5:** listen for one thing Warren says that tells you what he was feeling under the rock,
  without him naming the feeling.
- **Level 6:** listen for whose hours you actually hear about, and whose you do not.
- **Level 7:** listen for the difference between how Warren talks about the decision to climb and how
  Geert talks about it.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

Teach **Listen for Stated vs. Implied Opinion** directly. This source gives a clean pair of models,
one of each kind. Put both on the board.

Stated outright, from **[Segment 5]**, Geert:

> "I felt guilty that if it wasn't for me, he wouldn't have gone up that mountain."

There is no work to do here: the opinion is the sentence. He judges himself, and says so.

Implied, from **[Segment 5]**, Warren:

> "I've often said, given the chance to go back [in time] I wouldn't go back, which kind of blows
> people away."

Ask what opinion is inside that, and how you know. He never says the accident was worth it, or that
he is glad, or that people are wrong to pity him. He says only what he would choose, plus a note that
the answer surprises people. The opinion arrives through the choice and through his awareness of the
reaction, not through a judgment word. Name the giveaway: an implied opinion usually shows up as a
choice, a comparison, or a detail that the speaker leaves you to weigh.

Guided practice on a second short excerpt, from **[Segment 2]**, Warren under the rock:

> "Just that idea that the very thing that I've been chasing was now going to take me out."

Students write S or I, then one sentence on what the opinion actually is. Take three answers.

*Note on wording (teacher).* These lines come from ABC's written feature, not from a transcript of the
broadcast; check them against the audio on the first playthrough and pencil in any difference.

**Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

One task per Level; each student works at one. Replay any segment on request.

**Level 4 (★):**

- Put these five events in the order they happened: the crew were winched down into the creek bed; the
  two men set up camp near the creek; a rock broke loose above Warren; Geert reached the ferry;
  Geert started walking down at first light.
- Write the outcome of the rescue in one sentence.
- Write the reason the rescue took as long as it did, in one sentence.
- Underline the two words in your ordered list that told you the order.
- **Answer note:** order is the two men set up camp near the creek / a rock broke loose above Warren /
  Geert started walking down at first light / Geert reached the ferry / the crew were winched down
  into the creek bed. Outcome: Warren was lifted out alive and taken to hospital, where both legs were
  amputated. Reason for the delay: accept the day and a half walk out, the ferry radio that could not
  reach the mainland, or the remoteness of the island in 1997. Accept any ordering words used.

**Level 5 (★★):**

- Summarize what happened from the rock falling to the helicopter, in four sentences, in order.
- In one sentence, say why this event mattered to Warren.
- He never says he was terrified. Find one thing he says that tells you what he was feeling under the
  rock anyway, and write it down.
- Write one sentence saying what that detail tells you, and why you read it that way.
- **Answer note:** the strongest candidates are the thought he reports having under the rock, that the
  thing he had been chasing was now going to take him out, and the line about lasting one more night
  but not being sure about a second. Both convey fear and a calculation about dying without a feeling
  word. Why it mattered: accept the loss of his legs, or the connection to the outdoors he thought he
  had lost and later got back. Accept any detail argued from something actually said.

**Level 6 (★★★):**

- Summarize the ordeal in four sentences.
- Warren was under the rock for more than 40 hours. Write down how much of those hours the account
  actually gives you, and how you can tell.
- Name one clearly missing piece of information that would change how one-sided this account is, and
  write one sentence on what it would tell us that this version does not.
- Geert walked for a day and a half. Write one sentence on what we are told about that walk, and one
  on what we are not.
- **Answer note:** almost none of the 40 hours is narrated. We get the moment of the crack, one
  remembered thought, one remembered sentence to Geert, and then Warren's next memory is the hospital,
  because he went in and out of consciousness. The account is built from the few moments he can
  actually recall. Strong missing pieces: what the hours themselves were like, what Geert saw when he
  left, what the crew found on arrival beyond the medical facts, what the surgeon weighed. Of Geert's
  walk we are told the duration, his exhaustion, the failed ferry radio and the crab fisherman's radio;
  we are told nothing about what he thought was happening behind him. Accept any argued from the audio.

**Level 7 (★★★★, uses two narrators):**

Three people in this story describe the same hours. Their own words are printed below, paired subject
by subject, so the comparison can be made from the page (Quality Standards C9). Warren was under the
rock; Geert walked out for help; Daniel Portefaix was on the rescue crew.

*The decision to climb the mountain.*
Warren: "I remember at one time being confronted with this thought of, 'Hey, is this what you've been
looking for? Is this connected enough for you?'"
Geert: "Within five or 10 minutes, he came to me and said, 'Yeah, you're on'." and "I felt guilty that
if it wasn't for me, he wouldn't have gone up that mountain."

*The moment it went wrong.*
Warren: "I pulled up to make my way up and over, and I just heard this almighty crack." and "A
refrigerator-sized piece of rock broke loose out of that wall and then just literally slammed me back
down into the creek bed - and I mean slammed."
Geert: "We had to go out into the mangroves, and Goodie found an old crab fisherman - he looked like
Ernest Hemingway in his 90s - and he had a big radio on board and that's how we got the mayday out."

*How close it came.*
Warren: "I said, 'I reckon I can last another night, but I'm not sure I can do a second night'."
Daniel: "At that stage is when Warren went in and out of consciousness. And that's when the real
potential was for him not to survive the rescue."

*Who was responsible.*
Warren: "... he might have asked me to go but I mean, I made the decision to go and ... we got through
it at the end of the day - he saved my life."
Geert: "I felt guilty that if it wasn't for me, he wouldn't have gone up that mountain."

- Ask: Warren and Geert describe the same decision and the same days. Using the printed words only,
  write two to three sentences on how each one builds his account differently, and what each one
  leaves a listener concluding about whose fault it was.
- Ask: identify one thing one of them wants the listener to believe that he never states outright.
  Write one to two sentences and point to the words that led you there.
- **Answer note:** Warren's account is built around his own choices, and every sentence about the
  decision puts himself as the agent: he decided, he made the decision, and the closing clause makes
  Geert the rescuer rather than the cause. Geert's account is built around consequence: the same
  decision appears as something he initiated, and the guilt sentence names cause and effect directly.
  On what is wanted but not stated: Warren's version works to release Geert from blame, which is why
  "he saved my life" lands at the end of the sentence about who asked whom rather than anywhere else;
  Geert's version keeps the blame he has been told he does not have to keep. Neither says any of that.
  Accept any reading argued from the printed words.

*Board-dependent moment.* With about six minutes left, take the sequence chain on the board and fill
it from the floor, one link at a time, calling on a different Level for each: Level 4 supplies the
order, Level 5 the moment under the rock, Level 6 the hours nobody narrates, Level 7 what the two men
do differently with the same decision. Then return to the Phase 1 tally and ask whether anyone would
now move to the other side. The completed chain exists only once every Level has spoken into it.

**Phase 5: Listening Closing Transfer Check (10 min)**

Read this aloud once, at normal pace. Students do not have it in front of them yet.

> My brother finally sold the van last spring. He had rebuilt the engine twice, and the second time he
> did it in a car park in the rain. When the buyer asked whether he was sad to see it go, he said he
> had already booked a flight for the money. He has never once said the van was a mistake. He just
> mentions, most times it comes up, how much the second rebuild cost him.

(79 words. Topic unrelated to the source. Recycles the lesson's skill rather than its hazard
vocabulary, since the target words are specific to a rescue.)

Each student tells a partner, out loud, one opinion the speaker states and one they only imply, and
what gives the implied one away. Cold-call two or three pairs. After the sharing, students may turn to
the printed script and check themselves against it.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
DAY 2 (Unit B - Speaking)   |--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Differentiated Practice (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Oral Output (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

The skill is **Giving Examples**, and this story runs on them: every speaker reaches for a concrete
instance rather than a general statement. Put these three real moments on the board:

- From **[Segment 1]**, Warren making a place vivid by naming one: "I've always explained Hinchinbrook
  as a bit like Jurassic Park."
- From **[Segment 2]**, Warren giving the size of the rock by comparison rather than in tonnes: "A
  refrigerator-sized piece of rock broke loose out of that wall ..."
- From **[Segment 3]**, Geert making a stranger real in one clause: "Goodie found an old crab
  fisherman - he looked like Ernest Hemingway in his 90s."

Say to the class, in these words:

> "Today we are practicing making a point land by giving one concrete example of it, instead of saying
> the general thing twice."

Draw out the point students will use all period: each of those examples does a job a general word
could not. "A big rock" tells you nothing; "refrigerator-sized" puts it in the room with you. A good
example is chosen for the listener, from something the listener already knows.

Sentence frames for the day, on the board:

- "For example, ___."
- "It was a bit like ___."
- "To give you an idea, ___."
- "Say, for instance, ___."
- "I mean, ___ - that kind of thing."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Students work in pairs, taking turns. The story for all Levels: **a time you had to make a decision
quickly with someone you did not know well.**

- **Level 4 (★):** narrate the sequence in four or five sentences, choosing your own ordering words.
  Say how it turned out and give one brief reason. Include at least one example, using a frame above.
- **Level 5 (★★):** narrate it as an extended account, six to eight sentences. Say why it mattered to
  you. Include one detail that shows how you felt at the moment of deciding, without naming the
  feeling, and at least two concrete examples.
- **Level 6 (★★★):** narrate it fluently without a fixed template, moving between what happened and
  what it meant. Quote at least one thing the other person actually said. Keep an eye on your partner,
  and when they look lost, change your pace or your wording once and keep going.
- **Level 7 (★★★★):** narrate it twice to two different partners, choosing different examples each
  time: once so the other person sounds like the one who decided, once so you do. Same events, same
  order. If your sequence comes out unclear, catch it and repair it out loud as you go.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

Feature: **thought-group pausing**. A narrated documentary is built out of it, and this one is a good
model because the speakers are recalling rather than reading, so the groups fall where the meaning is.

Replay a short stretch of **[Segment 2]** and have students mark, on a printed copy of one confirmed
line, where the speaker breaks the sentence into groups. Use this line, which has an obvious internal
break: "A refrigerator-sized piece of rock broke loose out of that wall / and then just literally
slammed me back down into the creek bed." Students mark their guess first, then listen and correct it.

They then read the line aloud twice, once with no pauses at all and once with their marked groups, and
say what changed for the listener. Finally they run one of their own Phase 2 sentences with deliberate
groups, and their partner reports where they heard the breaks.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - small-group discussion (20 min)**

Groups of four, from where students sit. Three rotated prompts, roughly six minutes each:

1. Geert says he felt guilty; Warren says Geert saved his life. Both are talking about the same walk
   down the mountain. Can both be right?
2. Warren says that given the chance he would not go back and change it. What do you think he means,
   and do you believe him?
3. Think of a decision you made with someone else where it went wrong. Whose decision was it, really?

Per-Level sentence stems:

- ★ "He says ___. For example, ___."
- ★★ "He never says ___, but you can hear it when he ___."
- ★★★ "Geert states his opinion outright; Warren mostly implies his, for example when he ___."
- ★★★★ "The effect of putting ___ at the end of that sentence is ___, which lets him avoid saying ___."

Every student not currently speaking writes down one example a group-mate gives, and whether it was
concrete enough to picture. Two of those get read out at the end of each prompt.

*Differentiated participation.* Students working at Level 4 rehearse one prepared line with a partner
in the first two minutes of each prompt and contribute it at a natural pause. Foundation Support, if
any student needs it: the student keeps the group's tally of stated versus implied opinions heard, and
reads the count out at the end.

*Board-dependent moment.* Keep a live two-column board record as groups report: opinions stated
outright, and opinions the class had to infer. It is built only from what is said in the room, and
Phase 5 refers back to it.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

Each student, in pairs and out loud, gives **an opinion about a place they know well, without ever
using an opinion word**, in three or four sentences, using at least one concrete example. The partner
says what the opinion was and which example gave it away. This prompt is not used anywhere else in
this Set. Cold-call two or three pairs; the partner reports, not the speaker. Nothing is collected.

*Respectful Tiers check across the cycle.* Level 4 had genuine interpretive work on Day 1 (naming the
outcome and the reason the rescue took as long as it did, not just ordering events) and a genuine
production role on Day 2 (a full narration of their own with a real example, rehearsed rather than
diminished, inside the same discussion).

Close with two minutes on the cycle itself: what makes an example land, and why a speaker might choose
to imply an opinion rather than state it.
