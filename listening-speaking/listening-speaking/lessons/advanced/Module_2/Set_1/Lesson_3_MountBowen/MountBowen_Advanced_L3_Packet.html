Unit 3A: Follow Me Up a Mountain

Listening & Speaking · Narrating Advanced 2.1.3.0

Objective: I can tell the difference between an opinion a speaker states outright and one they only let me hear underneath what they say.

Good to Know

Hinchinbrook Island is a mountainous national park island off the coast of Far North Queensland, Australia. Mount Bowen is its highest peak. Getting on or off the island means taking a boat.

The accident in this story happened in April 1997, before mobile phones were common and before emergency beacons were easy to get. That is why raising the alarm took as long as it did.

Words to Know

1. pinning (someone down) holding them in place so they cannot move at all.
2. broke loose came away suddenly from what was holding it.
3. raise the alarm tell people that something is badly wrong so help can start.
4. winched lowered or lifted by a motor-driven cable.
5. toxins harmful substances that build up in the body.
6. survivor guilt the feeling of having done wrong by coming through something others did not, or by being the reason someone else was harmed.

Unit 3B: Follow Me Up a Mountain

Listening & Speaking · Narrating Advanced 2.1.3.0

Objective: I can make a point land by giving one concrete example of it, instead of saying the general thing twice.

Learn the Phrase

Giving Examples

Everyone in this story reaches for a concrete instance instead of a general word. Three real ones: "I've always explained Hinchinbrook as a bit like Jurassic Park." "A refrigerator-sized piece of rock broke loose out of that wall ..." "Goodie found an old crab fisherman - he looked like Ernest Hemingway in his 90s."

Each of those does a job a general word could not. A big rock tells you nothing; refrigerator-sized puts it in the room with you. A good example is chosen for the listener, out of something the listener already knows.

  • •"For example, ___."
  • •"It was a bit like ___."
  • •"To give you an idea, ___."
  • •"Say, for instance, ___."
  • •"I mean, ___ - that kind of thing."

Practice It

Do one task below. Work in pairs and take turns. When you are done, find someone who worked on a different task and take turns sharing what you told.

Task A ★

Tell a true story of a time you had to make a decision quickly with someone you did not know well.

  1. 1.Tell the sequence in four or five sentences, choosing your own ordering words.
  2. 2.Say how it turned out, and give one brief reason it turned out that way.
  3. 3.Include at least one example, using one of the frames above.

Task B ★★

Tell a true story of a time you had to make a decision quickly with someone you did not know well.

  1. 1.Tell it as a longer account, six to eight sentences, and say why it mattered to you.
  2. 2.Include one detail that shows how you felt at the moment of deciding, without naming the feeling.
  3. 3.Include at least two concrete examples.

Task C ★★★

Tell a true story of a time you had to make a decision quickly with someone you did not know well.

  1. 1.Tell it without a fixed pattern, moving between what happened and what it meant.
  2. 2.Quote at least one thing the other person actually said.
  3. 3.Keep an eye on your partner. When they look lost, change your pace or your wording once, and keep going.

Task D ★★★★

Tell a true story of a time you had to make a decision quickly with someone you did not know well.

  1. 1.Tell it twice, to two different partners, choosing different examples each time: once so the other person sounds like the one who decided, once so you do.
  2. 2.Keep the same events in the same order both times.
  3. 3.If your order comes out unclear at any point, catch it and fix it out loud as you go.

Say It Like a Native Speaker

People do not speak in a flat line. They break a sentence into small groups of words and pause between them, and the groups fall where the meaning is. Mark with a slash where you think this speaker breaks the line, then listen and correct your marks: "A refrigerator-sized piece of rock broke loose out of that wall and then just literally slammed me back down into the creek bed."

Read the line aloud twice, once with no pauses at all and once with your marked groups, and say what changed for the listener. Then say one of your own sentences from the task above with deliberate groups, and ask your partner where they heard the breaks.

Discuss It

Get into groups of four and talk through these questions, one at a time, from where you sit. While someone else is speaking, write down one example they give and whether it was concrete enough to picture. You will read one of those out at the end of each question.

1. Geert says he felt guilty; Warren says Geert saved his life. Both are talking about the same walk down the mountain. Can both be right?

2. Warren says that given the chance he would not go back and change it. What do you think he means, and do you believe him?

3. Think of a decision you made with someone else where it went wrong. Whose decision was it, really?

Starter phrases:

  • ★ "He says ___. For example, ___."
  • ★★ "He never says ___, but you can hear it when he ___."
  • ★★★ "Geert states his opinion outright; Warren mostly implies his, for example when he ___."
  • ★★★★ "The effect of putting ___ at the end of that sentence is ___, which lets him avoid saying ___."

Wrap It Up

With a partner, take turns. Give an opinion about a place you know well, in three or four sentences, without ever using an opinion word. Use at least one concrete example. Your partner says what your opinion was and which example gave it away.

  • ★ "It's the kind of place where ___."
  • ★★ "To give you an idea, ___."
  • ★★★ "Most days you'll see ___, and by about ___ it's ___."
  • ★★★★ "I always tell people ___, and I let them work out the rest."
