## Target vocabulary (grounded in the real transcript)

1. **stripped** (of a screw) - worn smooth so a tool can no longer grip it and turn it.
2. **access panel** - a removable cover over the working parts of a machine, so someone can reach them.
3. **tether** - a line clipping a person to a fixed point so they cannot drift away.
4. **treacherous** - dangerous in a way that is easy to underestimate.
5. **swap out** - take one part off and put a replacement in its place.
6. **buttoned up** (figurative) - closed and sealed so tightly that nothing is meant to come apart.

**Figurative chunks to gloss (Advanced band):**

- **"hang in there"** - keep going through something hard; do not give up yet.
- **"a house call"** - someone coming out to where you are in order to help you.

**Background Note 1:** The Hubble Space Telescope orbits about 350 miles above the Earth. The

scientists can work out what distant planets and stars are made of.

**Background Note 2:** STS-125 was the last space shuttle mission sent to repair Hubble. Its crew

- **Answer note, Part 1 (What was broken):** **the instrument's power supply blew, and it sits behind a panel

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (12 min)**

Teach **Listen for Sequence Markers** directly. Model it on this real stretch from
**[Segment 3]**, read aloud from the transcript file or replayed:

He has just said that the two screws on the top came off easily. He goes straight on:

> "And there was one screw on the bottom, right, and that came out easily. And the fourth screw is
> not moving."

Point out what is doing the ordering work across those three screws. The markers are not the textbook
set (*first*, *second*, *finally*). They are position words (*on the top*, *on the bottom*), the
counting word *fourth*, and a tense switch from past (*came out*) into the present (*is not moving*)
at the moment the trouble starts. Name that switch: storytellers move into the present tense to slow a moment down
and put you inside it.

Guided practice on a second short excerpt, from **[Segment 4]**:

> "And for the next hour or so, we tried all kinds of things."

Ask: what does *for the next hour or so* do to an hour of work? It compresses it into one clause.
Sequence markers can stretch a moment or skip a long stretch, and noticing which is happening tells
you what the teller thinks matters. Students underline the marker and say which it is doing.

  two names in one clause, and treats the difficulty as a design constraint that was expected. **Background for the teacher, no longer a
  separate item:** NASA's record has an institutional stake in the day reading as a competent team
  outcome, which is why "brute force" appears as a neutral technique and no one is alone in it;
  Massimino's telling has a storyteller's stake in the listener feeling the isolation, which is why
  "No one can help me" stands unqualified at the exact point in the day when, as he says at the end of
  the full telling, a team on the ground was in fact working the problem. Accept any reading argued
  from the printed words.

*Board-dependent moment.* With about six minutes left, take the sequence chain on the board and fill
it from the floor, one link at a time, calling on a different Level for each: Level 4 supplies the
order, Level 5 the turn where the day changes, Level 6 who else was in the story, Level 7 what each
account leaves out. The completed chain exists only once every Level has spoken into it; no packet
contains it.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
DAY 2 (Unit B - Speaking)   |--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Differentiated Practice (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Oral Output (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

The skill is **Sequencing Language**, and the source is full of the real thing. Replay or read these
three real moments and put them on the board:

- From **[Segment 3]**: "And the first thing I had to do was to pull off or remove a handrail from
  the telescope that was blocking the access panel."
- From **[Segment 4]**: "And for the next hour or so, we tried all kinds of things."
- From **[Segment 6]**: "A few days later, we get back."

Say to the class, in these words:

> "Today we are practicing telling a true story of our own in an order a listener can follow, using
> words that mark what came first, what came next, and how much time went by."

Draw out the point students will use all period: his ordering words do two different jobs. Some open a
step (*the first thing I had to do was*). Others move time in a jump (*for the next hour or so*,
*a few days later*), skipping what does not matter. A story a listener can follow needs both, and
choosing which to use is choosing what your listener will think mattered.

Sentence frames for the day, on the board:

- "The first thing I had to do was ___."
- "And then ___."
- "For the next ___ , ___."
- "By the time ___ , ___."
- "A few ___ later, ___."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Every student narrates something real of their own. Students work in pairs; each takes a turn while
the other listens.

**The shared core, printed once above the tasks and not repeated inside any of them:** tell a true
story of a time you were fixing, building, or setting up something and it did not work the first
time; tell what happened in order, choosing your own ordering words; say how it turned out; and
include the part for your own task below.

**Each task adds one thing to the task above it.** No task states a sentence count; the shared core
sets the shape and each star's single addition is the only thing that changes. **The page no longer
says the ladder is cumulative** - that line was removed at the user's direction as clutter - so the
teacher, not the packet, is what tells a ★★★★ student they are also doing ★★★'s part. See consequence
3 below.

- **Level 4 (★):** one brief reason it turned out that way.
- **Level 5 (★★):** and why it mattered to you.
- **Level 6 (★★★):** and one action or detail that shows how you felt at the worst moment, without
  naming the feeling.
- **Level 7 (★★★★):** and ask your partner what they think you felt, and which detail told them.
  Reworded 2026-09-14 from "when you finish, your partner says ...", which read as a continuation of
  the task above it once the tasks became single standalone lines. Addressing it to the student also
  gives ★★★★ an action of its own, which partly answers consequence 2 below - the asking is the
  student's, even though the naming is still the partner's.

**Note on Day 2's tiers (2026-09-14).** This shape was set by the user directly, and two consequences
are recorded here rather than left to be found later.

1. **Levels 6 and 7 no longer carry tasks matching their own CSV rows.** Level 6's row is about
   narrating a complex event fluently, integrating reported speech and adjusting register or pacing to
   a listener reaction; Level 7's is about rhetorical control, shaping structure and emphasis for an
   audience effect, and repairing an unclear sequence out loud. What ★★★ and ★★★★ now ask is the
   Level 5 row's own demand - "one clearly telling action or detail that signals a feeling without
   naming it outright" - and its partner check. All four stars now sit on or below the anchor Level.
2. **★★★★ adds no demand on the speaker.** Its line is something the *partner* does after the telling,
   so a ★★★★ student produces exactly what a ★★★ student produces. If that is to be tightened later
   without disturbing the shape, the smallest change that would do it is to give ★★★★ the speaker's
   half of the same exchange - having to say, before the partner answers, which detail they expect to
   be picked out.
3. **★★★★ no longer stands on its own on the page.** Its line asks the partner to name the feeling and
   the detail that carried it, which presumes the speaker included such a detail - and that requirement
   lives in ★★★, one task up. With the cumulative line gone, nothing printed tells a ★★★★ student to do
   ★★★'s part first. Two ways to close it if wanted: put the cumulative sentence back in shorter form,
   or fold the requirement into ★★★★'s own line ("Include one action or detail that shows how you felt,
   without naming it. When you finish, your partner says what they think you felt and which detail told
   them."), which makes the task self-contained at the cost of repeating ★★★.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

Feature: **reduced forms in fast speech**, pointed at the day's own skill rather than at reduction in
general. The words that get squeezed hardest are the ordering words themselves, because they are what a
speaker rushes through to reach the next event, so the pronunciation work and the sequencing work land
on the same words.

**No replay is needed for this phase.** The packet prints the same stretch twice, as written and as
said, so the gap is on the page and students work from it directly:

> **Printed:** "And for the next hour or so, we tried all kinds of things. ... And then they called up
> after about an hour and 10 or 15 minutes of this, they said they wanted me to go to the front of the
> shuttle to a toolbox and get vice grips and tape."
>
> **Said:** "n' for the next hour or so, we tried all kindsa things. ... N'en they called up after
> 'bout an hour n' ten or fifteen minutes 'a this, they said they wanned me da go to the fronna the
> shuttle to a toolbox n' get vice grips n' tape."

The box is **noticing only**: it names what happens to the ordering words and shows it, and asks the
student to do nothing with it. The three tasks that used to follow it - count the differences, read the
Said line aloud twice, run one of your own sentences the same way - were removed 2026-09-14 at the
user's direction.

**Consequence, recorded rather than hidden:** Day 2's Phase 3 now has no student-facing task at all.
The ten-minute phase is the teacher naming the feature and the class looking at the two lines. Nothing
in `shared/` requires a pronunciation task specifically, so no rule is breached, but this is the only
phase in either day with nothing for a student to produce, and the packet box is now a display rather
than an exercise. The smallest way back, if it is ever wanted, is the third task alone: say one
sentence of your own story with the ordering word squeezed, which is the only one of the three that
produced speech rather than page-marking.

**Two notes for the teacher, both worth keeping straight.** The Printed line is The Moth's published
transcript, verbatim. **The Said line is a written model of ordinary fast-speech reduction, not a
transcription of this speaker's own delivery** - nobody has checked it against the audio, and it is
not claimed to be what he does sound like, only what these words sound like at speed. If the stretch
is played (roughly 7:30 by this lesson's proportional segment map, inside Segment 4), treat any
mismatch as interesting rather than as an error in the page. The phase does not depend on playing it.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Town Hall (20 min)**

Whole-class Town Hall from where students sit. Two rotated prompts, roughly nine minutes each:

1. Massimino spends more words on a few seconds at a window than on an hour of failed attempts. Was
   that the right call for a story? Would it be the right call for a report?
2. Think of a story you have heard someone tell about their own worst day at work. What did they leave
   out, and why do you think they left it out?

The prompt comparing NASA's record with the telling was removed 2026-09-14 at the user's direction.
Two prompts still satisfies Quality Standards §D7, which asks for two to three rotated prompts. The
★★★ stem went with it: it read "His version and NASA's version disagree less about what happened than
about ___," which had no surviving prompt to sit under, and is now pointed at prompt 1's story-versus-
report axis instead.

Per-Level sentence stems:

- ★ "First he ___, and then ___."
- ★★ "The part he spends longest on is ___, and I think that is because ___."
- ★★★ "A story and a report would disagree less about what happened than about ___."
- ★★★★ "The effect of putting ___ at that point in the story is ___, which serves ___."

Every student not currently speaking keeps a running tally in their packet of which prompt each
speaker actually answers and one phrase they used to order their points; two of those tallies get read
out at the end of each prompt.

*Differentiated participation.* Students working at Level 4 get a rehearsed pair-share in the first
two minutes of each prompt and contribute one prepared line at a natural pause, rather than competing
for an open floor turn. Foundation Support, if any student needs it: the student holds and reads out
the running tally sheet for one prompt, contributing the count aloud rather than an argument.

*Board-dependent moment.* Keep a live two-column board record as people speak: what the story does
well, and what the record does well. It is built only from what is actually said in the room, and
Phase 5 refers back to it.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

Each student, in pairs and out loud, narrates in three or four sentences **the last meal they cooked
or put together that did not go to plan**, using at least two ordering words and one time jump. This
prompt is not used anywhere else in this Set. Cold-call two or three pairs; each says their sequence
and names the ordering word they leaned on. Nothing is collected.

*Respectful Tiers check across the cycle.* Level 4 had genuine interpretive work on Day 1 (naming the
outcome and the reason given for it, not just ordering events) and a genuine production role on Day 2
(a full narration of their own, with a rehearsed turn inside the Town Hall rather than a diminished one).

Close with two minutes on the cycle itself: what makes a spoken story easy to follow, and what a
listener loses when a teller compresses an hour into four words.
