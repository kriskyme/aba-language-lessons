## Target vocabulary (grounded in the real transcript)

1. **stripped** (of a screw) - worn smooth so a tool can no longer grip it and turn it.
2. **access panel** - a removable cover over the working parts of a machine, so someone can reach them.
3. **tether** - a line clipping a person to a fixed point so they cannot drift away.
4. **treacherous** - dangerous in a way that is easy to underestimate.
5. **swap out** - take one part off and put a replacement in its place.
6. **buttoned up** (figurative) - closed and sealed so tightly that nothing is meant to come apart.

Context sentences (paraphrased from the source, not quoted verbatim):

1. Three of the four screws came out easily; the last one was stripped, and the tool just spun.
2. The failed power supply sat behind an access panel held down by 117 screws.
3. He knew his safety tether would hold him even if he pushed off too hard.
4. The route along the side of the shuttle was the treacherous part of the job.
5. When the power tool went flat, he went back to the airlock to swap out the battery.
6. The instrument had been buttoned up on the ground so that nothing would shake loose at launch.

**Figurative chunks to gloss (Advanced band):**

- **"hang in there"** - keep going through something hard; do not give up yet.
- **"a house call"** - someone coming out to where you are in order to help you.
- **"running out of ideas"** - close to having nothing left to try.

**Background Note 1:** The Hubble Space Telescope orbits about 350 miles above the Earth. The

scientists can work out what distant planets and stars are made of.

**Background Note 2:** STS-125 was the last space shuttle mission sent to repair Hubble. Its crew

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

*Mystery Quote hook (5 min).* Put this real line from the telling on the board with no context:

> "And my tool is moving, but the screw is not."

Ask the class, in pairs from where they sit, for two guesses: who is speaking, and what job are they
in the middle of? Take three or four guesses aloud. Do not confirm or deny yet; the answer arrives in
Phase 2. Write the guesses in a corner of the board and leave them up: Phase 2's sequence chain is
built next to them, and the contrast between guess and actual is the day's first payoff.

*Listening Skill Spotlight (3 min).* Say to the class, in these words:

> "Today we are practicing following a spoken story by tracking the words that mark the order of
> events, so we can say what happened first, next, and last even when a lot is going on."

That is the whole Spotlight. It names the skill, not this story, and it is the same sentence for every
task Level. Day 2's closing check comes back to this same skill.

*Good to Know (3 min).* Read out Background Notes 1 and 2 above. Informational only; nothing in the
day's tasks turns on them.

*Vocabulary (9 min).* Pre-teach the six target words and the three figurative chunks using the context
sentences above. All six words occur in the real telling. Take the three chunks explicitly at this
band rather than leaving them to context: each is transparent only if you already know it.

**Phase 2: Listen & Listening Notes (15 min)**

Say in one sentence what Segment 1 covers (he wanted to be an astronaut since seeing a film in 1984,
applied to NASA four times, and got in on the fourth try), then play **Segments 2 through 5**
straight through, without stopping. Tell students before you press play that this is a real person
telling a true story to a live audience, so they will hear laughter.

Build this **sequence chain** on the board as a shared organizer, and have students fill the same
chain in their packets while they listen:

```
What was broken  ->  Getting out there  ->  What went wrong  ->  What they tried  ->  What finally worked  ->  How it ended
```

Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback:

- **Level 4:** listen for the order. Which happened first, the dead battery or the stripped screw?
- **Level 5:** listen for the moment the day stops going to plan, and one thing he does or says right
  after it that tells you how he is feeling.
- **Level 6:** listen for what other people in the story do, and for whose side of the day you are
  actually getting.
- **Level 7:** listen for what he chooses to spend the most time on, and what he passes over quickly.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

Teach **Listen for Sequence Markers** directly. Model it on this real stretch from
**[Segment 3]**, read aloud from the transcript file or replayed:

He has just said that the two screws on the top came off easily. He goes straight on:

> "And there was one screw on the bottom, right, and that came out easily. And the fourth screw is
> not moving."

Point out what is doing the ordering work across those three screws. The markers are not the textbook
set (*first*, *second*, *finally*). They are position words (*on the top*, *on the bottom*), the
counting word *fourth*, and a tense switch from past (*came out*) into the present (*is not moving*)
at the moment the trouble starts. Name that switch: storytellers move into the present tense to slow a moment down
and put you inside it.

Guided practice on a second short excerpt, from **[Segment 4]**:

> "And for the next hour or so, we tried all kinds of things."

Ask: what does *for the next hour or so* do to an hour of work? It compresses it into one clause.
Sequence markers can stretch a moment or skip a long stretch, and noticing which is happening tells
you what the teller thinks matters. Students underline the marker and say which it is doing.

**Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

One task per Level; each student works at one. Replay any segment on request.

**Level 4 (★):**

- Put these five events in the order they happened: the battery goes dead; three screws come out
  easily; Houston tells him to get the tape; the fourth screw will not turn; the handrail comes off.
- Write the outcome of the day in one sentence.
- Write the reason he gives for that outcome in one sentence.
- Underline the two words in your ordered list that told you the order.
- **Answer note:** order is three screws come out easily / the fourth screw will not turn / Houston
  tells him to get the tape / the handrail comes off / the battery goes dead. Outcome: they finished
  the repair and the instrument worked again. Reason: the ground worked out that he could tape the
  handrail and pull it off with about sixty pounds of force. Accept any ordering words the student
  actually used.

**Level 5 (★★):**

- Summarize the repair in four sentences, in order.
- In one sentence, say why this day mattered to him.
- He never uses the word *ashamed* or *guilty*. Find one thing he does or says that tells you he feels
  something like it anyway, and write it down.
- Write one sentence saying what that detail tells you, and why you read it that way.
- **Answer note:** the strongest candidates are refusing to look up at the window ("I could not stand
  to even think of looking at him because I felt so bad about the way this day was going") and the
  imagined science books in which he is the man who broke Hubble. Both show the feeling through an
  action or an image rather than naming it. Why it mattered: accept either the repair itself or what
  he learned about not being alone. Accept any detail argued from something actually said.

**Level 6 (★★★):**

- Summarize the day in four sentences.
- Two other people are named as being right there: Mike Good, who he calls Bueno, and Drew Feustel.
  Write down everything the story actually tells you each of them did.
- Name one clearly missing piece of information that would change how one-sided this account is, and
  write one sentence on what it would tell us that this version does not.
- The ground was working the problem for over an hour. Write one sentence on how much of that hour
  this account shows you, and how you know.
- **Answer note:** Bueno hands tools and is twice reduced to a look; Drew reads the checklist and
  speaks four or five quoted lines. Neither gets an inner life. Strong missing pieces: what Bueno was
  actually doing for those hours, what the Goddard team tried and rejected, what the rest of the loop
  was saying. The hour is compressed into one clause ("for the next hour or so, we tried all kinds of
  things") while single seconds at the window get several sentences, so the account gives us his
  experience of the hour, not the hour. Accept any missing piece argued from the telling.

**Level 7 (★★★★, uses both sources):**

Both sources describe the same spacewalk. Their own words are printed below, paired subject by
subject, so the comparison can be made from the page (Quality Standards C9). Massimino is the
storyteller on The Moth stage; NASA is writing its own record of the same day.

*The screw that would not come out.*
Moth: "And the fourth screw is not moving. And my tool is moving, but the screw is not." and "And I
look closer, and I realize it's stripped."
NASA: "Though the removal of the many screws was expected to be difficult, a handrail gave Good and
Massimino trouble." and "Like the ACS, designers had not intended STIS for in-orbit repair, posing a
challenge to the astronauts as the fix required the removal of more than 100 screws."

*How the handrail finally came off.*
Moth: "They wanted me to use that tape to take the bottom of the handrail and then see if I could
yank it off the telescope." and "And bam. That thing comes right off."
NASA: "In addition to that time-consuming challenge, Massimino could not remove one of the handrails,
causing him to use brute force to remove it."

*Who was there.*
Moth: "There are billions of people down there, but there's no way I'm going to get a house call on
this one. No one can help me."
NASA: "In the 8 hour, 2 minute spacewalk, Massimino and Good repaired the Space Telescope Imaging
Spectrograph (STIS) by replacing a power supply board."

*How it ended.*
Moth: "They tried it. They turned it on from the ground." and "... The instrument had come back to
life."
NASA: "The initial aliveness test reported the STIS as working properly." and "They completed the
repair of the STIS although the tasks took much longer than planned, resulting in a spacewalk lasting
eight hours and two minutes."

- Ask: both accounts cover the same hours. Using the printed words only, write two to three sentences
  on how each one is built differently, and what each one leaves the listener concluding.
- Ask: identify one interest or assumption behind one of the two versions that the version itself
  never states outright. Write one to two sentences and point to the words that led you there.
- **Answer note:** structurally, the Moth telling is built around one person in difficulty and
  reaches its point through feeling: the hour compresses, the seconds at the window expand, and the
  crew exist mainly as a look and a voice. The NASA record is built around a task and a duration, puts
  two names in one clause, and treats the difficulty as a design constraint that was expected. On
  unstated interests: NASA's record has an institutional stake in the day reading as a competent team
  outcome, which is why "brute force" appears as a neutral technique and no one is alone in it;
  Massimino's telling has a storyteller's stake in the listener feeling the isolation, which is why
  "No one can help me" stands unqualified at the exact point in the day when, as he says at the end of
  the full telling, a team on the ground was in fact working the problem. Accept any reading argued
  from the printed words.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
DAY 2 (Unit B - Speaking)   |--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Differentiated Practice (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Oral Output (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

The skill is **Sequencing Language**, and the source is full of the real thing. Replay or read these
three real moments and put them on the board:

- From **[Segment 3]**: "And the first thing I had to do was to pull off or remove a handrail from
  the telescope that was blocking the access panel."
- From **[Segment 4]**: "And for the next hour or so, we tried all kinds of things."
- From **[Segment 6]**: "A few days later, we get back."

Say to the class, in these words:

> "Today we are practicing telling a true story of our own in an order a listener can follow, using
> words that mark what came first, what came next, and how much time went by."

Draw out the point students will use all period: his ordering words do two different jobs. Some open a
step (*the first thing I had to do was*). Others move time in a jump (*for the next hour or so*,
*a few days later*), skipping what does not matter. A story a listener can follow needs both, and
choosing which to use is choosing what your listener will think mattered.

Sentence frames for the day, on the board:

- "The first thing I had to do was ___."
- "And then ___."
- "For the next ___ , ___."
- "By the time ___ , ___."
- "A few ___ later, ___."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Every student narrates something real of their own. The prompt for all Levels: **a time you were
fixing, building, or setting up something and it did not work the first time.** Students work in
pairs; each takes a turn while the other listens.

- **Level 4 (★):** narrate the sequence in four or five sentences, choosing your own ordering words.
  Say how it turned out and give one brief reason it turned out that way.
- **Level 5 (★★):** narrate it as an extended account, six to eight sentences. Say why it mattered to
  you. Include one action or detail that shows how you felt at the worst moment without naming the
  feeling. Your partner's job afterwards is to say what they think you felt and which detail told them.
- **Level 6 (★★★):** narrate it fluently without a fixed template, moving between what happened and
  what it meant. Quote at least one thing somebody actually said to you. Keep an eye on your partner, and when
  they look lost or look ahead of you, change your pace or your wording once and keep going.
- **Level 7 (★★★★):** narrate it twice to two different partners, shaping it for a different effect
  each time: once so the listener finds it funny, once so the listener finds it tense. Same events, same
  order. If your sequence comes out unclear at any point, catch it and repair it out loud as you go
  ("sorry, that was after the part where ___").

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

Feature: **reduced forms in fast speech**, which this band is the first to take on and which this
speaker does constantly. Replay a short stretch of **[Segment 4]** and have students listen for the
gap between what the transcript prints and what the audio actually does: *going to* comes out as
"gonna," *kind of* as "kinda," *want to* as "wanna," and *and* flattens to a schwa at the front of
almost every sentence he starts.

Note for the teacher: The Moth's published transcript writes these out in full. That gap is the
teaching point, not an error in the transcript. Students mark five places in a short printed stretch
where they hear a reduced form, check against the audio, then read the same stretch aloud twice, once
fully enunciated and once reduced, to hear how much of natural pace comes from reduction. They then
run one of their own Phase 2 sentences the reduced way.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Town Hall (20 min)**

Whole-class Town Hall from where students sit. Three rotated prompts, roughly six minutes each:

1. Massimino spends more words on a few seconds at a window than on an hour of failed attempts. Was
   that the right call for a story? Would it be the right call for a report?
2. NASA's record and his telling describe the same day. If you only ever read one, what would you not
   know?
3. Think of a story you have heard someone tell about their own worst day at work. What did they leave
   out, and why do you think they left it out?

Per-Level sentence stems:

- ★ "First he ___, and then ___."
- ★★ "The part he spends longest on is ___, and I think that is because ___."
- ★★★ "His version and NASA's version disagree less about what happened than about ___."
- ★★★★ "The effect of putting ___ at that point in the story is ___, which serves ___."

Every student not currently speaking keeps a running tally in their packet of which prompt each
speaker actually answers and one phrase they used to order their points; two of those tallies get read
out at the end of each prompt.

*Differentiated participation.* Students working at Level 4 get a rehearsed pair-share in the first
two minutes of each prompt and contribute one prepared line at a natural pause, rather than competing
for an open floor turn. Foundation Support, if any student needs it: the student holds and reads out
the running tally sheet for one prompt, contributing the count aloud rather than an argument.

*Board-dependent moment.* Keep a live two-column board record as people speak: what the story does
well, and what the record does well. It is built only from what is actually said in the room, and
Phase 5 refers back to it.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

Each student, in pairs and out loud, narrates in three or four sentences **the last meal they cooked
or put together that did not go to plan**, using at least two ordering words and one time jump. This
prompt is not used anywhere else in this Set. Cold-call two or three pairs; each says their sequence
and names the ordering word they leaned on. Nothing is collected.

*Respectful Tiers check across the cycle.* Level 4 had genuine interpretive work on Day 1 (naming the
outcome and the reason given for it, not just ordering events) and a genuine production role on Day 2
(a full narration of their own, with a rehearsed turn inside the Town Hall rather than a diminished one).

Close with two minutes on the cycle itself: what makes a spoken story easy to follow, and what a
listener loses when a teller compresses an hour into four words.
