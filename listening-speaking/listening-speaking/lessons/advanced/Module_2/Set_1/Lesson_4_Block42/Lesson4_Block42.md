## Background Notes (Section 0.3 item 6, informational only, never tested)

**1. The "loyalty questionnaire."** In February 1943 the War Relocation Authority, the civilian agency
running the camps, required every incarcerated person over seventeen to fill out a form it used to
sort people into "loyal" and "disloyal." Two of its questions were the contested ones. Tanimoto calls
it "this 'loyalty' papers" and "that particular paper." (Densho Encyclopedia, "Tule Lake"; National
Park Service, Tule Lake National Monument timeline.)

**2. Blocks.** Tule Lake was laid out in numbered blocks. Each block had its own barracks, its own
mess hall where residents ate together, and a **block office** where paperwork was dropped off.
Tanimoto lived in Block 42. Roughly two to three dozen men there refused the form. (Densho
Encyclopedia, "Tule Lake.")

**3. "Kibei," and the man Tanimoto cannot place.** A **Kibei** was a US-born Japanese American sent to
Japan for schooling and later returned to the United States. In Segment 16 Tanimoto is responding to
an article, sent to him before the interview, that named a "number one troublemaker" said to have
organized Block 42's refusal. He disputes that such a person was there. Students do not need the
article to follow the segment; they need only to know he is arguing with something he has read.

## Target vocabulary (grounded in the verified text)

All six appear in the played span. Pre-teach with these context sentences, which paraphrase what the
source says rather than quoting it at length.

| Word or phrase | Gloss | Context sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **pressure** | pushing someone hard to do something, without forcing them outright | There was pressure from the office to sign the form, but no one was dragged to the desk. |
| **surrounded** | standing all the way around something so there is no way out | By the time they finished dinner, soldiers surrounded the whole block. |
| **refuse ... period** | say no, and treat the matter as closed | They would not sign yes and they would not sign no. They refused, period. |
| **make up your own mind** | decide for yourself, without being told or organised | Nobody held a meeting. Each man made up his own mind. |
| **count off** | say numbers in turn along a line so a group can be split | The soldier told them to count off, one, two, and then split the ones from the twos. |
| **cart away** | take a group of people off somewhere, quickly and without ceremony | More than thirty men were sorted out and carted away to county jails. |

**Opaque chunks, glossed explicitly at this band rather than left to context.** Two are taken
directly:

- **"..., period."** placed after a statement means *and there is nothing more to discuss*. It is the
  speaker closing the subject, not a full stop being read aloud.
- **"went out of his way to"** means *made a special effort to*, and is nearly always used about
  effort a person did not have to make. Tanimoto uses it in the negative: the quiet man "never went
  out of his way to antagonize anybody."

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

Teach **Listen for Contrastive/Concession Language** directly. This speaker turns against himself
constantly, which is what makes the span a good model. Put both models on the board.

Model 1, a plain contrast, **[Segment 17]**:

> "There were some people in our block that did sign, but there was a lot of people in our block that
> wouldn't sign."

Two groups, one sentence, joined by *but*. The second half is the half he is actually reporting; the
first half is there to measure it against. Name the move: the information after *but* is the
information the speaker wants you to keep.

Model 2, a concession, **[Segment 17]**:

> "Well, there was some pressure, I don't know if it was warnings or not, but there was some pressure
> that we should sign 'yes' or 'no.'"

Harder, and worth the time. He states something, immediately gives ground on it ("I don't know if it
was warnings or not"), then returns to it anyway. That is concession: admitting the limit of your own
claim without dropping the claim. Give students the signal words to listen for, and note that in
speech the giveaway is often a hesitation before the word rather than the word itself: **but,
although, even though, regardless, still, I don't know if ..., or whatever it was**.

*Brief guided practice (3 min).* Replay roughly the last minute of **[Segment 16]** and have students
raise a hand each time they hear a turn. They will catch "But I wasn't charged with anything," which
is the pivot the whole segment rests on. Do not explain what it means yet; Phase 4 is where that is
worked out.

### Level 4 task (one star)

> CSV row, quoted verbatim: "Can listen to a short spoken narration of a sequence of past events,
> choosing their own time connectors, and identify the order, one outcome, and the brief reason given
> for that outcome."

1. Put these six events in the order Tanimoto tells them, numbering 1 to 6. They are printed
   scrambled: *the men count off one and two; forms are dropped at the block office; the men are taken
   to Klamath Falls and Alturas; soldiers surround the block; most of the block refuses to sign;
   someone from the WRA office comes to collect the forms.*

   **Answer note:** forms dropped at the block office (1); most of the block refuses to sign (2);
   someone from the WRA office comes to collect (3); soldiers surround the block (4); the men count
   off one and two (5); taken to Klamath Falls and Alturas (6).

2. Write the sequence out as three sentences, using your own time connectors. Do not use *first,
   then, finally* in that order; choose connectors that fit what you are saying.

   **Answer note:** any accurate three-sentence account with self-chosen connectors. Accept *after
   that, once, by the time, in the end, the next thing*. The connectors are the assessed part, not the
   vocabulary.

3. What happened to the men in the end, and what reason does Tanimoto give for it happening?

   **Answer note:** they were taken away to county jails in Klamath Falls and Alturas. The reason he
   gives is simply that they would not sign the form: not that they signed no, but that they refused
   to sign either way.

4. Tanimoto says the soldiers were not interested in his parents, only in the younger men. Why do you
   think the soldiers made that choice? Answer in one sentence. There is no wrong answer that uses
   what he says.

   **Answer note:** interpretive, not retrieval. He says the soldier picked out those who were, "in
   his eyes, draft age," so the strongest answers connect the round-up to military service rather than
   to who had refused. Accept any answer grounded in the draft-age line.

### Level 5 task (two stars)

> CSV row, quoted verbatim: "Can listen to an extended spoken narration of a personal event, summarize
> the sequence and why it mattered, and identify one clearly telling action or detail that signals how
> the speaker felt, even though the speaker never names the feeling directly."

1. Summarise the whole span in four to five sentences: what arrived, what the block did, what was done
   about it, and where it ended.

   **Answer note:** a form arrived at the block office; most of Block 42 would not sign it either way;
   after pressure from the WRA office, military police surrounded the block during dinner; the younger
   men were counted off into two groups as they left the mess hall; the two groups went to jails in
   Klamath Falls and Alturas.

2. In one sentence, say why this mattered to him, using what he says rather than what you assume.

   **Answer note:** he was being asked to prove something to a government that was holding him without
   charge, and signing would have accepted that position. His own formulation is that as long as he
   was a prisoner he would not sign anything, and that he would sign gladly once sent home.

3. Tanimoto never names a feeling anywhere in this span. Find **one** action or detail he describes
   that tells you how he felt that evening, write it down, and write what it tells you.

   **Answer note:** several work, and the item is graded on the link rather than the choice. The
   strongest is the soldier going into the empty mess hall to look around and satisfy himself nobody
   was left, which Tanimoto reports without comment and which registers the search as something done
   to him. Also accept "I don't know which number I was," where the arbitrariness of which jail he
   went to is left standing without protest, and the bayonets being mentioned in the same flat tone as
   the dinner.

4. Where in your summary did the second listen change what you wrote? Mark that sentence.

   **Answer note:** no fixed answer; this marks the ground Phase 5 checks. Most commonly the order of
   the soldier's actions, or the detail that they were still eating when the block was surrounded.

### Level 6 task (three stars)

> CSV row, quoted verbatim: "Can listen to a complex or nuanced narrative and identify one clearly
> missing piece of information (e.g. \"they never mention what the other person said\") that suggests
> the account may be one-sided, in addition to summarizing events."

1. Summarise the span in three sentences.

   **Answer note:** as Level 5 item 1, compressed.

2. Identify **one** clearly missing piece of information in this account, and say in one sentence what
   its absence does to the telling.

   **Answer note:** the strongest is that nobody from the WRA or the army is ever quoted saying
   anything except the soldier's "Get over there" and "count off, one, two." We are told there was
   pressure, but never what the pressure actually consisted of or who applied it, so the reader cannot
   judge whether it was a warning or a threat, which is a distinction Tanimoto himself raises and then
   leaves open. Also accept: we never hear what the people in the block who *did* sign said or what
   happened to them; we never hear his parents' or brothers' reaction at the moment of the round-up.

3. He says twice that there was no meeting and that everyone made up their own mind. What would you
   need to hear, and from whom, before you could treat that as settled?

   **Answer note:** a second Block 42 resident, or anyone who was in the block and did sign. The point
   is that the only witness to the absence of a meeting is the person whose account the absence of a
   meeting supports.

4. Write one question you would ask him if he were here, designed to get at what this account leaves
   out. It must be answerable by him, not by a historian.

   **Answer note:** accept any question aimed at the gap the student named in item 2 and directed at
   his own experience. Reject questions that ask him to confirm a fact he cannot know, such as what
   the camp director intended.

### Level 7 task (four stars)

> CSV row, quoted verbatim: "Can listen to two extended narratives covering the same or related
> events, critically assess how each storyteller's structural choices shape what the listener is meant
> to conclude, and identify at least one unstated motive behind one teller's version that isn't
> directly admitted."

Both tellers' own words are printed below, paired subject by subject. Read them against the recording
you have just heard.

**Subject A: why the men would not sign.**

> **Tanimoto, [Segment 16]:** "My reason for not signing was I was a prisoner. I never did anything
> wrong, I wasn't convicted."
>
> **Densho Encyclopedia, "Tule Lake":** "Pressure to answer ambiguous questions within a fixed
> deadline without adequate opportunity to discuss and evaluate them led to anger and civil
> disobedience."

**Subject B: whether the refusal was organised.**

> **Tanimoto, [Segment 16]:** "Everybody, I think, made up their own mind, and pretty much we didn't
> discuss it too often ... But we never had an organized meeting saying that, 'Hey, we're not gonna
> sign this thing.'"
>
> **Densho Encyclopedia, "Tule Lake":** "Several dozen young men in Block 42 refused to answer the
> questionnaire despite threats of $10,000 fines or twenty years in prison or both."

**Subject C: the arrest and what followed.**

> **Tanimoto, [Segment 17]:** "So one evening, after dinner, our block was surrounded by military
> police. They had rifles with bayonets on them."
>
> **Densho Encyclopedia, "Tule Lake":** "For refusing to cooperate they were imprisoned in Alturas and
> Klamath Falls County jails, but since there were no criminal charges, the protesters were removed to
> Tule Lake isolation center where the Constitution did not apply."

**Subject D: what caused the crisis.**

> **Tanimoto, [Segment 16]:** "I know in Tule Lake that eventually they got around to passing out this
> 'loyalty' papers."
>
> **Densho Encyclopedia, "Tule Lake":** "The infamous loyalty questionnaire was mishandled at Tule
> Lake, causing widespread dissent."

1. Each teller puts a different thing in the position of *cause*. Name what causes the refusal in each
   account, in one sentence each.

   **Answer note:** in the encyclopedia the cause is administrative: ambiguous questions, a fixed
   deadline, no chance to discuss, all of it summed up in "mishandled" and "mismanagement." In
   Tanimoto's telling the cause is his own legal position, that he was held without charge, which
   would have been just as true had the form been perfectly written.

2. Each account leaves out something the other supplies. Name one thing each is missing, and say what
   the reader is meant to conclude as a result.

   **Answer note:** the encyclopedia supplies the threatened penalties, which Tanimoto never mentions,
   so his refusal reads as far less dangerous in his own telling than it was; his account supplies the
   bayonets and the counting off, which the encyclopedia compresses into "imprisoned," so the
   institutional version loses the fact that this was done by armed men to people who were eating
   dinner.

3. Identify **one unstated motive** behind one teller's version, and give the evidence in the words
   printed above.

   **Answer note:** two are available and either is accepted with evidence. Tanimoto's: he returns
   repeatedly to there having been no meeting and no organiser, which reads as a direct refutation of
   the published claim that a "number one troublemaker" organised Block 42, a claim he was handed
   before the interview and never names as the thing he is answering. The encyclopedia's: by placing
   the cause in mismanagement it makes the episode a failure of administration rather than a
   constitutional claim by the men, which serves an account in which the camp system, not the
   refusers, is the thing under judgment.

4. One of these tellers was in the room when the other was recorded. Knowing that, which of your
   answers above would you now qualify, and why? One sentence.

   **Answer note:** the single bounded reflection question this band is allowed, tied to the student's
   own named answer. Strongest answers qualify item 3: if the encyclopedia's author was the secondary
   interviewer, then the questions that prompted Tanimoto's denials may themselves have shaped how
   often he issued them, so the repetition is weaker evidence of his motive than it first appears.
   Accept any answer that names one of its own earlier claims and weakens it for a stated reason.

**Phase 5: Check It Against the Source (7 min)**

This is Quality Standards Section D12's Verify and Return beats. It runs **only after every Level's
answers are committed and revised**, and nothing below appears on the page before that point.

Two windows are named here rather than one because this is the Advanced band and the two carry
different features. Both are verbatim, contiguous, and under eighty words.

**Window 1, [Segment 17].** Chosen because Level 4's ordering item and Level 5's telling-detail item
both turn on it, and because the false start is the kind of thing this band hears once and smooths
away:

> "So one evening, after dinner, our block was surrounded by military police. They had rifles with
> bayonets on them, and they surrounded our whole block. And we were having dinner when they did this,
> and as we came out the door, there was a soldier, there was a soldier standing by the door, and he
> directed us to..."

Feature: the repeated false start, "there was a soldier, there was a soldier," plus the linking across
"surrounded by" and "came out the door." Students who wrote a single clean soldier sentence have
smoothed a hesitation out of the record.

**Window 2, [Segment 16].** Chosen because Level 5's and Level 7's items both rest on it, and because
it carries a different feature entirely, the weak forms that make the concession pivot easy to miss:

> "My reason for not signing was I was a prisoner. I never did anything wrong, I wasn't convicted. If
> I was charged with something and I had a trial and I was convicted that I did this, whatever it was,
> and sentenced to jail, that's fine. But I wasn't charged with anything"

Feature: *wasn't* twice reduced almost to a single beat, and the pivot on *But* arriving after a long
conditional that sounds like agreement. Students who heard "that's fine" as concession and stopped
there missed that the sentence turns immediately after it.

**What students do (4 min).** Each student reads the window that matches where their answers moved in
Phase 4, against **what they actually wrote**, and marks in one line where their wording and his
diverge. That gap is the content of this phase. It is not graded, it is not a comprehension question,
and no new task is built on it.

**Return unsupported (3 min), required.** Replay just those two stretches once with the windows
**covered**. The packet prints the cover instruction. Without this beat the phase ends on reading and
the lesson has taught the eye rather than the ear.

Neither window is the basis of a scored item here or in this Set's assessment.

**Phase 6: Listening Closing Transfer Check (8 min)**

*Mechanism: a short script the teacher reads aloud.* It is invented, not found. Its topic is unrelated
to the recording, and it recycles most of the lesson's target vocabulary so the skill is tested rather
than the content. Students do not read it first. It is printed in the packet only after the response

> **Read aloud once, at normal speed:** "The allotment holders met on Tuesday about the new gate lock.
> There was some pressure from the committee to sign the new key agreement, and I do not know if it
> was a deadline or just a suggestion, but there was pressure. Some people signed. A lot of us
> refused, period. We never held a meeting about it. Everyone made up their own mind. By Friday the
> committee had surrounded the whole issue with so many forms that two of the old sheds got carted
> away before anyone could count off who still wanted one."

(84 words.)

Students then do the following, out loud to a partner: **say the two places where the speaker turns
against what they have just said, and say what each turn tells you.** That is the Phase 1 skill
applied to new material.

**Answer note:** the two turns are "I do not know if it was a deadline or just a suggestion, but there
was pressure," which concedes the limit of the claim and then keeps the claim, and "Some people
signed. A lot of us refused," which is the same two-group contrast as Model 1. A third, weaker turn
sits in "We never held a meeting about it. Everyone made up their own mind," where the contrast is
implied rather than signalled.

Cold-call two or three pairs. **Demonstration, not self-report:** students perform the skill on the
new script rather than saying whether they found it useful. After sharing, students may read the
script to check.

any unanswered W entries up; Day 2 Phase 4 uses them.

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

Skill: **Asking for Clarification.** This source carries the skill in its own audio and needs no
deviation flag. The interview is two people repeatedly working to get a narrative straight, and all
three moves below are real, quoted from the played span.

Say to the class, in these words:

> "Today we are practicing asking for clarification: getting someone to give you the part of the story
> you did not get, without making them start again."

Then play or read the three moves and name what each one does. They are three different tools, not
three versions of one.

*Move 1, rewind. Barbara Takei, **[Segment 17]**:*

> "Can we back up a little bit, and what your recollections are when you first heard of and saw that
> 'loyalty questionnaire.'"

She does not say she is lost. She names a point earlier in the story and asks him to restart from
there. Useful when you missed a whole stage.

*Move 2, narrow. Tom Ikeda, **[Segment 17]**:*

> "But Jim, before that, I mean, what was the reaction of the administration? Did they, were there any
> steps before carting you off?"

He holds the story still and asks for one specific missing piece, then narrows again when the first
try is vague. Useful when you have the events but not the detail.

*Move 3, check by restating. Tom Ikeda, **[Segment 17]**:*

> "So one group, like the ones went to one place, and the twos went to the other place, Klamath Falls
> and Alturas."

Not a question in form at all. He says back what he thinks he heard and lets the speaker confirm or
correct it, which Tanimoto immediately does. Useful when you think you have it and want to be sure.
This move is also the bridge into Phase 3, because it only works as a clarification request if the
intonation rises.

Phase 5 asks students to reproduce this exact skill.

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Every Level narrates. The three clarification moves above are available to every Level and are printed
once in the packet, before the tasks.

---

### Level 4 task (one star)

> CSV row, quoted verbatim: "Can narrate a short original sequence of past events including one
> outcome and a brief reason for it, choosing their own connectors, working independently."

In pairs, take turns. Narrate a short true sequence from your own life in which **you were asked to
put your name to something**: a form, a petition, a register, a group message, a receipt. Three or
four sentences, your own time connectors, ending with what happened and one reason it happened.

The listener uses **Move 2** at least once: hold the story still and ask for one specific missing
piece. Then swap.

**Answer note:** assessed on the connectors being chosen rather than recited, and on there being a
stated outcome with a reason attached. A minimal passing narration: "Last term the class rep passed
round a sheet for a trip. I signed it without reading it. By the time I read it I had agreed to pay a
deposit, so I had to go."

---

### Level 5 task (two stars)

> CSV row, quoted verbatim: "Can narrate an extended account of a personal event, stating why it
> mattered and including at least one clearly telling action or detail that signals a feeling without
> naming it outright, working independently."

In pairs, narrate an extended account of a time **you disagreed with a rule and had to decide whether
to go along with it.** Say why it mattered. Include at least one action or detail that shows how you
felt **without naming the feeling**, the way Tanimoto never names one.

The listener's job is to name, afterwards, which detail was doing that work. If they cannot find one,
the narrator adds one and tells it again from that point.

**Answer note:** the assessed part is the planted detail and the listener being able to find it.
Naming the feeling anywhere in the account is the one thing that fails the task. Accept details of the
Tanimoto kind: what the speaker checked, counted, re-read, avoided, or did not say.

---

### Level 6 task (three stars)

> CSV row, quoted verbatim: "Can narrate a complex event fluently, integrating reported speech and
> shifting between what happened and its significance without a fixed template, adjusting register or
> pacing at least once in response to a specific listener reaction, using complex sentence structures
> with sustained control."

In threes: one narrator, one listener, one tracker. Narrate a complex event in which **you and someone
else remembered the same thing differently.** Requirements: report at least two things somebody
actually said, in reported speech; move between what happened and why it mattered more than once,
without doing it in a fixed order.

The listener reacts genuinely and visibly at one point, by leaning in, by looking lost, or by
interrupting with a clarification move. **The narrator must adjust register or pacing in response**,
and the tracker writes down where that happened and what changed.

**Answer note:** the adjustment is the assessed item and it must be visibly caused by the listener,
not scheduled. Typical successful adjustments: slowing and adding a gloss after a lost look;
dropping into more informal register after the listener leans in; backing up to name a person the
listener clearly had not placed. The tracker's note is the evidence.

---

### Level 7 task (four stars)

> CSV row, quoted verbatim: "Can narrate with rhetorical control, deliberately shaping structure and
> emphasis for a specific audience effect, self-monitoring and smoothly repairing at least one point
> where the sequence of events comes out unclear."

In pairs. Narrate the same true event **twice**, to two different stated audiences, shaping structure
and emphasis differently each time. Name the audience before each telling: for example, a friend who
will take your side, then a committee deciding whether you were in the right.

Requirements: the two versions must differ in **what comes first** and in **what is given the most
time**, not merely in politeness. You must also **repair at least one point aloud** where your own
sequence comes out unclear, and the repair must be smooth, not an apology.

Afterwards, your partner says which version they found more convincing and names the structural choice
that did it.

**Answer note:** assessed on the reordering being deliberate and defensible, and on the repair being a
real one. A repair is "sorry, I have got that the wrong way round, the letter came after the meeting,
which matters because ..." and not "wait, no, hang on." The partner's naming of the structural choice
is the check that the shaping was actually perceptible.

---

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

Feature: **rising and falling intonation for questions versus statements.** It is genuinely audible in
this source and it is the thing that makes Phase 1's Move 3 work at all. Lessons 1, 2, and 3 of this
Set used reduced forms, contrastive stress, and thought-group pausing respectively, so this is new
here.

Replay Ikeda's restatement in **[Segment 17]**, "So one group, like the ones went to one place, and
the twos went to the other place, Klamath Falls and Alturas." On the page it is a statement and ends
in a full stop. In the audio the pitch rises at the end, which is the only thing turning it into a
request for confirmation. Replay it twice and let students hear the lift.

Then contrast it with Tanimoto's reply in the same segment, "Yeah. I don't know which group went
where, but that's the way they separated them," where the pitch falls and closes the subject.

*Students mark and check (4 min).* The packet prints both lines with no arrows. Students mark an
arrow up or down at the end of each, then check against the real audio on one replay.

*Applied rehearsal (4 min).* In pairs, students take one sentence from their own Phase 2 narration and
say it twice: once falling, as a statement of what happened, and once rising, as a check that their
partner has followed. The partner reports which was which with their eyes closed, so the judgment is
made on the sound alone.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output (20 min)**

Protocol: **Jigsaw Expert Panels**, run entirely from where students sit. Nobody moves furniture and
nothing is posted around the room.

*Setup (3 min).* Four expert groups, formed from whoever is sitting together. Each group takes one
question and becomes the group that has thought hardest about it. All four questions are about the
recording's actual subject:

1. What did Block 42 actually refuse to do, and why does the difference between refusing and answering
   "no" matter?
2. What does Tanimoto's account let you see that an official record would not?
3. What does the encyclopedia's account let you see that Tanimoto's does not?
4. Which of the class's unanswered **W** questions from Day 1 can this recording actually settle, and
   which would need a different source?

*Expert round (7 min).* Each group prepares a position and one piece of evidence from the recording,
and every member must be able to give it.

*Panel round (10 min).* One member of each group speaks in turn as that group's panel; the rest of the
class questions them **using the three clarification moves from Phase 1**, which stay printed in the
packet.

Per-Level stems, printed for the questioners:

- **One star:** "Can you go back to ...?" (Move 1)
- **Two stars:** "Before that, what happened when ...?" (Move 2)
- **Three stars:** "So what you are saying is ... is that right?" (Move 3, and it must rise)
- **Four stars:** "You have used ... as your evidence. What would have to be true for that to be wrong?"

**Differentiated participation.** Level 4 students get a **rehearsed pair-share before the public
round**: they practise their one question with a neighbour during the expert round and ask that
prepared question in the panel round. Foundation Support students take the tracking role described
below, which is verbal but minimal and has a printed form.

**Every non-speaker has an active task.** While a panel speaks, everyone else records, on a printed
two-column form, each clarification move they hear and whether it got the speaker to add something
new. Foundation Support students do only this, marking a tick or a cross.

**Board-dependent moment.** Keep a running board record of the panels' claims **as they are actually
spoken**, in the speakers' own words. That record is what Phase 5 refers back to and it cannot be
written in advance.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

*Transfer (6 min).* In pairs, out loud, every student produces **one new instance of Asking for
Clarification** on a prompt that has nothing to do with the recording and that has not been used
anywhere else in this Set:

> Your partner describes how they got to school or work this morning, in about four sentences,
> deliberately leaving one stage of the journey vague. You must get the missing stage out of them using
> **one** of the three moves, and then say which move you used.

Swap, and the second student must use a **different** move from the first. Cold-call two or three
pairs to perform theirs for the class. **Demonstration, not self-report:** nobody is asked whether
they found the skill useful.

**Answer note:** the assessed part is that the move chosen actually fits the gap. Move 1 for a missing
stage, Move 2 for a missing detail inside a stage, Move 3 for something heard but not trusted. A
student who uses Move 3 on something they genuinely did not hear has used the wrong tool, and that is
the teaching point when it happens.

*Respectful Tiers check across the cycle.* Level 4 had a genuine interpretive item on Day 1 (Phase 4
item 4, on why the soldiers sorted by age) and a genuine production role on Day 2 (a narration of
their own with a real outcome and reason, plus a prepared panel question). Not fact-retrieval and
silent participation.

*Wrap (4 min).* Brief whole-class reflection on the cycle: what the class now knows that it listed
under **W** on Day 1, and which questions this one man's account could never have settled on its own.
