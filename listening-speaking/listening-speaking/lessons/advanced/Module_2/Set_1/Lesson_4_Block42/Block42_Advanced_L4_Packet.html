Unit 4A: The Block That Would Not Sign

Listening & Speaking · Narrating Advanced 2.1.4.2

Objective: I can listen for the words a speaker uses to turn against what they have just said, so I can hear both halves of what they mean.

Good to Know

In February 1943 the United States government was holding about 120,000 people of Japanese descent in camps. Most were American citizens. That year the agency running the camps required every person over seventeen to fill out a form it used to sort people into "loyal" and "disloyal." People called it the "loyalty questionnaire."

The camp in this recording, Tule Lake, was laid out in numbered blocks. Each block had its own barracks, its own dining hall where residents ate together, and a block office where paperwork was dropped off. The speaker lived in Block 42. Roughly two to three dozen men there refused the form.

A Kibei was a Japanese American born in the United States, sent to Japan for schooling, and later returned. In the first part of the recording the speaker is arguing with an article he was sent before the interview, which claimed one man organised his block's refusal. He does not think that man was there.

Words to Know

Word bank: pressure · surrounded · refuse ... period · make up your own mind · count off · cart away · go out of your way to · antagonize · barracks
  1. 1. There was pressure from the office to sign the form, but no one was dragged to the desk.
  2. 2. By the time they finished dinner, soldiers surrounded the whole block.
  3. 3. They would not sign yes and they would not sign no. They refused, period.
  4. 4. Nobody held a meeting. Each man made up his own mind.
  5. 5. The soldier told them to count off, one, two, and then split the ones from the twos.
  6. 6. More than thirty men were sorted out and carted away to county jails.

Two phrases worth taking slowly

"..., period." placed after a statement means and there is nothing more to discuss. It is the speaker closing the subject. It is not a full stop being read out.

"went out of his way to" means made a special effort to, and it is nearly always used about effort a person did not have to make. In this recording it appears in the negative: a quiet man who never went out of his way to antagonize anybody.

Hearing a speaker turn

Speakers often say one thing and then turn against it in the same breath. The turn is where the meaning is.

A plain contrast. "There were some people in our block that did sign, but there was a lot of people in our block that wouldn't sign." Two groups, one sentence, joined by but. What comes after but is what the speaker wants you to keep.

A concession. "Well, there was some pressure, I don't know if it was warnings or not, but there was some pressure that we should sign 'yes' or 'no.'" He states something, gives ground on it, then returns to it anyway. He admits the limit of his own claim without dropping the claim.

Listen for: but · although · even though · regardless · still · I don't know if ... · or whatever it was. In speech the giveaway is often a hesitation just before the word.

Unit 4B: The Block That Would Not Sign

Listening & Speaking · Narrating Advanced 2.1.4.2

Objective: I can ask for the part of a story I did not get, without making the speaker start again.

Three ways to ask for what you missed

All three are real. The two interviewers in the recording use them to get the story straight. They are three different tools, not three versions of one.

1. Rewind. "Can we back up a little bit, and what your recollections are when you first heard of and saw that 'loyalty questionnaire.'" She does not say she is lost. She names a point earlier in the story and asks him to restart from there. Use it when you missed a whole stage.

2. Narrow. "But Jim, before that, I mean, what was the reaction of the administration? Did they, were there any steps before carting you off?" He holds the story still and asks for one specific missing piece, then narrows again when the first try is vague. Use it when you have the events but not the detail.

3. Check by restating. "So one group, like the ones went to one place, and the twos went to the other place, Klamath Falls and Alturas." Not a question in form at all. He says back what he thinks he heard and lets the speaker confirm or correct it. Use it when you think you have it and want to be sure. Your voice has to rise at the end or it is not a question.

Practice It

Do one task below. When you are done, find someone who worked on a different task and take turns sharing what you found.

Task A ★

  1. 1. In pairs, take turns. Tell a short true story from your own life about a time you were asked to put your name to something: a form, a petition, a register, a group message, a receipt. Three or four sentences. Choose your own words to show when things happened. End with what happened and one reason it happened.
  2. 2. When you are the listener, use tool 2 at least once. Hold the story still and ask for one specific missing piece. Write the question you asked.

Task B ★★

  1. 1. In pairs, tell a longer account of a time you disagreed with a rule and had to decide whether to go along with it. Say why it mattered.
  2. 2. Include at least one action or detail that shows how you felt without naming the feeling.
  3. 3. When you are the listener, name afterwards which detail was doing that work. If you cannot find one, the speaker adds one and tells it again from that point. Write the detail you found.

Task C ★★★

  1. 1. Work in threes: one speaker, one listener, one tracker. The speaker tells a complex event in which you and someone else remembered the same thing differently.
  2. 2. Report at least two things somebody actually said, in reported speech. Move between what happened and why it mattered more than once, and not in a fixed order.
  3. 3. The listener reacts honestly at one point, by leaning in, by looking lost, or by interrupting with one of the three tools. The speaker changes how formally or how fast they are talking in response.
  4. 4. The tracker writes down where that change happened and what changed.

Task D ★★★★

  1. 1. In pairs, tell the same true event twice, to two different audiences. Name the audience out loud before each telling. For example: a friend who will take your side, then a committee deciding whether you were in the right.
  2. 2. The two versions must differ in what comes first and in what you give the most time to, not only in how polite they are.
  3. 3. Repair at least one point aloud where your own order of events comes out unclear. Fix it smoothly and keep going. Do not apologise for it.
  4. 4. Your partner says which version they found more convincing and names the choice that did it. Write what they named.
