## Target vocabulary (grounded in the real transcript)

1. **elevated** (of a level) - higher than it should be, measured against a standard.
2. **roadblocks** - obstacles put in the way of someone trying to get something done.
3. **dismissed** - rejected without being taken seriously.
4. **acknowledged** - admitted that something was true, often after denying it.
5. **corroded** - eaten away, damaged slowly by a chemical reaction.
6. **negligence** - failing to take the care that a duty required.

Context sentences (paraphrased from the source, not quoted verbatim):

1. In one part of the city, the share of children with elevated lead levels tripled.
2. She hit roadblocks when she asked the state for the data she needed.
3. State health officials dismissed her findings when she first announced them.
4. Two weeks later the same officials acknowledged that she had been right.
5. The river water corroded the city's old pipes, which is how the lead got into the water.
6. Families filed lawsuits accusing officials of negligence.

**Figurative chunks to gloss (Advanced band):**

- **"red flags popped up"** - warning signs appeared.
- **"an economic tailspin"** - a long, steep decline that is hard to pull out of.
- **"we stand up straight"** - we immediately pay full attention.

**Background Note 1:** Lead is a metal that was once used in water pipes. When it gets into drinking
water it can damage a child's developing brain, and the damage cannot be reversed. That is why a
change in children's blood lead levels is treated as an emergency.

**Background Note 2:** Flint is a city of about 100,000 people in Michigan. In 2014 it changed where
it got its drinking water in order to save money. The story in this segment follows what happened
after that change.

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

*Visual Inquiry hook (5 min).* Show the lesson's two photos side by side: a city water tower with
"FLINT WATER PLANT" painted on it, and a person holding a small testing meter over a kitchen sink
(`Lesson2_FlintWater_Img_HookPlant.jpg`, `Lesson2_FlintWater_Img_HookTesting.jpg`, both embedded in
the student packet). Ask, in pairs from where they sit: what is each picture showing, and what would
have to be true for both of these to be happening in the same town at the same time?

  Image: `Lesson2_FlintWater_Img_HookPlant.jpg` - "Flint-water-treatment-plant-tower" by unknown, United States Environmental Protection Agency, Public domain, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flint-water-treatment-plant-tower.jpg.

Take three or four answers. Write the class's predictions in a corner of the board and leave them
there: Phase 2's sequence chain is built beside them, and comparing prediction against what the
segment actually says is this lesson's strategy in miniature.

*Listening Skill Spotlight (3 min).* Say to the class, in these words:

> "Today we are practicing making a prediction about what comes next in a spoken account, then
> listening on to confirm it or correct it, instead of waiting to be told."

That is the whole Spotlight. It names the skill, not this story, and it is the same sentence for
every task Level. Day 2's closing check comes back to this same skill.

*Good to Know (3 min).* Read out Background Notes 1 and 2 above. Informational only; nothing in the
day's tasks turns on them.

*Vocabulary (9 min).* Pre-teach the six target words and the three figurative chunks using the
context sentences above. All six words occur in the real segment. Take the three chunks explicitly at
this band rather than leaving them to context.

**Phase 2: Watch & Listening Notes (15 min)**

Play the whole segment once, without stopping. Tell students beforehand that they will hear several
different people, and that the person to follow most closely is the doctor.

Build this **sequence chain** on the board as a shared organizer, and have students fill the same
chain in their packets while they watch:

```
Why the water changed  ->  First warning signs  ->  What the doctor did  ->  What she found  ->  How officials answered  ->  What happened next
```

Differentiated watching purpose, set before playback:

- **Level 4:** watch for the order of what the doctor did, and for how it turned out.
- **Level 5:** watch for the point where she describes the work itself, and for one thing she says
  that tells you how urgent it felt to her.
- **Level 6:** watch for who is quoted about the state's side of the story, and who is not.
- **Level 7:** watch for how confident the segment sounds about what caused what.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

Teach **Predict from Context Before Confirming** directly. Model it on this real moment from
**[Segment 3]**. Play up to the correspondent's line and stop:

> "She released her findings at a news conference last September, but her data was dismissed by state
> health officials."

Stop there and ask the class to predict, out loud, what the doctor says next about how that felt or
what was said to her. Take two or three predictions. Then play on and confirm against her real
answer, which is that they were called "unfortunate researchers" causing "near-hysteria." Name what
just happened: the sentence before gave enough context to narrow the possibilities, and confirming
took one line of listening rather than a rewind.

Guided practice on a second short excerpt, from **[Segment 2]**. Play to here and stop:

> "But as soon as the riverwater began flowing into Flint in April of 2014, red flags popped up."

Students write one prediction of what the first red flag was, then listen on and mark whether they
were right, close, or wrong. The point is not to be right; it is that a prediction gives the next
ten seconds of listening something to attach to.

**Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

One task per Level; each student works at one. Replay any segment on request.

**Level 4 (★):**

- Put these five events in the order they happened: officials said her findings were correct; the
  city switched to the Flint River; she asked the state for data and hit roadblocks; she held a news
  conference; she pulled records from her own hospital.
- Write the outcome of what the doctor did in one sentence.
- Write the reason she gives for working as fast as she did, in one sentence.
- Underline the two words in your ordered list that told you the order.
- **Answer note:** order is the city switched to the Flint River / she asked the state for data and
  hit roadblocks / she pulled records from her own hospital / she held a news conference / officials
  said her findings were correct. Outcome: her findings were dismissed at first and then accepted two
  weeks later. Reason for the speed: the public health implications, in her words that they did not
  sleep and did it right away. Accept any ordering words the student actually used.

**Level 5 (★★):**

- Summarize what the doctor did, in four sentences, in order.
- In one sentence, say why this mattered to her.
- She never says she was frightened or angry. Find one thing she says that tells you how it felt
  anyway, and write it down.
- Write one sentence saying what that detail tells you, and why you read it that way.
- **Answer note:** the strongest candidates are "We didn't sleep. We did this right away." and her
  description of what pediatricians do on hearing the word lead, that they stand up straight and
  freak out. Both convey urgency and alarm through behaviour rather than a feeling word. Why it
  mattered: accept the children's irreversible harm, or that no one else was going to check. Accept
  any detail argued from something actually said.

**Level 6 (★★★):**

- Summarize the doctor's part of the story in four sentences.
- The state's side appears in this segment. Write down every way it is actually represented, and by
  whom.
- Name one clearly missing piece of information that would change how one-sided this account is, and
  write one sentence on what it would tell us that this version does not.
- Write one sentence on why the segment can say that no one has fully explained something, and still
  leave you feeling you know what happened.
- **Answer note:** the state appears only as the governor's apology, as reported dismissal relayed by
  the doctor herself, and as the report that officials later agreed. No state official is interviewed
  about the dismissal, and the words "unfortunate researchers" and "near-hysteria" reach us through
  the person they were aimed at. Strong missing pieces: what the health department actually said and
  why, what they knew in September, whether anyone disputed her method. The unexplained point about
  the missing anti-corrosion agent is named and left open, which reads as candour while the account
  stays one-sided. Accept any missing piece argued from the segment.

**Level 7 (★★★★, uses both sources):**

Both sources report the same finding: children's blood lead levels in Flint after the water changed.
Their own words are printed below, paired subject by subject, so the comparison can be made from the
page (Quality Standards C9). One is a doctor speaking on television months before the other; the
other is the CDC's written report.

*What the numbers were.*
Segment: "There was one neighborhood, one specific ward of a city where the percentage of lead
poisoning actually tripled." and "It went from about 5 percent of kids to over almost 16 percent of
kids who were tested who had elevated blood lead levels."
CDC: "During the period before the water source switch, among 2,408 blood lead tests, 3.1% of BLLs
were ≥5 µg/dL; during the early switch period, this percentage increased to 5.0%."

*How sure each one is about the cause.*
Segment: "The whole problem occurred because the Flint River corroded the city's old lead pipes,
leaching the toxic metal into the water supply."
CDC: "Although this analysis demonstrates increased prevalence of BLLs ≥5 µg/dL coincident with the
switch to FWS, its observational nature limits attribution exclusively to the switch in drinking
water source."

*What people should do now.*
Segment: "The city of Flint has stopped using the Flint River as its source, and corrosion control is
now being used."
CDC: "Flint residents have been recently advised by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency that
when using an approved and properly installed and maintained water filter, it is safe for persons to
drink filtered tap water."

*Who is in the account.*
Segment: "We didn't sleep. We did this right away."
CDC: "Blood Lead Levels Among Children Aged <6 Years - Flint, Michigan, 2013-2016."

- Ask: both accounts report the same finding. Using the printed words only, write two to three
  sentences on how each one is built differently, and what each one leaves a reader concluding.
- Ask: identify one interest or assumption behind one of the two versions that the version itself
  never states outright. Write one to two sentences and point to the words that led you there.
- **Answer note:** structurally, the segment reaches its point through one person and a single
  neighbourhood's worst number, states cause as settled fact, and puts a human agent in every
  sentence. The CDC report works from the whole tested population, gives the citywide figures rather
  than the extreme ward, hedges its own causal claim, and has no actor at all, not even in its title.
  On unstated interests: the CDC's hedge protects a scientific body from over-claiming and from being
  wrong later, which is why "coincident with" does the work "because of" does in the segment;
  the segment's stake is that a viewer should feel the urgency the doctor felt, which is why the
  tripled ward, not the citywide rise from 3.1% to 5.0%, is the number that gets said out loud.
  Neither number is false. Accept any reading argued from the printed words.

*Board-dependent moment.* With about six minutes left, take the sequence chain on the board and fill
it from the floor, one link at a time, calling on a different Level for each: Level 4 supplies the
order, Level 5 the point where the work itself happens, Level 6 who is missing from the account,
Level 7 what the two reports do differently with the same finding. The completed chain exists only
once every Level has spoken into it; no packet contains it.

**Phase 5: Listening Closing Transfer Check (10 min)**

Read the first sentence aloud, stop, and ask every student to write one prediction of what comes
next. Then read the rest.

> Our building manager kept saying the heating was fine. In November a tenant on the third floor
> bought a small thermometer and wrote down the temperature twice a day for three weeks. When she
> showed the list at the residents' meeting, the manager dismissed it and said one thermometer proved
> nothing. Two weeks later the company acknowledged the boiler had corroded, and the repair began.

(74 words. Recycles five of the six target words: dismissed, acknowledged, corroded, and by
implication elevated and roadblocks in the discussion. Topic is unrelated to the source.)

Students compare their written prediction with what they heard, then tell a partner in one sentence
what in the first line made them predict what they did. Cold-call two or three pairs. After the
sharing, students may turn to the printed script and check themselves against it.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
DAY 2 (Unit B - Speaking)   |--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Differentiated Practice (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Oral Output (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

The skill is **Summarizing What Someone Said**, and the segment does it constantly, because a
correspondent's job is largely to compress what other people told him. Replay or read these three
real moments and put them on the board:

- From **[Segment 3]**, the correspondent compressing several months of the doctor's work into one
  sentence: "Late last summer, Dr. Hanna-Attisha tried to get county and state data on how much lead
  Flint's kids had been absorbing into their blood, but she hit roadblocks."
- From **[Segment 3]**, the correspondent reporting what officials did without quoting them: "But two
  weeks later, officials acknowledged her findings were correct."
- From **[Segment 4]**, the correspondent summarizing a doctor's list: "He says lead causes a litany
  of problems for kids, among them, delayed mental development, irritability, aggression and learning
  problems."

Say to the class, in these words:

> "Today we are practicing saying back what someone else told us, shorter than they said it, without
> changing what they meant."

Draw out the point students will use all period: a good summary keeps the speaker visible. Notice
that all three models name who said it (*she*, *officials*, *he says*) before compressing what was
said. Dropping the attribution is what turns a summary into a claim of your own.

Sentence frames for the day, on the board:

- "She said that ___."
- "According to ___, ___."
- "His point was basically that ___."
- "What she is saying is that ___, not that ___."
- "In one sentence: ___."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Students work in pairs. Partner A tells the story; Partner B listens and then summarizes it back.
Then they swap. The story for all Levels: **a time you told someone about a problem and they did not
believe you at first.**

- **Level 4 (★):** narrate the sequence in four or five sentences, choosing your own ordering words.
  Say how it turned out and give one brief reason it turned out that way. Then, as the listener, say
  your partner's story back in two sentences, naming them at the start.
- **Level 5 (★★):** narrate it as an extended account, six to eight sentences. Say why it mattered to
  you. Include one detail that shows how you felt when you were not believed, without naming the
  feeling. As the listener, summarize in two sentences and then say which detail carried the feeling.
- **Level 6 (★★★):** narrate it fluently without a fixed template, moving between what happened and
  what it meant. Quote at least one thing the other person actually said. Keep an eye on your
  partner, and when they look lost, change your pace or your wording once and keep going. As the
  listener, summarize the account and then say one thing your partner left out.
- **Level 7 (★★★★):** narrate it once. Then summarize your own account twice for two different
  listeners: once so the other person sounds unreasonable, once so they sound reasonable. Change
  nothing that happened. If your sequence comes out unclear, catch it and repair it out loud as you go.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

Feature: **contrastive stress**, which this segment carries because so much of it is one account set
against another. Replay the exchange in **[Segment 3]** where the correspondent asks "Unfortunate?"
and the doctor repeats the word before finishing her sentence. The stress lands on the word being
challenged, not on the sentence's usual peak.

Students mark the stressed word in a short set of printed pairs built from the lesson's own content
("*She* said it was safe" against "She said it was *safe*"), say each pair aloud to a partner, and
check that the partner can tell which meaning was intended. They then run one summary sentence from
Phase 2 twice, stressing a different word each time, and ask their partner what changed.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Panel Round (20 min)**

Four to six students speak in turn from their seats on each prompt while the rest of the class works.
Three rotated prompts, roughly six minutes each:

1. The doctor's numbers and the CDC's numbers are both true. Which one should a news programme say
   out loud, and why?
2. When officials first said her data was wrong, she kept going. What would it take for you to keep
   going after being told publicly that you were wrong?
3. Think of a time an organization told people something was fine and it was not. How did the truth
   finally come out?

Per-Level sentence stems:

- ★ "She said that ___, and then ___."
- ★★ "According to the report, ___, which is not quite what the segment said, because ___."
- ★★★ "His point was basically that ___, though the segment never asks ___."
- ★★★★ "Summarized fairly, the disagreement is not about ___ but about ___."

Every student not speaking writes a one-sentence summary of what each panel speaker said, naming the
speaker. At the end of each prompt, two of those summaries are read out and the original speaker says
whether it was fair.

*Differentiated participation.* Students working at Level 4 rehearse their summary with a partner in
the first two minutes of each prompt and read it at a natural pause, rather than taking an open panel
turn. Foundation Support, if any student needs it: the student keeps the count of how many speakers
named their source, and reads that count out at the end.

*Board-dependent moment.* Keep a live board list of the summaries that the original speaker judged
fair, and what made them fair. It is built only from what happens in the room, and Phase 5 uses it.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

In pairs, one student describes in three or four sentences **a piece of news or a message they
received this week**, and the partner predicts how it ended before hearing the end, then summarizes
the whole thing back in one sentence naming the speaker. Swap. This prompt is not used anywhere else
in this Set. Cold-call two or three pairs: each says the prediction they made and whether it held.
Nothing is collected.

*Respectful Tiers check across the cycle.* Level 4 had genuine interpretive work on Day 1 (naming the
outcome and the reason she gives for her speed, not just ordering events) and a genuine production
role on Day 2 (a full narration plus a real summary turn, rehearsed rather than diminished).

Close with two minutes on the cycle itself: what makes a summary fair, and what a prediction gives a
listener that waiting does not.
