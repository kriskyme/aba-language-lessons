Good to Know

Lead is a metal that was once used in water pipes. When it gets into drinking water it can damage a child's developing brain, and the damage cannot be reversed. That is why a change in children's blood lead levels is treated as an emergency.

Flint is a city of about 100,000 people in Michigan. In 2014 it changed where it got its drinking water in order to save money. The story in this report follows what happened after that change.

Words to Know

1. elevated (of a level) higher than it should be, measured against a standard.
2. roadblocks obstacles put in the way of someone trying to get something done.
3. dismissed rejected without being taken seriously.
4. acknowledged admitted that something was true, often after denying it.
5. corroded eaten away, damaged slowly by a chemical reaction.
6. negligence failing to take the care that a duty required.

Unit 2B: The Doctor Who Counted

Listening & Speaking · Narrating Advanced 2.1.2.0

Objective: I can say back what someone else told me, shorter than they said it, without changing what they meant.

Learn the Phrase

Summarizing What Someone Said

The reporter in this story spends most of his time compressing what other people told him. Three real examples: "Late last summer, Dr. Hanna-Attisha tried to get county and state data on how much lead Flint's kids had been absorbing into their blood, but she hit roadblocks." "But two weeks later, officials acknowledged her findings were correct." "He says lead causes a litany of problems for kids, among them, delayed mental development, irritability, aggression and learning problems."

A good summary keeps the speaker visible. All three examples name who said it (she, officials, he says) before compressing what was said. Dropping that is what turns a summary into a claim of your own.

  • •"She said that ___."
  • •"According to ___, ___."
  • •"His point was basically that ___."
  • •"What she is saying is that ___, not that ___."
  • •"In one sentence: ___."

Practice It

Do one task below. Work in pairs: one of you tells the story while the other listens, then the listener says it back. Then swap. When you are done, find someone who worked on a different task and take turns sharing what you told.

Task A ★

Tell a true story of a time you told someone about a problem and they did not believe you at first.

  1. 1.Tell the sequence in four or five sentences, choosing your own ordering words.
  2. 2.Say how it turned out, and give one brief reason it turned out that way.
  3. 3.As the listener, say your partner's story back in two sentences, naming them at the start.

Task B ★★

Tell a true story of a time you told someone about a problem and they did not believe you at first.

  1. 1.Tell it as a longer account, six to eight sentences, and say why it mattered to you.
  2. 2.Include one detail that shows how you felt when you were not believed, without naming the feeling.
  3. 3.As the listener, summarize in two sentences, then say which detail carried the feeling.

Task C ★★★

Tell a true story of a time you told someone about a problem and they did not believe you at first.

  1. 1.Tell it without a fixed pattern, moving between what happened and what it meant.
  2. 2.Quote at least one thing the other person actually said.
  3. 3.Keep an eye on your partner. When they look lost, change your pace or your wording once, and keep going.
  4. 4.As the listener, summarize the account, then say one thing your partner left out.

Task D ★★★★

Tell a true story of a time you told someone about a problem and they did not believe you at first.

  1. 1.Tell it once, in full.
  2. 2.Then summarize your own account twice, for two different listeners: once so the other person sounds unreasonable, once so they sound reasonable. Change nothing that happened.
  3. 3.If your order comes out unclear at any point, catch it and fix it out loud as you go.

Say It Like a Native Speaker

When two accounts are set against each other, English moves the stress onto the word being challenged. In the report, the interviewer hears the word "unfortunate," and simply repeats it as a question with the stress hard on it: "Unfortunate?" That single stressed word does the work of a whole sentence.

Say each pair aloud to a partner, stressing the word in bold, and check that your partner can tell which meaning you meant. 1. "She said it was safe" / "She said it was safe." 2. "They acknowledged the problem" / "They acknowledged the problem." Then say one summary sentence from the task above twice, stressing a different word each time, and ask your partner what changed.

Discuss It

Four to six people will speak in turn on each question, from where they sit. While someone else is speaking, write a one-sentence summary of what they said, naming the speaker. At the end of each question, two summaries will be read out and the original speaker will say whether it was fair.

1. The doctor's numbers and the written report's numbers are both true. Which one should a news programme say out loud, and why?

2. When officials first said her data was wrong, she kept going. What would it take for you to keep going after being told publicly that you were wrong?

3. Think of a time an organization told people something was fine and it was not. How did the truth finally come out?

Starter phrases:

  • ★ "She said that ___, and then ___."
  • ★★ "According to the written report, ___, which is not quite what the news report said, because ___."
  • ★★★ "His point was basically that ___, though the report never asks ___."
  • ★★★★ "Summarized fairly, the disagreement is not about ___ but about ___."

Wrap It Up

With a partner, take turns. One of you describes a piece of news or a message you received this week, in three or four sentences. Before you hear the end, your partner predicts how it ended. Then they say the whole thing back in one sentence, naming you at the start. Swap, then say whether your prediction held.

  • ★ "I think it ended with ___."
  • ★★ "You said that ___, so I expected ___."
  • ★★★ "In one sentence, what you told me was ___."
  • ★★★★ "I predicted ___ because of the way you said ___, and what actually happened was ___."
