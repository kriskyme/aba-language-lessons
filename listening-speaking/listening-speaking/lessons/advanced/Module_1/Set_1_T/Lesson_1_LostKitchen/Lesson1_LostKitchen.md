## Governing CSV objective (Level 5, Listening/Speaking, Module 1: Describing)

> Listening: Can listen to an extended spoken description of a familiar or semi-familiar
> place or object, summarize how it's organized, and identify one clearly evaluative word
> choice that signals the speaker's opinion even though the speaker never states the opinion
> directly (e.g. a positive or negative adjective standing in for "I like/dislike this").
> Speaking: Can give an extended description, organizing details logically and including at
> least one clearly evaluative word choice that signals an opinion without stating it outright,
> working independently.

Listening half governs Day 1's task-Level basis column; Speaking half governs Day 2's.

## DAY 1: Unit A - Listening

```
|--Hook, Good to Know & Vocab (20)--|--Listen & Notes (15)--|--Skill Instr. (10)--|--Task (20)--|--Transfer Check (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

- **Mystery Quote:** put this real quote on the board with no context: _"I thought this would be a quiet little
  cafe in the middle of nowhere, with really good coffee, some homemade English muffins, and free Wi-Fi."_ Ask:
  who do you think said this, and about what? What do you think it actually became?
- **Listening Skill Spotlight:** "Today we're practicing telling a stated fact apart from an opinion or feeling
  someone signals without saying it outright." (This is what today's Listening Closing Transfer Check will ask
  students to reproduce.)
- **TOEFL Connection (Section 0.6A, TOEFL Track students):** this is exactly what TOEFL Listening's Attitude
  questions test - can you tell what the speaker actually thinks or feels, even when she never says "I like
  this" or "I don't like this" directly?
- Present the citation block above.
- Pre-teach the 6 target words using the context sentences. Gloss both idioms ("in the middle of nowhere," "keep
  my feet on the ground").
- **Background Note 1:** the James Beard Foundation is a well-known U.S. organization that recognizes chefs and
  restaurants; being noticed by "James Beard...taste-makers" is a real marker of culinary reputation.
- **Background Note 2:** a grist mill is a historic type of building, once common in small New England towns,
  originally built to grind local farmers' grain into flour - which is why a restored one now houses a restaurant
  built around local ingredients.
- Return to the Mystery Quote: "What do you think made this place go from a 'quiet little cafe' idea to what the
  segment calls a 'culinary phenomenon'?" Collect 3-4 guesses on the board.
- Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback: Level 4/5 listen for what changed and why; Level 6/7
  listen for how the speaker's own words reveal her feelings about that change, not just the facts of it.
  **Phase 2: Listen & Listening Notes (15 min)**
- Listen to the full segment once. While listening, students fill in a comparison T-chart: "What Erin French
  Originally Planned" vs. "What The Lost Kitchen Became" - rows: the space, the food, the scale/reputation.
- **TOEFL Track touchpoint (Section 0.6B):** TOEFL Track students add one extra column to the T-chart, "TOEFL
  Question Type," tagging each row with whichever of the 6 real TOEFL question types that note point would
  answer (e.g. the scale/reputation row tags as Factual; a row about why she calls herself a "cook" tags as
  Inference).
  **Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**
- Teach "stated fact vs. implied opinion" directly, modeling on Erin French's real quote from **[Segment 5]**:
  _"If you start changing it, if you start growing it, if you start doing things like that, the magic will be lost
  in a heartbeat."_ Point out this is not a fact about the restaurant - it signals a strong personal value
  (protecting something real over growing it) through evaluative language ("the magic"), not a plain statement.
- Guided practice: replay **[Segment 3]**'s "it feels fraudulent" line and have students identify what it signals
  about how she sees herself, not just what she said.
- **TOEFL Connection (Section 0.6A, TOEFL Track students):** this stated-fact-vs-implied-opinion skill is exactly
  what separates a TOEFL Factual question ("what did the speaker say?") from an Attitude or Inference question
  ("what does the speaker think or feel, even though she never says so directly?").
  **Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**
- Each student picks one task Level and completes it:
  - **Level 4:** In 2-3 sentences, name two features of The Lost Kitchen, one comparison to what French
    originally planned, and the stated reason things turned out differently. Populate
    the T-chart from Phase 2 with two features per column, one comparison, one stated reason. **Respectful
    Tiers check:** also ask - "According to the segment, why doesn't French call herself a 'chef'?" then "Do you
    think she's being modest, or does she really believe it? Say which, and why" - a genuine simplified
    interpretive step, not fact-retrieval alone.
    - **Answer note:** what she originally planned: a "quiet little cafe."
  - **Level 5:** Summarize how the segment is organized and identify one evaluative word that signals
    admiration without anyone saying "this is great" directly. Note the organizational flow segment by
    segment on the T-chart.
    - **Answer note:** organization: the scene, then her background, then her philosophy, then her
      present-day community work. Evaluative words: "phenomenon," "magic."
  - **Level 6:** List two stated facts and two clearly-signaled evaluative comments, keeping the two
    categories separate. Locate one clear tonal shift in the segment and describe what changes in her
    language at that shift.
    - **Answer note:** facts: 48 seats; a restored 1834 grist mill. Evaluative comments: "the magic will be
      lost"; calling herself a fraud for using the word "chef." Tonal shift: from a confident description of
      the restaurant to a vulnerable personal disclosure about addiction and divorce in **[Segment 4]**.
  - **Level 7 (uses both sources):** Present the three short Cherry Bombe excerpts (from French's own words: the
    "40 plus people," the "run by all women," and the "driven by what the produce is" lines) alongside the PBS
    segment. Ask: how does the PBS report frame French's story differently from how she frames it herself?
    Identify one unstated interest or assumption behind one framing. Note two or three specific descriptive
    choices the PBS segment makes (what it shows, what it lingers on) to ground the comparison.
    - **Answer note:** PBS's Jeffrey Brown builds a struggle-to-triumph narrative arc, from a difficult
      childhood and addiction to national acclaim - compelling television, but not something French
      emphasizes about herself; her own account instead centers an all-women team, refusing to expand, and
      letting the produce decide the menu. Unstated interest: PBS's narrative arc serves the story's dramatic
      shape more than it serves French's own priorities; her own account assumes, without directly arguing
      it, that growth would cost something worth protecting.
  - **Level 7 - TOEFL Track capstone (Section 0.6C, alongside the task above):** TOEFL iBT Listening-format
    practice questions, in the real TOEFL question-type styles - answered from the same PBS segment every
    student in the room already watched together in Phase 2, not a separately-authored or separately-read
    passage. TOEFL-track students work from the exact same single shared listening as every other Level.

    *Questions:*
    1. **[Main Idea]** What is the segment mainly about?
    2. **[Factual]** According to the segment, how does someone get a table at The Lost Kitchen?
    3. **[Inference]** What can be inferred about Erin French's approach to cooking?
    4. **[Attitude]** What is French's attitude toward her restaurant's growing popularity?

    *(Question-type labels shown here for the instructor only; the student packet does not label them.)*

    *Answer key:*
    - **1. Main Idea - (B) How a small-town restaurant became famous, and how its owner has tried to keep
      it feeling the same despite that growth.** The segment opens on French never planning for fame
      ([Segment 2]) and closes on her own words about still "struggling with that growth" ([Segment 6]) -
      the whole segment is organized around that tension. (A) Freedom, Maine is only a setting, not the
      focus. (C) No specific dish is described. (D) The segment describes what happened to one restaurant,
      not general advice.
    - **2. Factual - (A) She submits her name for a reservation and hopes it gets drawn, rather than
      booking a table directly.** Established by [Segment 2]'s "reservation system" and the target
      vocabulary's own context sentence 4 ("Thousands of people try to get a reservation there every
      year"). (B) Contradicts the drawing/lottery system described. (C) Applicants come from across the
      country and other countries, not only locally. (D) Not stated.
    - **3. Inference - (C) She prefers deciding what to cook based on what ingredients are available that
      day, rather than planning meals in advance.** Supported by [Segment 5]'s "cook by intuition" and the
      target vocabulary's own context sentence 2 ("French says she cooks by intuition, using whatever
      ingredients are freshest that day"). (A) Contradicts working with whatever is freshest each day. (B)
      Not supported; the segment emphasizes local ingredients. (D) Not supported.
    - **4. Attitude - (D) Cautious: she wants the restaurant to succeed but worries about losing what makes
      it special.** Her own real quoted words - "the magic will be lost in a heartbeat" ([Segment 5], also
      used in Phase 3) and "I continue to struggle with that growth of, where is that fine line?"
      ([Segment 6], also used in Day 2 Phase 1) - both signal worry, not simple enthusiasm. (A) Contradicts
      the stated tension. (B) Her quoted words show she has clearly thought hard about this, not that she's
      indifferent. (C) Overstated; nothing suggests regret, only caution.
- **Board-dependent moment:** students who worked on different Levels briefly compare and add their row(s) to
  the shared T-chart from Phase 2, so the full picture exists only once multiple Levels have contributed.
  **Phase 5: Listening Closing Transfer Check (10 min)**
- **Teacher-read-aloud script (invented, unrelated topic, recycling target vocabulary):**
  > My cousin runs a small vineyard about an hour outside the city. It's a seasonal business, really only busy
  > from late summer through the grape harvest in October. She never takes reservations for the tasting room;
  > she says planning too far ahead kills the intuition of the place, since some days call for a picnic table
  > outside and others don't. What started as a reinvention of her grandfather's old orchard has become a small
  > local phenomenon, with people driving in from three counties over just to sit in the field and taste that
  > year's wine.
  > (96 words; recycles seasonal, reservation, intuition, reinvention, phenomenon - 5 of 6 target words - on a
  > topic unrelated to The Lost Kitchen.)
- Teacher reads this aloud once. Students do not read it themselves first - printed in the student packet only
  after the response space.
- Students state, out loud to a partner, one fact the speaker states directly and one opinion/feeling she signals
  without stating outright (e.g. the stated fact: it's only open late summer through October; the signaled
  opinion: reservations would "kill the intuition of the place" - a value judgment, not a fact). Teacher
  cold-calls two or three pairs. After sharing, students may read the script to check.
- **TOEFL Track touchpoint (Section 0.6B):** alongside the partner retell above, TOEFL Track students also
  answer, in writing: **[Inference]** What can be inferred about why the speaker doesn't take reservations?
  *(Suggested answer: she believes planning too far ahead would hurt the natural, spontaneous feel of the
  place - the same "protecting something real over control/growth" value the lesson's main source models.)*

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Town Hall (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- Play French's real quote from **[Segment 6]**: _"Yes. And I continue to struggle with that growth of, where
  is that fine line? Where does it get to be too much?"_ - a real example of hedging: she doesn't give a flat
  answer, she qualifies it, admits uncertainty, and poses her own question back.
- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing hedging an opinion - showing you're not 100% certain, instead of
  stating something as flat fact." This is the exact skill this day's Speaking Closing Transfer Check will ask
  students to reproduce.
- Teach hedging frames: "I think..., but I'm not totally sure." / "It seems like..., though I could be wrong." /
  "In a way, **_, but then again, _**."
- **TOEFL Connection (Section 0.6A, TOEFL Track students):** hedging - qualifying an opinion instead of stating
  it flatly - is part of what TOEFL Speaking's Language Use score listens for: can a speaker use more than
  simple flat statements to express a real, nuanced opinion?
  **Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**
- Each student picks one task Level:
  - **Level 4:** Complete: "I think **_ is _**, but I'm not sure." using a familiar object. Then, describe the
    object with one comparison and a stated reason, choosing your own connectors, working independently.
  - **Level 5:** Give an extended hedged description of a place, organizing details logically and including one
    evaluative word.
  - **Level 6:** Deliver a fluent, hedged description that shifts register once in response to a partner's
    follow-up question (e.g. starts casual, becomes more careful/qualified once challenged), explicitly
    explaining why one feature matters.
  - **Level 7:** Give a hedged opinion using idiomatic phrasing, then self-correct one word choice mid-sentence
    on purpose, modeling the kind of real-time repair fluent speakers do.
  - **Level 7 - TOEFL Track capstone (Section 0.6D, alongside the task above):** a TOEFL iBT Speaking-format
    practice set. Original sentences/questions grounded in this lesson's real topic and vocabulary, not
    excerpted (the real TOEFL Speaking tasks are freshly authored in the actual test too). Split into an
    in-class untimed rehearsal and a take-home timed version, since the real individually-timed mechanics
    can't run live against one student while the rest of the class works a different task.

    **In class (untimed rehearsal, no stopwatch, no scoring):** scenario - "You're practicing what you'd say
    describing The Lost Kitchen to a friend who's never heard of it." Instruction, stated up front: "Take
    turns with a partner: one person reads, the other listens and repeats or answers. Switch roles halfway
    through."

    - Reader reads once, listener repeats it back: "The kitchen sits inside an old grist mill." / "Reservations

    *Take an Interview (recorded audio asks each question once; 45 sec response, no prep time):*
    scenario - "You are taking part in a short online interview about food and community for a local
    magazine."

    1. **[Personal Experience]** Can you describe a meal you've cooked using only ingredients you already
       had at home, without following a specific recipe?
    2. **[Personal Preferences/Interests]** Do you prefer eating at a small, lesser-known restaurant or a
       well-known one? Why?
    3. **[Opinions on Current Topics/Trends]** Some people think a small local business should stay small
       to protect what makes it special. Others think it should grow to reach more people. What do you
       think?
    4. **[Speculate About the Future]** Imagine a small restaurant you love suddenly became famous. What do
       you think would change about it, and would that change be good or bad?

    *Scoring guides (for the teacher, a peer, or self-assessment - there is no correct-answer key for
    spoken responses; reproduced from `source/TOEFL_Speaking_extracted_text.txt`):*

    **Listen and Repeat - holistic 0-5, Intelligibility and Accuracy:** 5 = an exact, fully intelligible
    repetition; 4 = captures the full meaning with only minor word/grammar changes; 3 = mostly complete but
    not fully accurate, occasional intelligibility trouble; 2 = a significant part missing or wrong, hard to
    understand without the prompt; 1 = only a few recognizable words remain; 0 = no response, unintelligible,
    or unconnected to the prompt.

    **Take an Interview - holistic 0-5, scored on Relevance, Elaboration, Delivery, and Language Use:**
    5 = fully addresses the question with clear elaboration, natural pace, intelligible delivery, accurate
    and varied grammar/vocabulary; 4 = addresses the question, reasonably clear, adequate grammar/vocabulary;
    3 = generally on topic but limited elaboration, choppy pace, limited grammar/vocabulary range; 2 =
    minimally connected, little elaboration, limited intelligibility; 1 = only vaguely connected, mostly
    unintelligible; 0 = no response, unintelligible, or unconnected to the prompt.

- Students mark contrastive stress in two more sentences built from the lesson's real content, then check their

- **TOEFL Connection (Section 0.6A, TOEFL Track students):** contrastive stress and clear pronunciation like
