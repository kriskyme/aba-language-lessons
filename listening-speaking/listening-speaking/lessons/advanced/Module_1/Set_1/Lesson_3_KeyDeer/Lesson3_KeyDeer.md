**Figurative chunks to gloss (Advanced band):**

- **"get your arms around [something]"** - to fully understand or get control of a difficult problem
  (Bergh: people aren't "getting their arms around" the climate change challenge fast enough)
- **"so to speak"** - a phrase speakers add to soften or flag a word choice that isn't perfectly literal
  (Bergh describes the deer being "rescued, so to speak," by bringing them into zoos - not a real
  rescue in the usual sense, more like a fallback)

**Background Note 1:** the National Key Deer Refuge, created by the U.S. federal government in 1957,

development - the same kind of "refuge" named in this lesson's target vocabulary.

**Background Note 2:** NOAA (the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration) is the U.S. federal
science agency that tracks and forecasts changes in the ocean and climate, including the sea-level

## DAY 1: Unit A - Listening

```
|--Hook, Good to Know & Vocab (20)--|--Watch & Notes (15)--|--Skill Instr. (10)--|--Task (20)--|--Transfer Check (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

- **Four-Corner Debate:** post four signs around the room - Strongly Agree, Agree, Disagree, Strongly
  Disagree - and read this statement: "If a wild animal's habitat is going to keep shrinking no matter
  what people do, it's still worth spending time and money trying to save that animal." Students move to
  the corner matching their view and briefly discuss with others there before a few volunteers explain
  their choice to the whole class.
- **Listening Skill Spotlight:** "Today we're practicing listening for cause and effect - noticing when
  a speaker tells us that one thing happening is the reason something else happens." (This is the exact
  skill today's Listening Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.)
- Present the citation block above.
- Pre-teach the 6 target words using the context sentences. Gloss both figurative chunks ("get your arms
  around," "so to speak").
- **Background Note 1** (refuge) and **Background Note 2** (NOAA), both brief and informational only.
- Return to the Four-Corner statement: "Today's video is about a real animal whose habitat is shrinking.
  Listen for what's causing that, and what people are doing about it."
- Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback: Level 4/5 listen for what the Key deer looks
  like, where it lives, and one reason people want to protect it; Level 6/7 listen for how the segment's
  tone changes as it moves from a story of past success to a warning about the future.

**Phase 2: Watch & Listening Notes (15 min)**

- Watch the full segment once. While watching, students fill in a comparison T-chart: "What Almost Wiped
  Out the Key Deer (the Past)" vs. "What Threatens the Key Deer Now (the Future)" - rows: the cause, the
  effect on habitat, and what people did or are doing in response.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

- Teach "listen for cause and effect" directly, modeling on real content from **[Segment 3]**: Bergh's
  point that it's "only a matter of time as the sea continues to rise" (cause) before the deer's shrinking
  habitat "undoes" them (effect). Point out the cause-effect connector language itself ("as... that,"
  "because of," "the thing that's going to get them").
- Guided practice: replay **[Segment 2]** and have students identify the cause-effect chain in the
  deer's 20th-century near-extinction (the construction of Highway 1 cutting the habitat in two, plus
  development and poaching, as causes; the population dropping to about 25 animals as the effect).

**Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

- Each student picks one task Level and completes it:
  - **Level 4:** In 2-3 sentences, describe what a Key deer looks like, including one comparison to a
    more familiar animal, and give the reason local resident Connie Ritchie gives for wanting to protect
    them. Add one row to the T-chart from Phase 2. **Respectful Tiers check:** also ask - "According to
    the segment, what almost caused the Key deer to disappear in the 20th century?" then "Do you think
    the 1957 refuge was a good solution? Circle yes or no and say why." - a genuine simplified
    interpretive step, not fact-retrieval alone.
  - **Level 5:** Summarize how the segment is organized and identify one evaluative word or phrase that
    signals the speaker's opinion without stating it directly. Note the organizational flow on the T-chart.
    - **Answer note:** organization: introducing the deer, then its history of near-extinction and the
      refuge's success, then the new climate threat, then the possible future, then a local activist's
      reflection. Evaluative words: "success story," "iconic," "very sad outcome."
  - **Level 6:** List two stated facts and two clearly-signaled evaluative comments, keeping the two
    categories separate. Locate one clear tonal shift in the segment and describe what changes in the
    language at that shift.
    - **Answer note:** facts: about 800 Key deer remain today; NOAA estimates 1.5 to 7 feet of sea-level
      rise around the Keys over the next 75 years. Evaluative comments: Bergh calling the recovery "a
      success story"; Bergh calling the possible zoo-only outcome "a very sad outcome for a very
      interesting iconic Florida species." Tonal shift: from Bergh's measured, statistic-driven tone
      describing sea-level numbers to his more personal, regretful tone describing the zoo outcome.
  - **Level 7 (uses both sources):** Read a short excerpt paraphrased from the National Key Deer Refuge's
    official U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service page (established 1957 "to protect and preserve the national
    interest in Key deer," about 9,200 acres of pine rockland, wetland, and mangrove habitat, open to
    visitors for fishing, wildlife viewing, and education) alongside the PBS segment. Ask: how does the
    refuge's own official description of itself differ in tone and focus from how the PBS segment
    describes the same refuge? Identify one unstated interest or assumption behind one framing. Note two
    or three specific descriptive choices the PBS segment makes (which numbers it cites, whose voices it
    features) to ground the comparison.
    - **Answer note:** the refuge's own page has reason to emphasize calm stewardship and public access
      rather than the existential climate threat the independent segment centers on.
- **Board-dependent moment:** students who worked on different Levels briefly compare and add their
  row(s) to the shared T-chart from Phase 2, so the full past-vs-future picture exists only once multiple
  Levels have contributed.

**Phase 5: Listening Closing Transfer Check (10 min)**

- **Teacher-read-aloud script (invented, unrelated topic, recycling target vocabulary and applying the
  day's actual skill - cause and effect):**
  > On a small island off the coast, there's a subspecies of tortoise found nowhere else in the world.
  > For years, the island's mangrove forests gave the tortoises everything they needed to survive. But
  > when a hotel company cleared part of the mangroves to build new docks, the tortoises lost a large
  > piece of their habitat almost overnight. Because of that construction, their population quickly
  > dwindled from several thousand to under two hundred. Local scientists set up a small wildlife refuge
  > to protect what was left, but they're still not sure the remaining group is large enough to stay
  > viable long-term.
  > (recycles subspecies, habitat, mangrove, dwindle, refuge, viable - all 6 target words - on a topic
  > unrelated to the Key deer, and contains a clear cause, "a hotel company cleared part of the
  > mangroves," and its effect, "their population quickly dwindled.")
- Teacher reads this aloud once. Students do not read it themselves first - printed in the student
  packet only after the response space.
- Students state, out loud to a partner, the cause they heard (a hotel company clearing mangroves for
  docks) and the effect that followed (the tortoise population dwindling and a refuge being created).
  Teacher cold-calls two or three pairs. After sharing, students may read the script to check.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Jigsaw (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- **Deviation flagged (per Section 0.5, item 11):** this segment is a narrated news piece, not a live
  conversation, so no speaker in it actually interrupts or takes a turn from another speaker on camera.
  Ground the spotlight instead in the source's real, verified situational context: the segment itself
  hands off between four different real stakeholders (a ranger, a scientist, a resident, an activist) who
  each hold a piece of the Key deer story - exactly the kind of situation (a public meeting or panel
  where several people with different roles all want to speak) where turn-taking and polite interrupting
  matter most in real life.
- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing turn-taking and interrupting politely - jumping into a
  conversation without being rude, and knowing when to let someone finish." This is the exact skill
  today's Speaking Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.
- Teach turn-taking/interrupting frames: "Sorry to jump in, but..." / "Can I add something here?" / "Let
  me just finish this thought, then I'd love to hear yours." / "Go ahead, you were saying...?"

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

- Each student picks one task Level:
  - **Level 4:** Complete: "Sorry to jump in, but ___." using a familiar idea about the Key deer. Then
    describe the Key deer in your own original words, including one comparison and a stated reason,
    choosing your own connectors.
  - **Level 5:** Give an extended description of the Key deer or its refuge, organizing details logically
    and including one evaluative word.
  - **Level 6:** Describe the Key deer's situation in a fluent, integrated way, explicitly explaining why
    one feature (its small size, its limited habitat, its refuge) matters, and adjusting your register
    once in response to a partner's reaction.
  - **Level 7:** Describe the Key deer's situation using idiomatic phrasing, then self-correct one word
    choice mid-sentence on purpose, modeling the kind of real-time repair fluent speakers do.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

- Teach thought-group pausing (chunking speech into short meaningful phrases with brief pauses between
  them) using a real line built from the source's own reflective closing: Svejkovsky weighing whether
  saving one small deer population matters, then answering himself in short chunks. Model breaking a
  sentence like "biologically / probably not / but personally / I think it makes a difference" into
  short thought groups with a brief pause after each one, rather than running the whole sentence together.
- Students mark thought-group breaks in two more sentences built from the lesson's real content, then
  check their marking by listening again, then apply it in brief paired rehearsal of Phase 2's
  descriptions, pausing briefly between thought groups rather than rushing through.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Jigsaw (20 min)**

- **Differentiated participation:** Level 4 gets a rehearsed pair-share of their stem before joining
  their expert group's discussion.
- Form four expert groups, one per real stakeholder in the segment: the Refuge Ranger's group (habitat
  and the deer's biology), The Nature Conservancy's group (the climate science and sea-level numbers),
  the Local Resident's group (what it's like living alongside the deer), and the Save Our Key Deer
  group (why the fight is worth it even if the odds are long). Each group prepares to explain their
  stakeholder's perspective, then reshuffles into mixed groups of four (one from each expert group) to
  share, practicing turn-taking and polite interrupting as each stakeholder's viewpoint is presented.
- **Board-dependent moment:** running board record of each stakeholder's key point as it's actually
  shared during the jigsaw, feeding the Closing Transfer Check.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

- Every student, in pairs and out loud, practices politely interrupting a partner mid-description on a
  new topic unrelated to the source (a class project, a group decision, a weekend plan), using Phase 1's
  frames. Teacher cold-calls two or three pairs.
- **Respectful Tiers check:** across the whole cycle, did Level 4 get a genuine interpretive step (Day 1)
  and a real production role (Day 2's rehearsed pair-share into the jigsaw), not fact-retrieval and
  silent participation only?
- Brief reflection: "What's one local animal or place near you that you think deserves more protection,
  and why?" No Part 2/presentation-project extension is being used for this cycle (not yet built).
