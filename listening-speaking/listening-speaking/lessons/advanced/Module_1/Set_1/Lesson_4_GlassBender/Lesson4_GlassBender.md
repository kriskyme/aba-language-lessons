## Governing CSV objective (Level 5, Listening/Speaking, Module 1: Describing)

> Listening: Can listen to an extended spoken description of a familiar or semi-familiar place or
> object, summarize how it's organized, and identify one clearly evaluative word choice that
> signals the speaker's opinion even though the speaker never states the opinion directly.

## DAY 1: Unit A - Listening

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

- **K-W-L Walk:** post three large sheets around the room labeled "What I Know," "What I Want to
  Know," and "What I Learned," each with the heading "Neon Signs and Glasswork." Students
  circulate in small groups, writing one sticky note per sheet on the first two columns only
  (leave "What I Learned" blank for now). After two minutes, a few groups share what they wrote.
- **Listening Skill Spotlight:** "Today we're practicing listening for signposting language,
  noticing the specific words and phrases a speaker uses to signal that they're about to explain,
  contrast, or emphasize something." (This is the exact skill today's Listening Closing Transfer
  Check will ask students to reproduce.)
- Present the citation block above.
- Pre-teach the 6 target words using the context sentences. Gloss all three figurative chunks
  ("aha moment," "flow state," "comes alive").
- **Background Note 1** (neon's 1910s-1920s origin) and **Background Note 2** (*createid*), both
  brief and informational only.
- Return to the K-W-L sheets: "Today's video follows one real craftsman making one real object,
  start to finish. Listen for the exact words he uses to walk you through the steps."
- Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback: Level 4/5 listen for what Kirkman makes
  and how he describes his craft, including one comparison he draws; Level 6/7 listen for how the
  video's tone shifts between technical explanation and personal reflection.

**Phase 2: Watch & Listening Notes (15 min)**

- Watch the full episode once. While watching, students fill in a two-column organizer: "The
  Steps (What He Does)" vs. "The Meaning (What He Says About It)" - rows for bending, filling with
  gas, and making the final connections.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

- Teach "listen for signposting language" directly, modeling on real content from **[Segment 5]**:
  Kirkman's line "The most critical part of the process you would think is making it, but
  actually, it's the filling." Point out that "you would think... but actually" is a signpost
  phrase that flags a coming contrast, telling the listener something surprising is about to be
  said before the surprising part even arrives.
- Guided practice: replay **[Segment 4]** and have students identify the signpost phrase "I'm not
  dissing LEDs... but neon is my heart," noting how "but" signals the shift from a polite
  concession to Kirkman's real opinion.

**Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

- Each student picks one task Level and completes it:
  - **Level 4:** In 2-3 sentences, describe what Wil Kirkman makes and how, including one
    comparison he draws and the reason he gives for preferring
    neon over LEDs. Add one row to the two-column organizer from Phase 2. **Respectful Tiers
    check:** also ask - "According to the video, what happens if a tube isn't heated hot enough
    before filling?" then "Do you think this craft is worth keeping alive even though LEDs are
    easier? Circle yes or no and say why." - a genuine simplified interpretive step, not
    fact-retrieval alone.
    - **Answer note:** the comparison: hot glass compared to taffy.
  - **Level 5:** Summarize how the video is organized and identify one evaluative word or phrase
    that signals Kirkman's opinion without him stating it directly as "I like this best." Note the
    organizational flow on the two-column organizer.
    - **Answer note:** organization: introducing Kirkman and his trade, then the science of the
      gases and colors, then the bending itself, then the emotional/flow-state description of the
      work, then powering the finished piece. Evaluative phrases: "neon is my heart," "that's
      really satisfying."
  - **Level 6:** List two stated facts and two clearly-signaled evaluative comments, keeping the
    two categories separate. Locate one clear tonal shift in the video and describe what changes
    in the language at that shift.
    - **Answer note:** facts: a transformer sends 22,000 volts through the tube; the two gases and
      rare earth coatings can produce about 140 different colors. Evaluative comments: Kirkman
      saying "I love the aha moment"; Kirkman saying "that's really satisfying" about seeing his
      finished work around town. Tonal shift: from technical, process-focused explanation of
      voltage and gases to a more personal, reflective tone in the closing lines.
  - **Level 7 (uses both sources):** Read a short paraphrased excerpt from Kirkman's own words in
    the Boise Art Scene interview (he does the work entirely by hand, with no jigs or templates,
    "kind of like paint by numbers"; he makes art because he wants to make it and says he doesn't
    care whether people like it or not; he considers himself very lucky to make his living this
    way) alongside the *createid* video. Ask: how does Kirkman's own first-person framing in the
    written interview differ in tone and focus from how the video profile frames the same craft?
    Identify one unstated interest or assumption behind one framing. Note two or three specific
    descriptive choices each source makes (what each chooses to show or say first) to ground the
    comparison.
    - **Answer note:** the video, as a public broadcasting arts profile, has reason to emphasize
      craft heritage and preservation ("nearly the last... glass bender") and present-tense
      process, while the interview, aimed at a local arts-scene readership, has reason to
      emphasize personal independence and authenticity.
- **Board-dependent moment:** students who worked on different Levels briefly compare and add
  their row(s) to the shared two-column organizer from Phase 2, so the full process-and-meaning
  picture exists only once multiple Levels have contributed.

**Phase 5: Listening Closing Transfer Check (10 min)**

- **Teacher-read-aloud script (invented, unrelated topic, recycling target vocabulary and
  applying the day's actual skill - signposting language):**
  > An old-fashioned jukebox restorer has worked in her trade for over fifteen years. You would
  > think the hardest part is polishing the wooden case, but actually, it's rebuilding the wiring
  > inside. She replaces the transformer that has weakened with age, and reconnects every wire
  > into one working circuit. Before that, she strips away a cracked coating of old varnish,
  > careful not to let the cleaning solvent vaporize too quickly in the summer heat. Any leftover
  > impurity in a single solder joint can stop the whole machine from lighting up again.
  > (recycles trade, transformer, circuit, coating, vaporize, impurity - all 6 target words - on a

- Teacher reads this aloud once. Students do not read it themselves first - printed in the student
  packet only after the response space.
- Students state, out loud to a partner, the signpost phrase they heard (You would think... but
  actually) and what it signaled was coming next (that the wiring, not the polishing, is the
  hardest part). Teacher cold-calls two or three pairs. After sharing, students may read the
  script to check.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Concentric Circles (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing making comparisons, using clear language to say how two
  things are alike or different, and which one you prefer and why." This is the exact skill
  today's Speaking Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.
- Model directly from the real source, **[Segment 4]**: Kirkman comparing neon signs to LEDs -
  "When I first started, illuminated signs were all neon. And now it's LEDs, and they don't have
  that warm glow that reaches out to you and sucks you in. I'm not dissing LEDs, they have their
  place. But neon is my heart." Point out the comparison structure: naming both things, giving one
  concrete difference, then stating a clear preference with a reason.
- Teach comparison frames: "Unlike ___, ___ actually ___." / "___ has its place, but ___ is what I
  really ___." / "Compared to ___, ___ is more ___ because ___."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

- Each student picks one task Level:
  - **Level 4:** Complete: "Unlike ___, neon signs actually ___." using a familiar idea from the
    video. Then describe Wil Kirkman's craft in your own original words, including one comparison
    and a stated reason, choosing your own connectors.
  - **Level 5:** Give an extended description of Kirkman's studio or process, organizing details
    logically and including one evaluative word.
  - **Level 6:** Describe Kirkman's craft in a fluent, integrated way, explicitly explaining why
    one feature (the flow state he describes, the century-old technique, the finished sign
    "coming alive") matters, and adjusting your register once in response to a partner's
    reaction.
  - **Level 7:** Describe Kirkman's craft using idiomatic phrasing, then self-correct one word
    choice mid-sentence on purpose, modeling the kind of real-time repair fluent speakers do.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

- Teach contrastive stress (putting extra vocal emphasis on the specific word being contrasted)
  using Kirkman's real comparison line: "I'm not DISSING LEDs, they have their place. But NEON is
  my HEART." Model how the stressed words carry the actual contrast, while the surrounding words
  are said more quickly and lightly.
- Students mark the stressed words in two more comparison sentences built from the lesson's real
  content, then check their marking by listening again, then apply it in brief paired rehearsal of
  Phase 2's comparisons, exaggerating the stress on the contrasted words rather than speaking in a
  flat, even rhythm.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Concentric Circles (20 min)**

- **Differentiated participation:** Level 4 prepares and rehearses one comparison sentence with a
  partner before joining the circles, so their first rotation is a rehearsed line rather than
  fully spontaneous speech.
- Form two circles, an inner circle facing out and an outer circle facing in, each student paired
  with one partner across from them. Prompt: compare Kirkman's neon craft to a modern technology
  that could replace it (LEDs, digital signage, or another example students suggest), stating a
  preference and a reason, using Phase 1's comparison frames. After each exchange, the outer
  circle rotates one spot to a new partner and the prompt repeats with a new comparison (old
  craft skills vs. new tools in general; slow, handmade work vs. fast, mass-produced work).
- **Board-dependent moment:** running board record of each comparison pair students actually
  produce during the rotations, feeding the Closing Transfer Check.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

- Every student, in pairs and out loud, produces a new comparison on a topic unrelated to the
  source (comparing two ways of doing something they do often, such as texting versus calling a
  friend, or cooking at home versus ordering food), using Phase 1's frames and today's
  contrastive-stress pattern. Teacher cold-calls two or three pairs.
- **Respectful Tiers check:** across the whole cycle, did Level 4 get a genuine interpretive step
  (Day 1) and a real production role (Day 2's rehearsed comparison feeding into Concentric
  Circles), not fact-retrieval and silent participation only?
- Brief reflection: "What's one traditional skill or trade near you that you think is at risk of
  disappearing, and why?" No Part 2/presentation-project extension is being used for this cycle
  (not yet built).
