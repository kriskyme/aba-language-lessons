Unit 5A: A Park in the Sky

Listening & Speaking · Describing Advanced 1.2.5.1

Objective: I can predict what a speaker is about to say from what they have already said, then listen to check whether I was right.

Good to Know

The High Line is an elevated freight railway on the west side of Manhattan, built in the 1930s to lift trains off the street. The last train ran in 1980, the structure stood empty for nearly thirty years, and the first section reopened as a public park in 2009.

The two speakers had different jobs on the same project. Robert Hammond was a neighbor who co-founded the group that campaigned to stop the city demolishing the structure. James Corner is the landscape architect whose firm designed what was built on it.

Words to Know

1. derelictleft to fall apart from long neglect; run-down and unused
2. liabilitysomething that causes trouble, cost, or risk to whoever owns it
3. eyesoresomething so ugly that people notice it and wish it were gone
4. amenitiesthe useful or pleasant things a place offers, such as cafes, seating, or shops
5. plantingsthe flowers, grasses, shrubs, and trees deliberately put into a space
6. skyrocketto rise extremely fast and by a very large amount
Phrase Spotlight
a leap of faith: a decision to go ahead with something before you can be sure it will work.
get a kick out of it: to enjoy something in a small, delighted, slightly surprised way.
a victim of its own success: so popular that the popularity itself has become the problem.
it's not Disney: a way of saying a place feels genuine rather than built to entertain, using a theme-park company's name to stand for the polished and artificial.
You'll watch: "Above Manhattan's bustle, a reshaped public space," a PBS NewsHour segment in which a reporter walks the High Line in New York with James Corner, the landscape architect who designed it.

Task D ★★★★

This task compares two real sources about the same park. The first is the PBS segment you watched, recorded in 2016, in which the reporter walks the finished park with James Corner, the landscape architect who designed it. The second is a recorded talk given in 2011 by Robert Hammond, the co-founder of the group that saved the structure, when only the first section was open. Each row below gives each source's own words on a single subject.

What the abandoned structure looked like PBS segment: Corner says "the High Line was really perceived by a lot of people to be a liability, derelict, abandoned, dangerous, dark, drugs, crime." and "it was a beautifully surprising and delightful one-and-a-half-mile ribbon of green." 2011 talk: Hammond says "what first attracted me, or interested me, was this view from the street, which is this steel structure, sort of rusty, this industrial relic." and "when I went up on top, it was a mile and a half of wildflowers running right through the middle of Manhattan with views of the Empire State Building and the Statue of Liberty and the Hudson River."
Why the park worked PBS segment: Corner says "We believed that if we were to simply leverage that, make it more of a garden than it was then... that there would be something very charming about that." and "We amplify found conditions." 2011 talk: Hammond says "even though I love the designs that we were building, I was always frightened that I wouldn't really love it, because I fell in love with that wildscape, and how could you recreate that magic?" and "what I found is it's in the people and how they use it that, to me, makes it so special."
Money and the neighborhood PBS segment: the reporter, walking beside Corner, says "property values around it have skyrocketed, pushing all but the rich, individuals and companies, further away." 2011 talk: Hammond says "we said over a 20-year time period, the value to the city in increased property values and increased taxes would be about 250 million. That was enough. It really got the city behind it." and "Now people estimate it's created about a half a billion dollars... in tax revenues for the city." and "the city has planned, for better or for worse, 12 million square-feet of development that the High Line is going to ring around."
What the place does to the people on it PBS segment: Corner says "It affects people's psyche. It affects their imagination... In a sense, they're theatricalizing everyday life." and "We're social animals... we also want to have a place to participate with other people." 2011 talk: Hammond says "I realized right after we opened that there were all these people holding hands on the High Line. And I realized New Yorkers don't hold hands; we just don't do that outside." and "that's the power that public space can have to transform how people experience their city and interact with each other."

Unit 5B: A Park in the Sky

Listening & Speaking · Describing Advanced 1.2.5.1

Objective: I can make a general statement and then give a specific example that shows it is true.

Learn the Phrase
Giving Examples

Robert Hammond announces his example out loud before he gives it: "Just one quick example is I realized right after we opened that there were all these people holding hands on the High Line." A claim about what makes the park special, then one small, concrete, checkable thing.

James Corner puts the general idea first and the examples second: "People want the cosmopolitanism, the diversity, the exposure, the amenities that a city provides. They want restaurants and cafes and theaters and museums, and parks and public spaces."

Now it's your turn. In the tasks below, you'll give examples using frames like these:

  • • "For example, ___."
  • • "Just one quick example is ___."
  • • "Take ___, for instance."
  • • "___, like ___ and ___."
Practice It

Every task below is about a public place you already know well: a park, a square, a station, a market, or a street you walk often. Pick one task. When you're done, find someone who worked on a different task and take turns sharing.

Task A ★

  1. 1.
    Describe your place in a few sentences, choosing your own connectors. Include one comparison ("It's like ___") with a brief reason for it, and give one example using a frame above.

  1. 1.

    Describe your place twice to the same partner, using different examples each time: once so a listener would want to go there, once so a listener would think it had been neglected. Every fact stays true both times. Somewhere in the second version, catch one imprecise word of your own and repair it aloud as you go.
Say It Like a Native Speaker

When English speakers list examples, the voice steps up on each item and falls on the last one. That fall is how a listener hears that the list is finished.

Mark where the voice rises and where it finally falls on each of these two real lines, then listen again to check.

  • • "They want restaurants and cafes and theaters and museums, and parks and public spaces."
  • • "derelict, abandoned, dangerous, dark, drugs, crime."

Then say your own example list from Practice It twice, once flat and once with the rise and fall, and tell your partner which one sounded finished.

Discuss It

Your teacher will run this as a whole-class discussion and call on speakers. Every time you speak, include at least one concrete example, introduced with a starter phrase from the list. Use the questions below in order.

1.
A city has an old, unused structure taking up space. Should public money turn it into a park? Give one example that supports your position.
2.
Around the High Line, property values skyrocketed and long-time residents and businesses moved away. Is that a fair price for a public space that millions of people use?
3.
What run-down or unused place near here could become somewhere people would travel to see, and what specific things would you put in it?
4.
While others are speaking, keep a list here: the speaker's name and the concrete example they gave. You may be asked to read one back.
