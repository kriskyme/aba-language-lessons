## Governing CSV objective (Level 5, Listening/Speaking, Module 1: Describing)

> Listening: Can listen to an extended spoken description of a familiar or semi-familiar place or
> object, summarize how it's organized, and identify one clearly evaluative word choice that
> signals the speaker's opinion even though the speaker never states the opinion directly.
> Speaking: Can give an extended description, organizing details logically and including at least
> one clearly evaluative word choice that signals an opinion without stating it outright, working
> independently.

Listening half governs Day 1's task-Level basis column; Speaking half governs Day 2's.

## Target vocabulary (grounded in the real transcript)

derelict (adj) - left to fall apart from long neglect; run-down and unused
liability (n) - something that causes trouble, cost, or risk to whoever owns it
eyesore (n) - something so ugly that people notice it and wish it were gone
amenities (n) - the useful or pleasant things a place offers, such as cafes, seating, or shops
plantings (n) - the flowers, grasses, shrubs, and trees deliberately put into a space
skyrocket (v) - to rise extremely fast and by a very large amount

Context sentences (paraphrased from the source, not quoted verbatim):

1. For nearly thirty years after the last train, the structure sat derelict above 10th Avenue.
2. Most New Yorkers treated the old railway as a liability, something the city would have to pay
   to take down.
3. Jeffrey Brown calls the abandoned line an eyesore that nobody could think of a use for.
4. Corner says people are moving back into cities for the amenities that only a city offers:
   restaurants, theaters, museums, parks.
5. The plantings along the walkway were chosen to make it feel like nature had taken the structure
   back on its own.
6. Since the park opened, property values on the blocks around it have skyrocketed.

**Figurative chunks to gloss (Advanced band):**

- **"a leap of faith"** - a decision to go ahead with something before you can be sure it will work
  (Corner: "It was a huge effort and a big leap of faith")
- **"get a kick out of it"** - to enjoy something in a small, delighted, slightly surprised way
  (Corner: "people get a kick out of it")
- **"a victim of its own success"** - so popular that the popularity itself has become the problem
  (Brown: "the High Line may be a victim of its own success")
- **"It's not Disney"** - here, a way of saying a place feels genuine rather than built to
  entertain, using a theme-park company's name to stand for the polished and artificial
  (Corner: "It's real and authentic. It's not Disney.")

**Background Note 1:** the High Line is an elevated freight railway on Manhattan's West Side, built
in the 1930s to lift freight trains off the street. The last train ran in 1980, the structure stood
empty for nearly thirty years, and the first section reopened as a public park in 2009. Both of
today's speakers give this history in their own words.

**Background Note 2:** the two speakers had different jobs on the same project. Robert Hammond was
a neighbor who co-founded Friends of the High Line, the group that campaigned to stop the city
demolishing the structure. James Corner is the landscape architect whose firm, with the architects
Diller Scofidio and Renfro and the Dutch garden designer Piet Oudolf, designed what was built on it.

- **Listening Skill Spotlight:** "Today we're practicing predicting from context before confirming:
  using what a speaker has already said to guess what is coming next, then listening to check
  whether we were right." (This is the exact skill Phase 6's Closing
  Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.)
- Present the citation block above, both sources.
- Pre-teach the 6 target words using the context sentences. Gloss all four figurative chunks.

- Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback: Level 4 and 5 listen for what the place

**Phase 2: First Watch & Listening Notes (15 min)**

- Play the full PBS segment once, start to finish (6:32).
- **No wording on the page or the screen.** Captions off and the screen's own subtitles turned off, no transcript on the page or the board, and the Phase 5 windows not yet visible. This is the students' one
  uncued first contact with the source and it cannot be given back.
- While watching, students fill a T-chart organizer printed in the packet: "What was here before"
  against "What is here now," with a third narrow column, "The words they use for it," where
  students copy the actual word or phrase a speaker chose for that row.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

- Teach "predict from context before confirming" directly, modeling on real content from
  **[Segment 2]**. Replay to Brown's line and stop the audio mid-sentence, at: "the site wasted
  away, an eyesore that no one could figure out what to do with, until they did:". Ask the class
  what they expect to hear next and why the wording "until they did" is the signal to predict.
  Take two or three guesses on the board, then play on to confirm: "Create a new kind of public
  park."
- Guided practice on a second short excerpt, from **[Segment 6]**. Stop after Brown's "If anything,
  the High Line may be a victim of its own success." Students write one prediction of what problem
  comes next, then listen to check: "It can be clogged with people, and property values around it
  have skyrocketed."
- Name what made both predictions possible: a phrase that promises a turn ("until they did," "a
  victim of its own success") tells you something is coming before it arrives.

**Phase 4: Second Watch & Differentiated Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

This phase runs in three beats inside its own twenty minutes.

1. **Commit (about 10 min).** Students answer their chosen Level's task below in writing, from the first
   watch and their Phase 2 organizer, before anything is replayed.
2. **Second watch (about 5 min).** Play the source a second time, still with no wording on the page, under
   this narrower purpose, which is not Phase 2's: Level 4 and 5 count the different things the segment says the High Line was before it was a park; Level 6 and 7 listen only for the sentences about crowds and property values, and note which speaker says each one.
3. **Revise (about 5 min).** Students change whatever they now hear differently, and mark which of their
   answers moved. What moved is what Phase 5 checks.

Each student picks one task Level and completes it.

- **Level 4:**
  1. In 2-3 sentences, describe what this place was before and what it is now, choosing your own
     connectors.
  2. Write down one comparison Corner makes between the park and something else, and the reason he
     gives for it.
     - **Answer note:** Corner compares the raised walkway to a balcony or a catwalk, and to a
       theater; his reason is that people on it are watching each other and some of them want to be
       seen. He also compares the park to Disney by contrast, his reason being that the High Line
       is real and authentic.
  3. Add one row to the class T-chart from Phase 2, with the speaker's own word for it.
  4. Corner says the park is "real and authentic. It's not Disney." Circle the one you would
     rather visit and write one sentence saying why: **a place that feels finished and polished**
     or **a place that feels found and a little wild**.
     - **Answer note:** either choice is correct. Look for a reason that names something from the
       segment (the self-seeded plants, the old rails left in the paving, the crowds) rather than a
       general preference.

- **Level 5:**
  1. Summarize how this description is organized, in order, in 3-4 sentences.
     - **Answer note:** it opens on the tracks with the designer pointing at a detail underfoot,
       goes back to the line's history as freight and then as an abandoned structure, returns to
       why the design worked, moves to what the park does to the people on it, widens out to
       Corner's other work and why people want cities again, and closes on the costs of the park's
       success and what is being built next.
  2. Identify one evaluative word choice by which Corner signals his opinion of the place without
     ever saying he likes it, and write what the opinion is.
     - **Answer note:** "beautifully surprising and delightful," "charming," "a sort of strange, haunting
       quality," "irresistible." Any of these signals that he found the abandoned line worth keeping
       and beautiful as he found it, which he never states outright.
  3. Note the organizational flow on the class T-chart from Phase 2.

- **Level 6:**
  1. List two stated facts and two clearly-signaled evaluative comments from the segment, keeping
     the two lists separate.
     - **Answer note:** facts: the tracks were built in the 1930s, 30 feet above street level; the
       last train ran in 1980; the first section opened in 2009. Evaluative comments: "I think this
       is what a lot of people like"; "It's real and authentic. It's not Disney"; "there would be
       something very charming about that"; "I believe it is now, perhaps more than ever, more
       fundamental to what it means to be a democracy."
  2. Locate one clear tonal shift in the segment and describe what changes in the language at that
     point.
     - **Answer note:** the shift from the joking on-site exchange over the Sleepwalker sculpture
       ("But is he going to wink at us?") to Corner's closing statement about social animals,
       democracy and tolerance: playful second-person banter gives way to abstract nouns and a
       formal register.
  3. One of the two evaluative comments you listed was made by the reporter, not the designer, or
     could have been. Say which speaker made each of your four items and why that matters to how
     the park comes across.
     - **Answer note:** every hard criticism in the segment is Brown's, not Corner's: "an eyesore,"
       "a victim of its own success," "pushing all but the rich further away." Corner's evaluative
       language is all warm. The park comes across as admired by the person who made it and
       questioned only by the person reporting on it.

- **Level 7 (uses both sources):** both speakers' own words are printed below, paired subject by
  subject, so the comparison can be made from the page. Corner is the landscape architect, walking
  the finished park with PBS in 2016. Hammond is the co-founder, on a TED stage in 2011 with only
  the first section open.

  - *What the abandoned structure looked like.*
    - Corner **[Segment 3]**: "the High Line was really perceived by a lot of people to be a
      liability, derelict, abandoned, dangerous, dark, drugs, crime." and "it was a beautifully
      surprising and delightful one-and-a-half-mile ribbon of green."
    - Hammond **[1:30]**: "what first attracted me, or interested me, was this view from the street
      -- which is this steel structure, sort of rusty, this industrial relic." and "when I went up
      on top, it was a mile and a half of wildflowers running right through the middle of Manhattan
      with views of the Empire State Building and the Statue of Liberty and the Hudson River."
  - *Why the park worked.*
    - Corner **[Segment 3]**: "We believed that if we were to simply leverage that, make it more of
      a garden than it was then... that there would be something very charming about that." and
      **[Segment 1]**: "We amplify found conditions."
    - Hammond **[4:28]**: "even though I love the designs that we were building, I was always
      frightened that I wouldn't really love it, because I fell in love with that wildscape -- and
      how could you recreate that magic?" and "what I found is it's in the people and how they use
      it that, to me, makes it so special."
  - *Money and the neighborhood.*
    - Corner's segment **[Segment 6]**, the reporter speaking beside him: "property values around
      it have skyrocketed, pushing all but the rich, individuals and companies, further away."
    - Hammond **[2:01]**: "we said over a 20-year time period, the value to the city in increased
      property values and increased taxes would be about 250 million. That was enough. It really
      got the city behind it." and "Now people estimate it's created about a half a billion
      dollars... in tax revenues for the city." and **[3:51]**: "the city has planned -- for better
      or for worse -- 12 million square-feet of development that the High Line is going to ring
      around."
  - *What the place does to the people on it.*
    - Corner **[Segment 4]**: "It affects people's psyche. It affects their imagination... In a
      sense, they're theatricalizing everyday life." and **[Segment 6]**: "We're social animals...
      we also want to have a place to participate with other people."
    - Hammond **[4:28]**: "I realized right after we opened that there were all these people
      holding hands on the High Line. And I realized New Yorkers don't hold hands; we just don't do
      that outside." and "that's the power that public space can have to transform how people
      experience their city and interact with each other."

  1. Working only from the words printed above, write what impression of the High Line each speaker
     leaves, and name the choices in their wording that build it.
  2. Name one interest or assumption behind one speaker's framing that neither speaker states
     outright, and point to the words that led you to it.
     - **Answer note:** Hammond argues in numbers. Three of his four subjects arrive with a figure
       attached, the neighborhood appears as property values and tax revenue on the city's side of
       the ledger, and the 12 million square feet of development next door is waved past inside a
       four-word aside. Corner argues in sensation: charm, psyche, theater, delight, social animals.
       Money and the people priced out enter his segment only in the reporter's voice. The unstated
       interest behind Hammond's framing is that in 2011 he still needed the city's money and
       permission, so his account has to prove the park pays for itself and cannot afford to dwell
       on who rising property values push out. The unstated assumption behind Corner's is that the
       design is the reason the place works: "We amplify found conditions" credits the firm for the
       wildness, while Hammond says plainly that the wildscape arrived on its own after the trains
       stopped and that what makes the park special is the people, not the design. Accept any
       reading argued from the printed words.

- **Board-dependent moment:** students who worked at different Levels add their rows and findings

  the reporter's, exist only once several Levels have contributed.

- **Item pacing:** Level 4 has four short steps, Level 5 three, Level 6 three, Level 7 two long

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Town Hall (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing giving examples: making a general statement, then naming
  something specific that shows it is true." This is the exact skill today's Speaking Closing
  Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.
- Model directly from the real sources. Hammond, **[4:28]**, says the flat announcement out loud:
  "Just one quick example is I realized right after we opened that there were all these people
  holding hands on the High Line." Point out the shape: a claim about what makes the park special,
  then one small, concrete, checkable thing.
- Second model, Corner, **[Segment 5]**: "People want the cosmopolitanism, the diversity, the
  exposure, the amenities that a city provides. They want restaurants and cafes and theaters and
  museums, and parks and public spaces." The general noun comes first, the list of concrete
  examples second.
- Teach the frames: "For example, ___." / "Just one quick example is ___." / "Take ___, for
  instance." / "___, like ___ and ___."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Each student picks one task Level. Every Level describes a public place the student already knows
well and can describe from memory: a park, a square, a station, a market, a street they walk often.
The place is chosen from the student's own life, so nothing has to be looked up or brought in.

- **Level 4:** Describe your place in a few sentences, choosing your own connectors. Include one
  comparison ("It's like ___") with a brief reason for it, and give one example using a frame from
  Phase 1.
- **Level 5:** Give an extended description of your place, organized in a clear order, using at
  least two example frames, and including one evaluative word choice that lets a listener hear your
  opinion of the place without your stating it.
- **Level 6:** Describe your place fluently, moving between what is there and why it matters, and
  explain explicitly why one feature of it matters. Watch your partner, and when they react, adjust
  your register or your approach once in response.
- **Level 7:** Describe your place twice to the same partner, using different examples each time:
  once so that a listener would want to go there, once so that a listener would think it had been
  neglected. Every fact stays true both times; only the selection and emphasis change. Somewhere in
  the second version, catch one imprecise word of your own and repair it aloud as you go.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

- Teach list intonation, the rise-and-fall pattern English uses when a speaker gives several
  examples: the voice steps up on each item and falls on the last one, which is how a listener
  hears that the list is finished.
- Model from the real audio, **[Segment 5]**: replay Corner's "They want restaurants and cafes and
  theaters and museums, and parks and public spaces." Play it twice and have students mark on the
  printed line where the voice rises and where it finally falls.
- Students mark a second printed line from the segment the same way, **[Segment 3]**: Corner's
  "derelict, abandoned, dangerous, dark, drugs, crime." Then check both markings against the real
  audio.
- Apply it: in pairs, students say their own Phase 2 example lists aloud twice, once flat and once
  with the rise-and-fall, and tell each other which one sounded finished.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Town Hall (20 min)**

- The class runs a town hall from where students sit. The teacher takes the chair and calls on
  speakers; every contribution has to include at least one concrete example, introduced with a
  Phase 1 frame.
- **Three rotated prompts,** roughly six minutes each:
  1. A city has an old, unused structure taking up space. Should public money turn it into a park?
     Give one example that supports your position.
  2. Around the High Line, property values skyrocketed and long-time residents and businesses moved
     away. Is that a fair price for a public space that millions of people use?
  3. What run-down or unused place near here could become somewhere people would travel to see, and
     what specific things would you put in it?
- **Active task for every non-speaker:** keep a running examples ledger in the packet, one line per
  speaker: the speaker's name and the concrete example they gave. Anyone may be called on to read
  back an example someone else gave, so the ledger has to be kept as the round goes.
- **Differentiated participation:** Level 4 students pair up and rehearse one full contribution
  with a partner, out loud, before the first prompt opens, so their first turn in the public round
  is a line they have already said once.
- **Foundation Support:** for a student working below Level 4, the role is the ledger itself, read
  back aloud when the teacher asks for it, plus one prepared line written with a partner during the
  rehearsal minute and delivered at a natural pause.
- **Board-dependent moment:** the teacher writes each concrete example on the board as it is
  actually spoken, in the order it is spoken. The finished board list is the class's own, exists
  nowhere in any packet, and feeds Phase 5.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

- Every student, in pairs and out loud, makes one general statement about something they do often
  outside of class (how they get to school, what they cook, how they spend a free evening) and
  supports it with two concrete examples, using a Phase 1 frame and today's list intonation. The
  topic is deliberately not a public place, so the skill has to move off the lesson's own subject.
  Teacher cold-calls two or three pairs.
- **Respectful Tiers check:** across the cycle, did Level 4 get a genuine interpretive step on
  Day 1 (the polished-or-found choice with a reason, not fact-retrieval alone) and a real
  production role on Day 2 (a rehearsed contribution that goes into the public Town Hall round,
  not silent participation)?
- Brief reflection, one bounded question: "Look at the board list of examples. Which single example
  that someone else gave today would change your mind, and what about it does the work?"
