## Governing CSV objective (Level 5, Listening/Speaking, Module 1: Describing)

> Listening: Can listen to an extended spoken description of a familiar or semi-familiar place or
> object, summarize how it's organized, and identify one clearly evaluative word choice that
> signals the speaker's opinion even though the speaker never states the opinion directly.
> Speaking: Can give an extended description, organizing details logically and including at least
> one clearly evaluative word choice that signals an opinion without stating it outright, working
> independently.

Listening half governs Day 1's task-Level basis column; Speaking half governs Day 2's.

## Target vocabulary (grounded in the real transcript)

interference (n) - unwanted signals that get in the way of the signal you are trying to receive
frequency (n) - the particular band a radio signal travels on
readings (n, plural) - the measurements an instrument takes
radius (n) - the distance from a centre point outward in every direction
relay (v) - to pass a message on from one point to the next
content (adj) - satisfied with the way things are, not wanting a change

Context sentences (paraphrased from the source, not quoted verbatim):

1. Energy from a Wi-Fi router is enough to cause interference with the telescope.
2. One local station survives inside the zone because it broadcasts on a low enough frequency.
3. Stray signals can confuse the readings the telescope takes.
4. An observatory engineer drives a 20-mile radius around the telescope every week.
5. During a storm that took out the phone lines, ham radio operators could relay news across the
   county.
6. Hu ends by saying the people there seem content with the way things are.

**Figurative chunks to gloss (Advanced band):**

- **"skipped over"** - passed by without stopping, so left out of something everyone else got
  (Hu: "a place the wireless revolution skipped over")
- **"chit-chat"** - light, unimportant conversation for its own sake
  (Hu: "some of them chit-chat")
- **"run afoul of"** - to get into trouble with someone, usually by breaking their rule
  (Hu: "without running afoul of the astronomers")
- **"change is in the air"** - something is clearly about to change, though nothing has yet
  (Hu: "So change is in the mountain air here in West Virginia")

**Background Note 1:** the Green Bank Telescope is a radio telescope in Green Bank, West Virginia. It
sits at the centre of the National Radio Quiet Zone, a region of about 13,000 square miles in which
new radio transmitters have to be coordinated with the observatory so that the telescope can work.

**Background Note 2:** ham radio is a licensed hobby in which people use two-way radios to talk
directly to one another over long distances, without going through a phone network or the internet.

**Background Note 3:** this report was broadcast in 2013, and it calls the site the National Radio
Astronomy Observatory. The site is now called the Green Bank Observatory, and the observatory's own
description of the quiet zone is the second source in this lesson's top task.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

- Teach "listen for contrast and concession" directly, modeling on real content from
  **[Segment 3]**. Replay Hu's line: "Unlike the tube-shaped optical telescopes you might be thinking
  of, a radio telescope looks like a giant satellite dish." Point out that the sentence starts by
  naming what the listener is probably already picturing, and only then says what is actually there.
  Put the two halves on the board: what you expect, what is true.
- Guided practice on a second short excerpt, from **[Segment 6]**. Play Niday's "It's nice to be able
  to just pull something out of your pocket and send a message to someone, and get a response within
  30 seconds or so. But I don't know that it's that necessary." Students write what he gives ground
  on and what he actually thinks, and circle the word that turns the sentence.
- Name the pattern: a speaker who is about to disagree often agrees first. The word that turns it
  ("unlike," "but," "still," "at least") is the signal that the real point is coming.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Jigsaw (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing asking for clarification: a short question that checks or
  narrows what someone has just said, instead of nodding and hoping." This is the exact skill today's
  Speaking Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.
- Model directly from the real source. Twice in the piece the reporter stops and asks a short question
  rather than moving on: **[Segment 1]** "How many bars do you have?", answered "Zero. Searching.";
  and **[Segment 2]** "For fun?", which is what turns a mention of ham radios into Pat Schaffner's
  account of the storm. Point out how little a clarification question has to be: three words, aimed at
  one thing.
- Teach the frames: "Sorry, do you mean ___ or ___?" / "When you say ___, what exactly do you mean?" /
  "Can you say more about ___?" / "So is that ___?"

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Each student picks one task Level. Every Level works on the same thing: something people usually get
wrong about a place, a hobby, or a job the student knows well. Choose it from your own life; nothing
has to be looked up or brought in. Work in pairs, and while one partner speaks the other listens for
something to ask about and puts one clarification question from Phase 1, then swap.

- **Level 4:** Say what people usually think, then what it is really like, in a few sentences,
  choosing your own connectors. Include one comparison ("It's not like ___, it's more like ___") with
  a brief reason for it.
- **Level 5:** Give an extended description of the thing and the misunderstanding, organized in a
  clear order, giving ground at least twice before you correct it, and include one evaluative word
  choice that lets a listener hear your opinion without your stating it.
- **Level 6:** Describe it fluently, moving between what people think and what is true, and explain
  explicitly why one part of the misunderstanding matters. Watch your partner, and when they react,
  adjust your register or your approach once in response.
- **Level 7:** Give the misunderstanding its strongest form first, well enough that a listener would
  believe it, then take it apart. Every fact stays true in both halves; only the selection and the
  emphasis change. Somewhere in the second half, catch one imprecise word of your own and repair it
  aloud as you go.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

- Teach reduced forms in fast speech: in ordinary speed English the small function words shrink and
  run together, so "kind of" arrives as "kinda" and "to be able to" as something closer to "tuh be
  able tuh." Nothing is being dropped carelessly; this is what the words normally sound like at speed.
- Model from the real audio, **[Segment 2]**: replay Caleb Diller's "we were kind of, you know, back
  in time a little bit." Play it twice and have students write what "kind of" actually sounds like.
- Students do the same for a second printed line, **[Segment 6]**: Niday's "It's nice to be able to
  just pull something out of your pocket and send a message to someone." Then check both against the
  real audio.
- Apply it: in pairs, students say one clarification question from Phase 1 twice, once with every word
  fully pronounced and once at natural speed, and tell each other which one sounded like a real
  question rather than a recitation.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Jigsaw (20 min)**

- Students form groups of four from where they sit. Within each group, and without anybody moving, the
  four members take one topic each from the piece: (A) what the zone actually restricts, (B) how people
  in the zone stay in touch, (C) the telescope and why the quiet is needed, (D) what is changing. Each
  works alone for three minutes from their own notes and packet, then teaches their topic to the other
  three at the same table.
- **Active task for every non-speaker:** while a group member teaches, each listener writes one line of
  what they heard and asks at least one clarification question from Phase 1, noting the answer. Every
  listener asks at least once per round.
- **Three rotated prompts** for the group once all four topics have been taught, roughly three minutes
  each:
  1. Of the four things you have just heard, which would matter most to someone deciding whether to
     move here?
  2. In one sentence, how would you describe this place to somebody who has never heard of it?
  3. Is there anywhere you know where a rule changes how people use technology? Describe it.
- **Differentiated participation:** Level 4 students take their topic and rehearse their teaching turn
  with one partner in the group, out loud, before the round starts, so their first turn is a line they
  have already said once.
- **Foundation Support:** for a student working below Level 4, the role is the listener's line-and-
  question record, read back aloud when the group asks for it, plus one prepared sentence written with
  a partner during the rehearsal minutes and delivered at a natural pause.
- **Board-dependent moment:** each group reads out its one-sentence description from prompt 2, and the
  teacher builds a single class description on the board out of the words the groups actually used,
  cutting and merging live. The finished sentence exists in no packet.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

- Every student, in pairs and out loud, says one sentence about a plan they have for the week, and
  their partner asks two clarification questions using the Phase 1 frames, at natural speed rather
  than word by word. Then they swap. The topic is deliberately nothing to do with the quiet zone or
