Good to Know

Haute couture is clothing made to order for one client, fitted to that person and finished largely by hand. An embroidery house like Lesage does not sell finished clothes: it makes decoration to order for the designers who do.

Charles Frederick Worth, named at the end of the report, was a designer working in Paris in the second half of the 1800s, often described as the first high-fashion designer.

This report was broadcast in 2011. The second source in Task D is a magazine profile of the same house published in 2018.

This task compares two real accounts of the same workshop. The first is the radio report you listened to, broadcast in 2011. The second is a magazine profile of the same house, published in 2018 after a visit to the same rooms. Each row below gives each account's own words on a single subject.

The embroidery, seen up close Radio report: Stamberg says "Workers in white lab coats attach sequins, beads, rhinestones, shells, ribbons, feathers to pieces of air-thin fabric, which will adorn de luxe designs by the top names in fashion." and "The most delicate, exquisite, improbable sewing." The people who do the work Radio report: Stamberg says "The House of Lesage is a house of handwork, done by what the French call petit mains - little hands. They do it all." Angelique Ginguene says "The drawing, the sewing, the embroidery. It's very precious hands." and "They have to count how many blue pearl, how many red pearl the girl will need for realize embroidery." Magazine profile: "These artisans, who often work in the shadows, are the lifeblood of luxury brands." and "anything from 35 to 70 hand-workers, depending on workflow" and "must deliver embroideries for each collection within four weeks of receiving Lagerfeld's croquis."
Who owns the house Radio report: Stamberg says "Their chief designer, Karl Lagerfeld, bought Lesage in 2002 to ensure that this 130-year-old embroidery business would stay in business in a world of mass-produced, made-in-China clothing." The mural on the end wall of the sports hall was commissioned by the council for four hundred pounds. Twelve people worked on it over one summer. The design is intricate at the bottom, where children painted their own hands, and plain at the top, where nobody could reach. The paper drawings were so flimsy they tore in the wind. The photographs went into the library's archive. Nobody calls it adornment now. It is simply the wall, and the town repaints it every spring to perpetuate it.

Unit 8B: The House of Little Hands

Listening & Speaking · Describing Advanced 1.2.8.1

Objective: I can say clearly whether I go along with what someone has just said, and add my own reason.

Learn the Phrase
Agreeing and Disagreeing

The report is full of small agreements landing in real time. Checking a fabric, the reporter asks "Muslin or chiffon. Chiffon, no?" and Angelique Ginguene answers "Chiffon, yes. Exactly."

Later the reporter finishes a teacher's thought for her, "A dream since you were a little girl... to work in couture," and gets back a single word: "Exactly." Francois Lesage, making a claim about the past, backs himself the same way: "It's true." Each of these confirms one specific thing, not the whole conversation.

Now it's your turn. In the tasks below, you'll agree and disagree using frames like these:

  • • "Exactly."
  • • "That's true, and ___."
  • • "I'd go further: ___."
  • • "I see that, but ___."
  • • "I'd put it differently: ___."
  • • "I'm not sure about that, because ___."
Practice It

Every task below is about a workspace you know where somebody makes, mends or prepares something: a kitchen, a garage, a salon, a shop counter, a market stall. Choose it from memory. Work in pairs: when one of you finishes describing, the other gives an opinion about the place, and the describer agrees or disagrees using a frame above. Then swap. Pick one task.

Say It Like a Native Speaker

In connected speech, a word ending in a consonant runs straight into a word beginning with a vowel, and the join disappears: the two words arrive as one.

Listen again and draw a link mark wherever two words run together in each of these three real lines.

  • • "Everything, everything is possible to do."
  • • "And it's on this little, thin, flimsy piece of fabric."
  • • "It's very precious hands."

Then say one agreeing line and one disagreeing line twice, once with every word separated and once linked, and tell your partner which one sounded like a person rather than a list.
