## Governing CSV objective (Level 5, Listening/Speaking, Module 1: Describing)

> Listening: Can listen to an extended spoken description of a familiar or semi-familiar place or
> object, summarize how it's organized, and identify one clearly evaluative word choice that
> signals the speaker's opinion even though the speaker never states the opinion directly.
> Speaking: Can give an extended description, organizing details logically and including at least
> one clearly evaluative word choice that signals an opinion without stating it outright, working
> independently.

Listening half governs Day 1's task-Level basis column; Speaking half governs Day 2's.

## Target vocabulary (grounded in the real transcript)

adornment (n) - something added to a thing or a person to make it look richer or more striking
intricate (adj) - made of many small parts fitted together, so it takes close attention to follow
commission (v) - to order a piece of work specially, from the person who will make it
archive (n) - an organised store of material kept because of what it records
flimsy (adj) - thin and weak, so light that it seems it could tear
perpetuate (v) - to keep something going so that it does not die out

Context sentences (paraphrased from the source, not quoted verbatim):

1. What Lesage makes is adornment: it goes onto a dress that somebody else designed.
2. One top needed eight separate drawings, one for each intricate section of it.
3. Nothing here is sold by the yard; every piece is commissioned for one particular collection.
4. The house keeps an archive of tens of thousands of samples, filed by designer and season.
5. The beads are fixed to fabric so flimsy that the reporter can hardly believe it holds them.
6. Lesage started a school to perpetuate the craft at a time when work was scarce.

**Figurative chunks to gloss (Advanced band):**

- **"little hands"** - the French name for the people who do the handwork, translated literally
  (Stamberg: "done by what the French call petit mains - little hands")
- **"if butterflies could sew"** - an imagined comparison, used to say that work is impossibly fine
  and light (Stamberg: "If butterflies could sew, they would work for Lesage.")
- **"where the magic begins"** - the point at which something ordinary turns into something
  remarkable (Stamberg: "carried off to big embroidery frames where the magic begins")
- **"the City of Light"** - a long-standing nickname for Paris
  (Greene: "the oldest embroidery studio in the City of Light")

**Background Note 1:** haute couture is clothing made to order for one client, fitted to that person
and finished largely by hand. An embroidery house like Lesage does not sell finished clothes: it makes
decoration to order for the designers who do.

**Background Note 2:** Charles Frederick Worth, whom the report names at the end, was a designer
working in Paris in the second half of the 1800s, often described as the first high-fashion designer.

**Background Note 3:** this report was broadcast in 2011. The second source in this lesson's top task
is a magazine profile of the same house published in 2018.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

- Teach "listen for the main idea" directly, modeling on real content from **[Segment 4]**. Replay the

3. **Revise (about 5 min).** Students change whatever they now hear differently, and mark which of their
   answers moved. What moved is what Phase 5 checks.

Each student picks one task Level and completes it.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Rotating Partners (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing agreeing and disagreeing: saying clearly whether you go along
  with what someone has just said, and adding your own reason." This is the exact skill today's
  Speaking Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.
- Model directly from the real source. The report is full of small agreements landing in real time.
  **[Segment 4]**: Stamberg checks a fabric, "Muslin or chiffon. Chiffon, no?" and Ginguene answers
  "Chiffon, yes. Exactly." **[Segment 6]**: Stamberg finishes Marie-Lee Lyon's thought, "A dream since
  you were a little girl... to work in couture," and Lyon answers "Exactly." **[Segment 6]**: Lesage
  makes a claim about the past and then backs it himself, "It's true."
- Point out what the agreements do: each one confirms a specific thing, not the whole conversation.
- Teach the frames. Agreeing: "Exactly." / "That's true, and ___." / "I'd go further: ___."
  Disagreeing: "I see that, but ___." / "I'd put it differently: ___." / "I'm not sure about that,
  because ___."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Each student picks one task Level. Every Level describes the same kind of thing: a workspace the
student knows where somebody makes, mends or prepares something, such as a kitchen, a garage, a salon,
a shop counter or a market stall. Choose it from memory; nothing has to be visited or brought in. Work
in pairs: when one of you finishes describing, the other gives an opinion about the place, and the
describer agrees or disagrees using a frame from Phase 1. Then swap.

- **Level 4:** Describe the workspace in a few sentences, choosing your own connectors: what is in it,
  where things are kept, and what somebody would notice first. Include one comparison ("It's like
  ___") with a brief reason for it.
- **Level 5:** Give an extended description of the workspace in a clear order, and include one
  evaluative word choice that lets a listener hear your opinion of it without your stating it.
- **Level 6:** Describe the workspace fluently, moving between what is in it and why that matters, and
  explain explicitly why one feature of it matters. Watch your partner, and when they react, adjust
  your register or your approach once in response.
- **Level 7:** Describe the same workspace twice, to two different listeners in turn: first to someone
  who might come and work there, then to someone who might buy what is made there. Every fact stays
  true in both; what changes is which facts you choose and how formally you speak. Somewhere in the
  second description, catch one imprecise word of your own and repair it aloud as you go.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

- Teach linking: in connected speech a word that ends in a consonant runs straight into a word that
  begins with a vowel, so the join disappears and two words arrive as one.
- Model from the real audio, **[Segment 4]**: replay Ginguene's "Everything, everything is possible to
  do" and Stamberg's "And it's on this little, thin, flimsy piece of fabric." Play each twice and have
  students draw a link mark on the printed lines wherever two words run together.
- Students mark a third printed line the same way, **[Segment 2]**: "It's very precious hands." Then
  check all three against the real audio.
- Apply it: in pairs, students say one agreeing line and one disagreeing line from Phase 1 twice, once
  with every word separated and once linked, and tell each other which one sounded like a person
  rather than a list.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Rotating Partners (20 min)**

- Students pair up from where they sit and work the first prompt. When a pair finishes a prompt, each
  student finds a new partner and takes the next one, so each of the three descriptions is given to a
  different person. No formation and no class-wide rotation signal.
- **Three rotated prompts,** roughly six minutes each. In every one, one partner describes and the
  other responds with an agreeing or disagreeing frame and a reason, then they swap:
  1. Describe the workspace you chose in Practice It. Your partner says whether the picture you gave
     them matches a place they know.
  2. Describe a workroom, kitchen or shop you have seen where all the tools were left out on show.
     Your partner says what that arrangement tells them about the person who works there.
  3. Describe the tidiest workspace and the messiest workspace you have ever seen. Your partner says
     which of the two they would rather work in.
- **Active task for every non-speaker:** the listener is never idle, because the response is theirs to
  make. Each listener also writes down, in the packet, the frame their partner used and one word from
  the description that made them agree or disagree.
- **Differentiated participation:** Level 4 students pair up and rehearse their first description with
  one partner, out loud, before the round begins, so their first exchange is a line they have already
  said once.
- **Foundation Support:** for a student working below Level 4, the role is the frame-and-word record,
  read back aloud when a pair asks for it, plus one prepared sentence written with a partner during
  the rehearsal minute and delivered at a natural point in the first exchange.
- **Board-dependent moment:** the teacher writes each agreeing or disagreeing phrase on the board as it
  is actually spoken in the room, building one live list of what this class really said, which feeds
  Phase 5.
