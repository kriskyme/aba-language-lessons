## Governing CSV objective (Level 5, Listening/Speaking, Module 1: Describing)

> Listening: Can listen to an extended spoken description of a familiar or semi-familiar
> place or object, summarize how it's organized, and identify one clearly evaluative word
> choice that signals the speaker's opinion even though the speaker never states the opinion
> directly (e.g. a positive or negative adjective standing in for "I like/dislike this").
> Speaking: Can give an extended description, organizing details logically and including at
> least one clearly evaluative word choice that signals an opinion without stating it outright,

## Target vocabulary (grounded in the real transcript/page)

preserve / preservation (v/n) - to protect something so it stays in its original condition instead of being
lost or destroyed
trove (n) - a valuable collection of things (as in "a trove of architecture")
mid-century (adj) - from around the middle of a certain century (here, roughly 1945-1965)
register (n) - an official list, here of buildings recognized and protected for their history
covenant (n) - a formal condition written into a legal document, like a property deed
beholden (adj) - having a duty or obligation toward someone or something

## DAY 1: Unit A - Listening

```
|--Hook, Good to Know & Vocab (20)--|--Watch & Notes (15)--|--Skill Instr. (10)--|--Task (20)--|--Transfer Check (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

- **Visual Inquiry:** show a real, uncaptioned photo of a mid-century modern Phoenix home (a Ralph Haver
  "breeze-block" home, easily found via Modern Phoenix's own website) with no context. Ask: what decade do you
  think this was built? Do you think it's still standing today, or was it torn down?
- **Listening Skill Spotlight:** "Today we're practicing recognizing examples - noticing when a speaker gives
  one specific case to support a bigger, more general idea." (This is what today's Listening Closing Transfer
  Check will ask students to reproduce.)
- Present the citation block above.
- Pre-teach the 6 target words using the context sentences. Gloss both figurative chunks ("the land of
  opportunity," "living textbooks").
- **Background Note 1:** restrictive covenants were clauses once written into many U.S. property deeds,
  including in Arizona, that barred certain groups - often Black families - from buying homes in a
  neighborhood. They are no longer legally enforceable, but the old wording still turns up in historic deeds,
  which is why Shabazz calls them "a reminder of Arizona's relatively recent history."
- **Background Note 2:** "mid-century modern" describes an architecture style popular roughly from the 1940s
  through the 1960s. Phoenix has an unusually large collection of it partly because so many soldiers trained at
  Arizona military bases during World War II and returned afterward to build new lives there.
- Return to the photo: "This is a real home in Phoenix. Some people want to save buildings like this one;
  others think old buildings should make way for something new. What do you think decides whether a building
  gets saved or torn down?" Collect 3-4 guesses on the board.
- Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback: Level 4/5 listen for what makes Phoenix's mid-century
  buildings special and who wants to save them; Level 6/7 listen for how different speakers' own word choices
  reveal their position on preservation vs. development, not just the facts of the debate.
  **Phase 2: Watch & Listening Notes (15 min)**
- Watch the full segment once. While watching, students fill in a comparison T-chart: "The Old Post Office,
  Before" vs. "Postino Wine Bar, Now" - rows: what it was used for, what it looked like, why people value it
  now.
  **Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**
- Teach "recognize examples" directly, modeling on real content: King's general claim in **[Segment 4]** -
  "these buildings act as living textbooks to help us understand the progression of time" - followed by
  Sternberg's specific case in **[Segment 5]**, raising a family in and adapting a Haver home, as a concrete
  instance of that general idea.
- Guided practice: replay **[Segment 6]** (DeMarco on the old post office/Postino) and have students identify
  which earlier general claim DeMarco's specific story is an example of (Pela's "beholden to honor it," from
  **[Segment 2]**).
  **Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**
- Each student picks one task Level and completes it:
  - **Level 4:** In 2-3 sentences, name two reasons speakers give for valuing mid-century buildings, one
    comparison to what could happen instead (demolition or new development), and the stated reason preservation
    advocates give. Populate the T-chart from Phase 2 with two features per column, one comparison, one stated
    reason. **Respectful Tiers check:** also ask - "According to the segment, what does Adam Millsap say could
    happen to housing prices if preservation rules are too strict?" then "Do you think that's a fair concern, or
    is it overstated? Circle one and say why" - a genuine simplified interpretive step, not fact-retrieval alone.
  - **Level 5:** Summarize how the segment is organized (Phoenix's postwar architecture boom, then reasons to
    preserve, then the growth/cost counterpoint, then two homeowners'/community stories, then the closing
    philosophical statement) and identify one evaluative word or phrase (e.g. "living textbooks," "trove") that
    signals admiration without anyone saying "this is great." Note the organizational flow segment by segment on
    the T-chart.
  - **Level 6:** List two stated facts (over a million soldiers trained in Arizona in the 1940s; Haver homes
    are affordable and easy to customize) and two clearly-signaled evaluative comments (e.g. King's "architecture
    is shelter... a symbol of who we are as humans," Pela's "beholden to honor it"), keeping the two categories
    separate. Locate one clear tonal shift in the segment (from Millsap's practical, economic tone about growth
    and cost to Shabazz's more personal, historically-weighted tone about covenants) and describe what changes
    in the language at that shift.
  - **Level 7 (uses both sources):** Present a short excerpt from the Modern Phoenix archive page on White Gates
    (its "floating rectangle" form, the custom brise-soleil screen wall, and its description as "probably
    Beadle's most instagrammed architecture") alongside the PBS segment. Ask: how does this archive page's
    description of one home differ from how the PBS segment describes Phoenix's preservation movement as a
    whole? (The archive, written by the same organization Alison King is quoted representing, zooms into one
    building's design details and legacy; the PBS segment builds a wider human-interest story with several,
    sometimes disagreeing, voices.) Identify one unstated interest or assumption behind one framing - for
    example, the archive's close technical focus assumes a building's design significance alone justifies saving
    it, without directly weighing the economic tradeoffs Millsap raises in the broadcast piece. Note two or
    three specific descriptive choices the PBS segment makes (which speakers it features, what values it
    foregrounds) to ground the comparison.
- **Board-dependent moment:** students who worked on different Levels briefly compare and add their row(s) to
  the shared T-chart from Phase 2, so the full picture exists only once multiple Levels have contributed.
  **Phase 5: Listening Closing Transfer Check (10 min)**
- **Teacher-read-aloud script (invented, unrelated topic, recycling target vocabulary and applying the day's
  actual skill - recognizing examples):**
  > My grandfather's old fishing cabin sits on a lake two hours north of here. Small mid-century buildings like
  > his hold a lot of local history, and the town recently put it on the local historic register because of
  > that. Take my grandfather's cabin, for example: it's one of the last buildings left with the original
  > covenant still in its deed, and inside it's still a small trove of old fishing gear and photographs from
  > the 1950s. My uncle wasn't thrilled about the paperwork, but he says he feels beholden to preserve it
  > anyway, for my grandmother's sake.
  > (recycles mid-century, register, covenant, trove, beholden, preserve - all 6 target words - on a topic
  > unrelated to Phoenix architecture, and contains a clear general claim followed by a specific supporting
  > example.)
- Teacher reads this aloud once. Students do not read it themselves first - printed in the student packet only
  after the response space.
- Students state, out loud to a partner, the general claim the speaker makes ("small mid-century buildings hold
  a lot of local history") and the specific example she gives to support it (the cabin's covenant, its trove of
  old gear). Teacher cold-calls two or three pairs. After sharing, students may read the script to check.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Fishbowl (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- Play John Yang's real framing line that opens the segment: _"Some see older buildings as revered artifacts
  that have stood the test of time. Others see them as occupying sites that could be used for new
  development."_ - a real example of summarizing: two sentences that compress an entire community debate into
  its two core positions.
- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing summarizing what someone said - taking a person's point and
  restating it in your own words, shorter and clearer." This is the exact skill this day's Speaking Closing
  Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.
- Teach summarizing frames: "Basically, **_ is saying that _**." / "In other words, **_ thinks _**." / "To sum
  up what **_ said, _**."
  **Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**
- Each student picks one task Level:
  - **Level 4:** Complete: "Basically, **_ is saying _**." using one interviewee's real quote and a familiar
    idea. Then describe the same idea in your own original words, including one comparison and a stated reason,
    choosing your own connectors.
  - **Level 5:** Give an extended summary of one speaker's overall position, organizing details logically and
    including one evaluative word.
  - **Level 6:** Summarize two different speakers' positions, explicitly noting where they disagree, shifting
    register once between the two summaries (e.g. more measured for the "growth" side, warmer for the
    "preserve" side), explaining why one point matters.
  - **Level 7:** Summarize a speaker's position using idiomatic phrasing, then self-correct one word choice
    mid-sentence on purpose, modeling the kind of real-time repair fluent speakers do.
    **Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**
- Teach contrastive stress (Advanced-band feature) using a real line built from the source's own contrast:
  John Yang's "Some see older buildings as revered artifacts... others see them as occupying sites that could
  be used for new development." Model stressing "revered artifacts" and "new development" harder than the rest
  of the sentence, since the contrast between them is the whole point.
- Students mark contrastive stress in two more sentences built from the lesson's real content, then check their
  marking by listening again, then apply it in brief paired rehearsal of Phase 2's summarizing mini-exchanges,
  adding contrastive stress where a summary sets up a contrast between two speakers' positions.
  **Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Fishbowl (20 min)**
- **Differentiated participation:** Level 4 does a rehearsed pair-share of their stem before taking a turn in
  the fishbowl's inner circle.
- Rotating through 3 prompts: 1) "Should the city require owners to preserve buildings like White Gates, even if
  the owner wants to tear it down?" 2) "Millsap says preservation rules can raise housing prices. Is that a good
  enough reason to allow demolition?" 3) "Compare the PBS segment and the Modern Phoenix archive page. Which
  description makes you care more about saving a building like White Gates, and why?"
- **Board-dependent moment:** running board record of summarized opinions actually spoken during the fishbowl,
  feeding the Closing Transfer Check.
  **Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**
- Every student summarizes, in pairs and out loud, a partner's opinion on a new topic unrelated to the source
  (a class rule, a neighborhood change, a school policy), using Phase 1's summarizing frames. Teacher cold-calls
  two or three pairs.
- **Respectful Tiers check:** across the whole cycle, did Level 4 get a genuine interpretive step (Day 1) and a
  real production role (Day 2's rehearsed pair-share into the Fishbowl), not fact-retrieval and silent
  participation only?
- Brief reflection: "What's one old building in your own city or town that you think should be preserved, and
  why?" No Part 2/presentation-project extension is being used for this cycle (not yet built).
