## DAY 1: Unit A - Listening

```
|--Hook, Good to Know & Vocab (20)--|--Listen & Notes (15)--|--Skill Instr. (10)--|--Task (20)--|--Transfer Check (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

- **K-W-L Chart (K and W only):** students fill the "What I already know" and "What I want to know"
  columns of the chart printed in their own packet, on the topic of very large wild places, then compare
  with a neighbour. The "What I learned" column is completed in Day 2, Phase 5. Build the shared K and W
  lists live on the board. Prompt questions: "What do you already know about national parks or protected
  wild land?" / "What would you want to know before travelling to one?"
- Two photographs are printed with the chart: a stretch of the park's mountains, and an aerial view of
  braided river channels, so the two hardest target words have something visible attached to them
  before any listening starts.
  Image: `Lesson8_WrangellStElias_Img_HookMountains.jpg` - "Wrangell-St. Elias National Park and Preserve ENBLA03" by Enrico Blasutto, CC BY-SA 4.0, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wrangell-St._Elias_National_Park_and_Preserve_ENBLA03.jpg.
  Image: `Lesson8_WrangellStElias_Img_HookBraidedRiver.jpg` - "Braided Streams near the Katmai Coast (43417091514)" by Katmai National Park and Preserve, public domain, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Braided_Streams_near_the_Katmai_Coast_(43417091514).jpg.
- **Listening Skill Spotlight:** "Today we're practicing listening for contrast. A speaker often admits
  one thing and then turns it around: they say what you might expect, and then a word like but, however,
  although, or unlike tells you the real point is coming." (This is exactly what today's Listening
  Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.)
- Present the citation block above so students know what they are about to hear and who is speaking, and
  tell them the recording will be stopped partway through.
- **Good to Know:** "Flightseeing" is not a standard English word. It is a word people in Alaska made
  from "flight" and "sightseeing," for a short plane or helicopter trip taken only to look at scenery
  that has no roads.
- **Good to Know:** A hectare is a measure of land area, about the size of two football fields.
  Wrangell-St. Elias covers more than 5.3 million of them.
- Pre-teach the 6 target items above using the context sentences, matching braided and range and
  landscape to what is visible in the two photographs.

**Phase 2: Listen & Listening Notes (15 min)**

- Play the recording from the start, stopping at the line "From up above, these rivers can look like
  braided hair." Play that portion once, straight through.
- While listening, students fill a two-column T-chart printed in their packet, headed WHAT YOU MIGHT
  EXPECT and WHAT THE SPEAKER SAYS INSTEAD, with three rows: how famous the park is, how easy it is to
  reach, what the rivers do.
- Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback:
  - **Level 2 (fix the wrong word):** one line from the recording is printed with a single word changed.
    Listen for it, cross it out, and write the right word above it.
  - **Level 3:** "Listen for two words that tell you how big this park is and how far away it is."
  - **Level 4:** "Listen for two things the park is compared with, and for the reason the speaker gives
    for most people never having heard of it."
  - **Level 5:** "Listen for words the speaker chooses that show what she thinks of this place, without
    her ever saying what she thinks of it."

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

- Teach contrast and concession directly from two real, named moments.
  - From **[Segment 1: How big, and how unknown]**: "Do not worry if you have not heard of America's
    biggest national park. Most Americans do not know its name." then "But Wrangell-St. Elias contains
    some of North America's largest glaciers and volcanoes." Ask what the speaker gives away first and
    what the "But" is protecting. Establish the two-part move: admit the weak point, then turn.
  - From **[Segment 3: A world of water]**: "The Copper, the largest river of them all, flows into the
    Gulf of Alaska. Other rivers take a more dramatic path." Ask which words set the second sentence
    against the first. Establish that "Other" plus a comparison can carry a contrast with no "but" in
    sight.
- Guided practice on a second short excerpt: play "The mountain landscape is wild. Much of it is also
  difficult to reach." from **[Segment 2: The mountains]**. Students decide in pairs whether "also" is
  contrasting or adding, and say which word settles it.

**Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Each student picks one task Level. Item counts are calibrated for balanced time-on-task.

- **Level 2 (3 items).**
  1. **Choose what was heard.** Eight words are printed in a row: glaciers, volcanoes, deserts,
     mountains, rivers, cities, waterfalls, beaches. Circle the ones the recording says are in this park
     and cross out the ones it does not.
     **Answer note:** circle glaciers, volcanoes, mountains, rivers, waterfalls; cross out deserts,
     cities, beaches.
  2. **Better of two.** Two lines about the park are printed. Circle the one that fits what you heard,
     and say the word that makes it fit: "The speaker thinks this park deserves to be better known." /
     "The speaker thinks this park is not worth knowing about."
     **Answer note:** the first line; the word is but, or beauty. This is the interpretive step required
     by Quality Standards Section B: the speaker never says the park deserves attention, and the student
     has to hear the "But" turn as showing it.
  3. **The frame, produced once.** The teacher names one part of the park. Complete the frame in writing
     and then say it aloud, using one word from the recording: "It is \_\_\_."
     **Answer note:** any accurate word from the recording, for example vast, remote, or wild.

- **Level 3 (4 items).** A model card is printed once at the top of this Level's task: **It is \_\_\_
  and \_\_\_.**
  1. Circle the sentence that best describes this park: (a) It is small and crowded. (b) It is vast and
     remote. (c) It is flat and dry.
     **Answer note:** b.
  2. Complete the frame about how big the park is, using the word bank (smaller, bigger, the same /
     Switzerland, Alaska, Canada): "It is \_\_\_ than \_\_\_."
     **Answer note:** bigger; Switzerland.
  3. Complete the frame about the mountains, using the word bank (one, two, four / ranges, rivers,
     roads): "\_\_\_ mountain \_\_\_ come together here."
     **Answer note:** four; ranges.
  4. The recording says most Americans do not know this park's name. Write the word the speaker uses
     right after that to turn the description around, then circle one: she is agreeing that it does not
     matter / she is about to say why it does matter.
     **Answer note:** but; she is about to say why it does matter.

- **Level 4 (2 items).**
  1. In three or four sentences, name two features of this landscape that the speaker describes, make
     one comparison the speaker herself makes between this park and somewhere else, and give the reason
     she gives for so much of the park being hard to visit. Choose your own connectors.
     **Answer note:** features from four mountain ranges, glaciers, volcanoes, nine of the highest
     mountains, rivers, a 91-meter waterfall; the speaker's own comparisons are with Switzerland and with
     Yellowstone; the stated reason is that the mountain landscape is wild and much of it is difficult to
     reach, which is why companies fly people over it instead.
  2. The speaker describes two kinds of river in the park. Write two sentences that put them next to each
     other, and add a brief reason for the difference, in your own connectors.
     **Answer note:** the Copper simply flows into the Gulf of Alaska while other rivers take a more
     dramatic path, one of them dropping 91 meters as Chitistone Falls; the riverbeds twist and turn
     because sediment from meltwater keeps forcing new channels.

- **Level 5 (3 items).**
  1. In one paragraph, summarize how this part of the recording is organized: what the speaker opens
     with, what she moves to next, and where she leaves you.
     **Answer note:** she opens with size and with the fact that nobody knows the park, turns that around
     into what it holds, moves to the mountains and how hard they are to reach, and ends on the rivers
     and how they look from the air.
  2. Find one word or phrase where the speaker clearly admires this place but never says so. Write it
     down and explain what the word choice signals.
     **Answer note:** "witness Wrangell-St. Elias's beauty," "wild," "vast," "a more dramatic path," or
     "look like braided hair"; each carries admiration without any speaker stating an opinion.
  3. The speaker opens by admitting that most Americans have never heard of this park. In two or three
     sentences, say what admitting that does for the description that follows, and what would change if
     she had left it out.
     **Answer note:** admitting it removes the listener's likely objection before it is raised and makes
     the size claims land harder; leaving it out would make the piece read as a straight list of
     superlatives with nothing at stake.

- **Board-dependent moment:** students who worked at different Levels read one line each into the shared
  T-chart on the board. The WHAT THE SPEAKER SAYS INSTEAD column cannot be completed from any single
  Level's task, since Level 2 supplies the features and Levels 4 and 5 supply the turns.

- **Respectful Tiers check:** Level 2's item 2 is a genuine, simplified interpretive judgment about the
  speaker's attitude, not fact retrieval alone.

**Phase 5: Listening Closing Transfer Check (10 min)**

- **Teacher-read-aloud script (invented, unrelated topic, recycling the target vocabulary):**

  > There is a public garden behind the bus station that almost nobody uses. From the road it looks
  > small and dull. But walk through the gate and the landscape opens out: a low stone wall marks the
  > boundary, and behind it the ground rises into a little range of grassy humps. A stream runs through
  > the middle in braided channels. The far end feels genuinely remote, even though the traffic is
  > forty meters away. It is not vast. It is just quietly good.

  (91 words; recycles all six target items on a topic unrelated to Alaska, and carries three contrast
  moves, "But," "even though," and "It is not vast. It is just," for students to catch.)

- The teacher reads the script aloud once, at a natural but clear pace. **Students do not read it
  first.** It is printed in the packet only after the response space, upside down, under a "don't read
  ahead" instruction.
- Out loud to a partner, each student names what the speaker admits about the garden and what the turn
  is, and says which words made the turn. The teacher cold-calls two or three pairs. Demonstration, not
  a self-report vote.
  **Answer note:** the admission is that it looks small and dull from the road and that it is not vast;
  the turns are carried by "But," "even though," and "It is just quietly good."
- After sharing, students may turn the packet around and read the script to check.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Rotating Partners (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- The model is drawn from the source's own language. In **[Segment 1: How big, and how unknown]** the
  speaker concedes a point and then disagrees with it in the next breath: "Most Americans do not know its
  name. But Wrangell-St. Elias contains some of North America's largest glaciers and volcanoes." Play
  both sentences. That is exactly the shape a polite disagreement takes in conversation: grant the other
  person something first, then say what you actually think.
- **Deviation, flagged:** this source is a narrated report, so there is no live exchange in which one
  speaker agrees or disagrees with another. The model above is the source's real concede-then-turn move,
  which is the closest genuine instance the transcript offers; the agreeing half of the skill is taught
  from the frames below rather than from a quoted exchange. No invented dialogue is presented as real.
- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing agreeing and disagreeing, which usually means giving the other
  person something first and only then saying where you differ." (This is exactly what this day's
  Speaking Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.)
- Teach the frames: "I agree, and \_\_\_." / "That's true, but \_\_\_." / "I see what you mean, though
  \_\_\_." / "I'm not so sure, because \_\_\_." / "Exactly, and that's why \_\_\_."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Each student picks one task Level. Item counts are calibrated for balanced time-on-task.

- **Level 2 (1 item, three rounds).**
  1. **Say from sound.** A partner says one target word from Day 1 out loud. Repeat it, write it down,
     and check it together against the printed list. Do this three times, with a different word each
     time, then swap roles.
     **Answer note:** any three of vast, remote, range, boundary, landscape, braided.

- **Level 3 (3 items).** A model card is printed once at the top of this Level's task: **It is \_\_\_
  and \_\_\_.**
  1. Using the model, describe the park to a partner in one sentence with both slots filled.
     **Answer note:** for example, It is vast and remote.
  2. Using the model, describe a place near where you live to a partner in one sentence with both slots
     filled.
     **Answer note:** any two accurate describing words.
  3. Your partner says their sentence. Agree with one part of it and differ on another, using one of the
     Phase 1 frames.
     **Answer note:** the frame must be audible and both halves must actually be there.

- **Level 4 (2 items).**
  1. Describe a place you have travelled to in four or five sentences: two features of the landscape, one
     comparison with somewhere else, and a brief reason for that comparison, using your own connectors.
     Deliver it to a partner without notes in front of you.
     **Answer note:** the comparison and the reason must both be audible, for example greener than the
     coast because it rains most of the year.
  2. Your partner says they would not want to go there. Answer them with a Phase 1 frame that gives them
     something first, then say what you actually think.
     **Answer note:** the concession must come before the disagreement.

- **Level 5 (2 items).**
  1. Give an extended description of a place you think more people should visit, at least six sentences,
     organized so a listener can follow the order you chose. Somewhere in it, admit the obvious argument
     against the place and then turn it, the way the recording does. Include one clearly evaluative word
     that signals your opinion without your ever stating it.
     **Answer note:** the concede-then-turn move and the evaluative word should both be nameable
     afterwards.
  2. Your partner pushes back on your turn. Hold your position with one Phase 1 frame and give ground
     with another, then say which of the two moved the conversation further.
     **Answer note:** the student names the effect, not just the frames used.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

- **Feature: content-word stress.** English speakers push the important words forward and let the small
  grammar words fall away. The real model is in **[Segment 1: How big, and how unknown]**: "It is SIX
  TIMES the size of YELLOWSTONE." The numbers and the name carry the weight; "it is," "the," and "of"
  almost disappear.
- Students underline the words they expect to be stressed in each printed line, then check against the
  real audio. The teacher models live from the recording, since real emphasis can differ from a written
  prediction.
- Everyone then applies content-word stress in brief paired rehearsal of one of their own Phase 2
  sentences, saying it once flat and once with the content words pushed forward, and telling each other
  which was easier to follow.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Rotating Partners (20 min)**

- Students pair up from where they sit and take the first question; when a pair finishes, each student
  finds a new partner and takes the next one, so each of the three is discussed with a different person.
  Nobody moves furniture and nothing is posted around the room.
- **Rotated prompts:**
  1. "Should a place this hard to reach be kept hard to reach, or should there be roads?"
  2. "Describe a landscape you know well. What would somebody who has never been there not expect?"
  3. "Is a place worth protecting if almost nobody ever sees it?"
- **Every student uses at least one Phase 1 frame with each partner**, since these questions are made to
  be disagreed with.
- **Active task for every non-speaker:** across your partners, keep a running tally of each agreeing or
  disagreeing frame you hear, and write down one description a partner gives that you would like to
  borrow.
- **Differentiated participation:** Level 2 students rehearse their one prepared sentence with a partner
  before the rotation begins, and may read it from their packet.
- **A partner's object, the Day 2 production:** in that same rehearsal, each Level 2 student chooses one
  thing their partner has with them, a bag, a bottle, a pencil case, and says the frame about it once,
  out loud, with one describing word.
- **Board-dependent moment:** as pairs rotate, the teacher builds a live board record of the frames
  actually used and of the positions people took on prompt 1. That record is what Phase 5 draws on.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

- In pairs, out loud, one student states an opinion on a subject other than the park and other than
  their Phase 4 prompts, a film, a rule at school, a weekend plan, and the other answers with a Phase 1
  frame that gives ground first and then differs. Then they swap. The teacher cold-calls two or three
  pairs. Demonstration, not self-report.
- **Respectful Tiers check across the cycle:** Level 2 had a genuine interpretive task on Day 1 (item 2,
  judging the speaker's attitude) and a genuine production role on Day 2 (the partner's-object frame plus
  a rehearsed rotation sentence), not fact retrieval and silence.
- Return to the K-W-L chart from Day 1: as a class, fill in the "What I learned" column together,
  closing the loop on the Day 1 hook and on this Set as a whole. Brief reflection: "Across these four
  lessons you have described an animal, a workshop, an object, and a place. Which was hardest to
  describe, and why?"
