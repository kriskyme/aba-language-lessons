## DAY 1: Unit A - Listening

```
|--Hook, Good to Know & Vocab (20)--|--Listen & Notes (15)--|--Skill Instr. (10)--|--Task (20)--|--Transfer Check (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

- **Mystery Quote:** one line from the end of the recording is printed in the packet with no context:
  "For the first time in 3,000 years Ancient Egyptians spoke directly to us." Students write, in pairs,
  who they think said it and what could possibly have made it true. Collect three or four guesses on the
  board and leave them there; they are checked at the end of Phase 4.
- **Listening Skill Spotlight:** "Today we're practicing listening for cause and effect, which means
  catching the words that tell you one thing made another thing happen: because, so, helped, after, as a
  result." (This is exactly what today's Listening Closing Transfer Check will ask students to
  reproduce.)
- Present the citation block above so students know what they are about to hear and who is speaking.
- **Good to Know:** Hieroglyphs were the writing system of ancient Egypt for thousands of years. After
  Egyptians began using other kinds of writing, the knowledge of how to read hieroglyphs was lost
  completely for around 1,400 years.
- **Good to Know:** The British Museum is a very large public museum in London. Many of the objects in
  it were taken from other countries during the years when Britain ruled or fought in them, which is why
  several countries now ask for objects back.
- Pre-teach the 6 target items above using the context sentences. Hold back any photograph of the stone
  itself until Phase 4, so the Level 2 picture item is answered from the description in the audio rather
  than from something already seen.

**Phase 2: Listen & Listening Notes (15 min)**

- Play the full audio once, straight through.
- While listening, students fill a cause-and-effect chain printed in their packet: four rows, each with
  a WHAT HAPPENED box and a WHAT THAT MADE POSSIBLE box.
- **Organizer note (flagged):** Section 0.4 matches the cause-and-effect chain to Module 3 and offers a
  T-chart, Venn, or outline for Module 1. The chain is used here anyway, because Phase 2's notes have to
  feed the day's named listening strategy, and this piece's whole shape is a run of causes and effects.
  Lesson 6 used a T-chart, so no organizer repeats from the immediately preceding lesson either way.
- Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback:
  - **Level 2 (choose what was heard):** eight words are printed. Circle the ones the story actually
    says and cross out the ones it does not.
  - **Level 3:** "Listen for two words that tell you what the stone is and what is on it."
  - **Level 4:** "Listen for what people could not do before 1822 and what they could do after, and for
    the reason the museum gives for holding this exhibition."
  - **Level 5:** "Listen for words the speaker chooses that show what she thinks of this object, without
    her ever saying what she thinks of it."

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

- Teach cause-and-effect language directly from two real, named moments.
  - From **[Segment 4: What it made possible]**: "the writing on the stone was an important clue that
    helped experts learned to read Egyptian hieroglyphs." Ask which single word joins the cause to the
    effect. Establish "helped" as a cause-and-effect word that does not look like one.
  - From **[Segment 4: What it made possible]**, Regulski's quote: "We decided because the Rosetta Stone
    was such an important key to that decipherment that we will do this properly." Ask what "because"
    links to what. Establish that the cause can come before the effect in the sentence.
- Guided practice on a second short excerpt: play the line in **[Segment 2: How the stone travelled]**,
  "They gave it to British forces as part of a surrender agreement." Students decide in pairs whether
  "as part of" is giving a cause or a condition, and say which words settle it.

**Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Each student picks one task Level. Item counts are calibrated for balanced time-on-task.

- **Level 2 (3 items).**
  1. **Point or name, the Day 1 production.** Three photographs are printed side by side, labeled a, b,
     and c. The teacher says the frame sentence aloud: "It is covered in writing." Circle the photograph
     that matches, then say aloud what it is.
     Image: `Lesson7_RosettaStone_Img_ChoiceStone.jpg` - "Rosetta Stone" by Hans Hillewaert, CC BY-SA 4.0, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Rosetta_Stone.JPG.
     Image: `Lesson7_RosettaStone_Img_ChoiceVase.jpg` - "Attic red-figure vase, 450 BC, AM Fira, 176780" by Zde, CC BY-SA 4.0, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Attic_red-figure_vase,_450_BC,_AM_Fira,_176780.jpg.
     Image: `Lesson7_RosettaStone_Img_ChoiceStatue.jpg` - "Museum of Us - Ancient Egyptian granite lion statue 01" by Joe Mabel, public domain, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Museum_of_Us_-_Ancient_Egyptian_granite_lion_statue_01.jpg.
     **Answer note:** photograph a, the Rosetta Stone.
  2. **Fix the wrong word.** This printed line has one word changed from what the audio said. Cross out
     the wrong word and write the right one above it: "It contains the same writing in four languages."
     **Answer note:** four should be three.
  3. **Interpretive item (not a bank shape; see the flagged conflict above).** The woman from the museum
     calls this moment "a wonderful moment to celebrate." Circle one: she is pleased about the show / she
     is worried about the show. Then say one word from her sentence that told you.
     **Answer note:** she is pleased; wonderful or celebrate.

- **Level 3 (4 items).** A model card is printed once at the top of this Level's task: **It is \_\_\_
  and \_\_\_.**
  1. Circle the sentence that best describes the stone: (a) It is small and painted. (b) It is old and
     carved. (c) It is new and printed.
     **Answer note:** b.
  2. Complete the frame about what is written on the stone, using the word bank (one, two, three /
     languages, colors, pictures): "The same text is written in \_\_\_ \_\_\_."
     **Answer note:** three; languages.
  3. Complete the frame about where the stone is now, using the word bank (Egypt, London, France /
     museum, school, shop): "It is in a \_\_\_ in \_\_\_."
     **Answer note:** museum; London.
  4. Before 1822, nobody could read hieroglyphs. Write the word in the story that names what Young and
     Champollion did to change that, then circle one: they copied the writing / they worked out what it
     meant.
     **Answer note:** decoded (decipherment is also acceptable); they worked out what it meant.

- **Level 4 (2 items).**
  1. In three or four sentences, name two features of the stone that the speaker describes, make one
     comparison between what people could do with hieroglyphs before 1822 and after it, and give the
     reason the museum states for putting on this exhibition. Choose your own connectors.
     **Answer note:** features from about 2,400 years old, carved, the same text in three scripts,
     hieroglyphs plus Demotic plus ancient Greek; the comparison is that nobody could read hieroglyphs
     when the stone was found and experts could read them afterwards; the stated reason is that the
     stone was such an important key to the decipherment that the museum wanted to mark it properly.
  2. The story gives two different answers to the question of where the stone belongs. Write two
     sentences that put those answers next to each other, and add the brief reason each side gives, in
     your own connectors.
     **Answer note:** Egyptian archaeologists say it should be returned and call such objects stolen;
     the museum says Egypt has never made an official request and calls the stone a universal object
     that should simply be available to people.

- **Level 5 (3 items).**
  1. In one paragraph, summarize how this story is organized: what the speaker opens with, where she
     puts the history, and what she closes on.
     **Answer note:** it opens with the new exhibition and the pressure to return the stone, goes back
     to how the stone was found and moved, describes the writing and the decoding, returns to the
     ownership dispute, and closes on the museum director's line about Egyptians speaking to us.
  2. Find two words or phrases where the speaker or a speaker she quotes clearly shows what they think
     of this object, without anyone stating an opinion outright. Write them down and explain what each
     signals.
     **Answer note:** "the star of a new show," "star objects," "an important clue," "universal object,"
     or "treasures he called 'stolen'"; each carries a judgment that is never stated as one.
  3. The same object is called a "universal object" by one speaker and one of the "stolen" treasures by
     another. In two or three sentences, say what each choice of words is doing, and what it tells you
     about what each speaker wants.
     **Answer note:** "universal" argues that the object belongs to everyone and so to no single
     country, which supports keeping it where it is; "stolen" argues that it has a rightful owner, which
     supports returning it. Each word does the arguing without an argument being made.

- **Mystery Quote check:** return to the guesses on the board from Phase 1 and see which came closest.
  The line is the museum director's, and what made it true was the decoding of hieroglyphs.

- **Board-dependent moment:** students who worked at different Levels read one line each into the shared
  cause-and-effect chain on the board. The last row, what the decoding made possible, cannot be filled
  from Level 2's or Level 3's task alone.

- **Respectful Tiers check:** Level 2's item 3 is a genuine, simplified interpretive judgment about a
  speaker's attitude, not fact retrieval alone.

**Phase 5: Listening Closing Transfer Check (10 min)**

- **Teacher-read-aloud script (invented, unrelated topic, recycling the target vocabulary):**

  > Last winter a builder found a wooden box under the floor of our town library. Inside were forty
  > letters with strange carvings on the covers, and nobody could read a word of them. A retired teacher
  > spent three months on the decipherment, because she recognized an old shorthand her grandmother used.
  > Once she managed to decode the first page, the whole set made sense. They were written during the
  > reign of Queen Victoria. The library is putting them in a small exhibition this spring.

  (89 words; recycles all six target items on a topic unrelated to the Rosetta Stone, and carries two
  cause-and-effect signals, "because" and "Once," for students to catch.)

- The teacher reads the script aloud once, at a natural but clear pace. **Students do not read it
  first.** It is printed in the packet only after the response space, upside down, under a "don't read
  ahead" instruction.
- Out loud to a partner, each student names one thing that happened, one thing it made possible, and the
  word that joined them. The teacher cold-calls two or three pairs. Demonstration, not a self-report
  vote.
  **Answer note:** recognising the grandmother's shorthand made the decipherment possible, joined by
  "because"; decoding the first page made the whole set readable, joined by "Once."
- After sharing, students may turn the packet around and read the script to check.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Town Hall (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- The model is drawn from the real speakers. In **[Segment 4: What it made possible]** Regulski finishes
  one long thought and then adds a second, separate one: "'It's a wonderful moment to celebrate,' she
  added." Play both parts. Point out that she takes her full turn first and only then adds; nobody cuts
  across her.
- **Deviation, flagged:** this source is a narrated report, so it contains no live exchange where one
  speaker interrupts another. The teaching model above is real turn-taking behaviour from the source,
  and the interrupting half of the skill is taught from the frames below rather than from a quoted
  interruption, since no such interruption exists in the transcript to quote. No invented dialogue is
  presented as real.
- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing taking a turn politely, which means both knowing how to come
  into a conversation without cutting someone off, and knowing when to let the other person finish."
  (This is exactly what this day's Speaking Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.)
- Teach the frames: "Can I add something here?" / "Sorry to come in, but \_\_\_." / "Could I just
  finish this part?" / "Go ahead, you first." / "I'd like to hear the rest of that."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Each student picks one task Level. Item counts are calibrated for balanced time-on-task.

- **Level 2 (2 items).** The frame is printed once at the top of this Level's task: **It is \_\_\_.**
  1. **Better of two.** Two lines about the stone are printed. Circle the one that fits what you heard,
     then say aloud the word that makes it fit: "It is old." / "It is new."
     **Answer note:** the first line; the word is old.
  2. **A partner's object, the Day 2 production.** Choose one thing your partner has with them: a bag, a
     bottle, a pencil case. Say the frame about it once, out loud, with one describing word. Then let
     your partner finish their own sentence before you say anything else.
     **Answer note:** any accurate describing word; the waiting is the point of the item.

- **Level 3 (3 items).** A model card is printed once at the top of this Level's task: **It is \_\_\_
  and \_\_\_.**
  1. Using the model, describe the stone to a partner in one sentence with both slots filled.
     **Answer note:** for example, It is old and carved.
  2. Using the model, describe an object in this room to a partner in one sentence with both slots
     filled.
     **Answer note:** any two accurate describing words.
  3. Your partner starts a second sentence. Wait until they finish, then come in with one of the Phase 1
     frames and add one thing.
     **Answer note:** the frame must be audible and the waiting must actually happen.

- **Level 4 (2 items).**
  1. Describe an object you would put in a museum in four or five sentences: two features, one
     comparison with another object, and a brief reason why yours belongs there, using your own
     connectors. Deliver it to a partner without notes in front of you.
     **Answer note:** the comparison and the reason must both be audible.
  2. Give the description again with a partner deliberately trying to come in halfway. Once let them in
     with a Phase 1 frame, once hold your turn with one, and say afterwards which was harder.
     **Answer note:** both moves must be audible; either judgment is acceptable if reasoned.

- **Level 5 (2 items).**
  1. Give an extended description of an object that matters to a group of people, at least six
     sentences, organized so a listener can follow the order you chose. Include one clearly evaluative
     word that signals your opinion without your ever stating it.
     **Answer note:** the organizing order should be nameable afterwards; the evaluative word might be
     priceless, ordinary, stolen, or shared.
  2. Your partner comes in twice while you speak. Let one in and hold the other off, each with a
     different Phase 1 frame, then say which of your two choices changed what your listener took away.
     **Answer note:** the student names the effect of the choice, not just the frames used.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

- **Feature: linking.** When a word ending in a consonant is followed by a word starting with a vowel,
  fluent speakers run them together into one sound. The real model is in **[Segment 4: What it made
  possible]**: "an important clue that helped experts" comes out as "an_important clue that
  helped_experts."
- Students draw a link mark between the two words in each printed pair, then check against the real
  audio. The teacher models live from the recording.
- **Say from sound (Level 2's slot for this shape):** a partner says one target word from Day 1 at
  normal speed. The student repeats it, writes it, and checks it against the printed list together.
- Everyone then applies linking in brief paired rehearsal of one of their own Phase 2 sentences, saying
  it once with every word separated and once linked, and telling each other which sounded more natural.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Town Hall (20 min)**

- The class takes questions one at a time from where they sit, with the teacher calling on raised hands
  and keeping a speaking order. Nobody moves furniture and nothing is posted around the room.
- **Rotated prompts** (one per round):
  1. "Should the Rosetta Stone go back to Egypt? Say what you think and give one reason."
  2. "Describe one object from your own country or family that you think belongs in a museum, and say
     why."
  3. "The museum says an object like this belongs to everyone. Is there such a thing as an object that
     belongs to everyone?"
- **Every speaker uses at least one Phase 1 frame**, either to come in or to hold their turn, since
  people will want to answer each other on these questions.
- **Active task for every non-speaker:** keep a running tally of each turn-taking frame you hear, and
  write down one reason a classmate gives that you had not thought of.
- **Differentiated participation:** Level 2 students rehearse their one prepared sentence with a partner
  before the first round begins, and may read it from their packet when they are called on.
- **Board-dependent moment:** as students speak, the teacher builds a live board record of the reasons
  actually given, in two columns, keep it in London and return it to Egypt. That record is what Phase 5
  draws on.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

- In pairs, out loud, every student comes into a partner's sentence once with a Phase 1 frame and holds
  their own turn once, on a subject other than the stone and other than their Town Hall prompt: a film,
  a weekend plan, a rule at school. The teacher cold-calls two or three pairs. Demonstration, not
  self-report.
- **Respectful Tiers check across the cycle:** Level 2 had a genuine interpretive task on Day 1 (item 3,
  judging a speaker's attitude) and a genuine production role on Day 2 (the partner's-object frame plus a
  rehearsed Town Hall sentence), not fact retrieval and silence.
- Brief whole-class reflection: "Is it easier to wait for your turn or to take one? Which did you have
  to work at today?"
