Unit 6A: Three Hundred Years of Weaving

Listening & Speaking · Describing Intermediate 1.2.6.0

Objective: I can catch the small words a speaker uses to show me where I am in a description and what is coming next.

Good to Know

A tapestry is a picture made by weaving colored thread, not by painting. It is made to hang on a wall, and a large one can take months to finish.

In weaving, a "cartoon" is not a drawing for children. It is the full-size drawing on paper that a weaver copies from while working.

Words to Know

1. palacethe official home of a king, queen, or president
2. dyea liquid used to change the color of cloth, usually for good
3. recycleto make something new out of something that has already been used
4. warpthe threads that run up and down on a loom, the ones the other threads cross over
5. weaveto make cloth by crossing threads over and under each other
6. bobbins and spinning wheelsthe small wooden holders that carry thread, and the wheels that twist wool into thread

Our school's costume room is in the oldest part of the building, which was a small palace a hundred years ago. First, the students there cut up old curtains instead of buying new cloth, so almost everything is recycled. Then they dye the pieces in big pots in the sink. After that, they weave strips together on a wooden frame, pulling the colors across the warp threads. Bobbins and spinning wheels sit on a shelf, still working.

Learn the Phrase
Giving Back the Short Version

Twice in this story the speaker takes what the factory's director told her and gives it back in her own shorter words instead of quoting him: "The order is worth more than 1 million dollars and it will provide work for up to five years, Klecker de Elizalde said." Compare that with his own long answer about materials, and you can hear how much a short version leaves out.

Now it's your turn. In the tasks below, you'll practice giving back the short version using lines like these:

  • • "So you're saying ___."
  • • "In other words, ___."
  • • "If I understood you, ___."
  • • "Basically, ___."
  • • "Let me check I've got it: ___."

Say It Like a Native Speaker

When a speaker reads a list, each item is its own short group of sound with a small pause after it, instead of the whole list running together. Mark the pause points with a slash on the line below, then listen again and check.

"Here the only products we work with are silk, wool, jute, cotton, linen."

Then say one of your own sentences twice, once as one rushed string and once broken into groups, and ask your partner which was easier to follow.
