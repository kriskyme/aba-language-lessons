## Governing CSV objective (Level 7, Listening/Speaking, Module 1: Describing)

## Target vocabulary (grounded in the real source's subject matter)

displacement (n) - the forcing of people to leave their home or community, often because of money or development
pressure
redevelopment (n) - the process of rebuilding or reorganizing an area, often after damage or decline
investment (n) - money put into something, such as a neighborhood or project, with the goal of improving or
growing it
gentrification (n) - a process in which a lower-income neighborhood changes as wealthier residents move in and
prices rise
newcomer (n) - a person who has recently arrived or moved to a place
incentive (n) - something, often money or a tax benefit, offered to encourage a particular action

**Idiom to gloss (Proficient band, opaque, explicit gloss):**

- **"mixed blessing"** (from the source's own title) - something that has both good and bad results or effects
  at the same time, so it's hard to call it simply good or bad

**Background Note 1:** Hurricane Katrina struck the U.S. Gulf Coast in August 2005; the levee failures that
followed flooded roughly 80 percent of New Orleans, and the Lower Ninth Ward, a historically Black,
working-class neighborhood, was among the hardest-hit and slowest-to-recover areas in the city.

**Background Note 2:** the Make It Right Foundation was a nonprofit founded in 2007 by actor Brad Pitt to build
affordable, storm-resistant homes for displaced Lower Ninth Ward families; it is the organization behind the
"Make It Right" homes named in the primary source.

## DAY 1: Unit A - Listening

```
|--Hook, Good to Know & Vocab (20)--|--Watch & Notes (15)--|--Skill Instr. (10)--|--Task (20)--|--Transfer Check (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (20 min)**

- **Four-Corner Debate:** post four signs around the room - "Strongly Agree," "Agree," "Disagree," "Strongly
  Disagree" - against the statement: "A neighborhood's most important people are the ones who have lived there
  the longest." Students move to the corner matching their view, briefly discuss in their corner, then two or
  three volunteers from different corners explain their reasoning to the whole class.
- **Listening Skill Spotlight:** "Today we're practicing listening for stated versus implied opinion, noticing
  when a speaker's word choice signals how they feel about something even though they never say it outright."
  (This is the exact skill today's Listening Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.)
- Present the citation block above.
- Pre-teach the 6 target words using the context sentences. Gloss the idiom "mixed blessing."
- **Background Note 1** (Hurricane Katrina and the Lower Ninth Ward) and **Background Note 2** (the Make It
  Right Foundation), both brief and informational only.
- Return to the Four-Corner statement: "Today's segment follows one real New Orleans neighborhood ten years
  after a disaster, through the voices of people with very different stakes in what happens next. Listen for
  how each speaker really feels, not just what facts they state."
- Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback: Levels 5/6 listen for what has changed in the
  neighborhood and how different speakers seem to feel about it; Levels 7/8 listen for what each speaker's
  framing leaves out or downplays.

**Phase 2: Watch & Listening Notes (15 min)**

- Watch the full segment once. While watching, students fill in a two-column organizer: "Who's Speaking (Their
  Stake in the Neighborhood)" vs. "What They Say / How They Seem to Feel" - rows for a longtime resident, a
  newcomer, and an official.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

- Teach "listen for stated vs. implied opinion" directly, modeling on real content from **[Segment 1]**:
  Rev. Willie Calhoun's line, "There's no economic development. There's no way to sustain this community
  whatsoever if you don't generate business here." Point out that he never says "I am frustrated" or "this
  upsets me" - the repeated "no" and the word "whatsoever" are the evaluative signal that carries his opinion.
- Guided practice: replay **[Segment 4]** and have students identify the evaluative signal in Keisha Henry's
  line, "It's offensive when people say 'I'm here to save this neighborhood.' My family's been here 90 years,"
  noting that "offensive" is a clearly stated evaluative word (not implied), a useful contrast case for today's
  distinction before Level 6's task asks students to separate the two categories directly.

**Phase 4: Differentiated Listening Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

- Each student picks one task Level and completes it:
  - **Level 5:** Summarize how the segment is organized (opening on the neighborhood's post-Katrina rebuilding
    and its numbers, then longtime residents' concerns, then newer residents' perspective, then an official and
    an academic voice) and identify one clearly evaluative word choice that signals a speaker's opinion without
    them stating it directly - for example, Rev. Calhoun's repeated "no... whatsoever." Note the organizational
    flow on the two-column organizer. **Respectful Tiers check:** also ask - "Based on his words, does Rev.
    Calhoun sound hopeful or discouraged about the neighborhood's recovery? Say which, and quote the exact
    words that show it." - a genuine simplified interpretive step, not fact-retrieval alone.
  - **Level 6:** List two stated facts (over $500 million in public and private investment has reached the
    neighborhood; the population is roughly half of what it was before Katrina) and two clearly-signaled
    evaluative comments (Kim Ford: "Developers market these to people not from this community, primarily white
    people"; Keisha Henry: "It's offensive when people say 'I'm here to save this neighborhood'"), keeping the
    two categories separate. Locate one clear tonal shift in the segment (from the hopeful, statistics-driven
    opening about investment and rebuilding to the more skeptical, personal tone of the longtime residents'
    interviews) and describe what changes in the language at that shift.
  - **Level 7 (uses both sources):** Read the Make It Right Foundation's own stated mission (a paraphrased
    excerpt: it promised "150 safe, energy-efficient and affordable homes" for displaced families, each built to
    "Cradle to Cradle" and LEED Platinum standards) alongside the PBS segment's own account of the same
    neighborhood. Ask: how does the foundation's own framing differ in tone and focus from how the news segment
    frames the same recovery? Identify one unstated interest or assumption behind one framing - for example, the
    foundation's promotional description assumes that architectural and design innovation are themselves enough
    to secure residents' long-term stability, an assumption Rev. Calhoun's point about the lack of ongoing
    economic development does not share. Note two or three specific descriptive choices each source makes (what
    each chooses to lead with or emphasize) to ground the comparison.
  - **Level 8:** Listen again to Mayor Mitch Landrieu's line from **[Segment 5]**: "If a neighborhood that got
    hit was wealthier with insurance, it would be doing better." Reconstruct both the surface content (a
    factual-sounding claim that a neighborhood's recovery speed depends on residents' insurance coverage and
    wealth) and what the explanation downplays or leaves unnamed (compare it against Kim Ford's more direct
    line about who developers market the rebuilt homes to). Articulate how the specific words "insurance" and
    "wealthier" keep the explanation in economic, technical terms rather than naming the more sensitive,
    longer-standing dynamic Kim Ford's own words point to more directly.
- **Board-dependent moment:** students who worked on different Levels briefly compare and add their row(s) to
  the shared two-column organizer from Phase 2, so the full who's-speaking-and-how-they-feel picture exists only
  once multiple Levels have contributed.

**Phase 5: Listening Closing Transfer Check (10 min)**

- **Teacher-read-aloud script (invented, unrelated topic, recycling target vocabulary and applying the day's
  actual skill - stated vs. implied opinion):**
  > A small fishing village on the coast is changing quickly. City leaders have offered tax incentives to a
  > hotel company, hoping new investment will fund the redevelopment of the old harbor. Several newcomers have
  > already bought vacation homes near the water. A longtime fisherman just shrugs and says the harbor "doesn't
  > feel like home anymore, but I guess that's progress." Some worry this kind of gentrification could lead,
  > eventually, to the displacement of the families who have fished here for generations.
  > (recycles displacement, redevelopment, investment, gentrification, newcomer, incentive - all 6 target words
  > - on a topic unrelated to the Lower Ninth Ward, and contains a clear implied-opinion moment: the fisherman's
  > "I guess that's progress" signals resignation and quiet disapproval without him ever saying "I am unhappy
  > about this.")
- Teacher reads this aloud once. Students do not read it themselves first - printed in the student packet only
  after the response space.
- Students state, out loud to a partner, what opinion the fisherman implies about the harbor's changes and
  exactly which words signal it (not what he says on the surface, but what his word choice reveals). Teacher
  cold-calls two or three pairs. After sharing, students may read the script themselves to check.

## DAY 2: Unit B - Speaking

```
|--Skill Spotlight (15)--|--Task (20)--|--Pronunciation (10)--|--Town Hall (20)--|--Transfer Check & Wrap (10)--|
```

**Phase 1: Speaking Skill Spotlight (15 min)**

- Name the skill: "Today we're practicing making comparisons, using clear language to describe how two things,
  time periods, or perspectives are alike or different." This is the exact skill today's Speaking Closing
  Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.
- **Deviation flagged openly (per Section 0's rule for sources without directly usable dialogue):** no speaker
  in the primary source could be confirmed, from the material available during sourcing, to use an explicit
  comparison sentence on-screen. Rather than quote invented dialogue as if it were real, this spotlight is
  grounded in the source's own real, verified situational contrast instead: the segment's own before/after
  numbers (the neighborhood's population is roughly half of pre-Katrina levels, against $500+ million in new
  investment) and the real tension between Keisha Henry's ninety-years-here claim and the "newcomer" framing
  used elsewhere in the segment.
- Teach comparison frames: "Unlike ___, ___ actually ___." / "Compared to how it was, ___ is now more ___,
  but ___." / "On the surface, ___ looks like ___, but underneath, ___."

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

- Each student picks one task Level:
  - **Level 5:** Give an extended description contrasting the neighborhood right after Katrina with the
    neighborhood today, organizing your details logically and including one evaluative word choice that signals
    an opinion without stating it outright.
  - **Level 6:** Describe the tension between longtime residents and newcomers in a fluent, integrated way,
    explicitly explaining why one specific detail (Keisha Henry's "ninety years" claim, or Rev. Calhoun's point
    about the lack of ongoing economic development) matters, and adjusting your register once in response to a
    partner's reaction.
  - **Level 7:** Deliver a well-structured comparison of how the two real sources (the news segment and the
    Make It Right Foundation's own description) each select and emphasize different details to shape a
    different impression of the same recovery, self-monitoring and repairing one imprecise word choice
    mid-delivery, using idiomatic vocabulary.
  - **Level 8:** Deliver an unrehearsed, extended comparison under real-time pressure: describe the
    neighborhood's recovery first in official, statistics-driven terms, then, when your partner pushes back
    with a follow-up question, shift to a more personal or critical register that names something the
    statistics leave out - self-correcting at least once when a word choice proves inaccurate or misleading.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

- Teach contrastive stress (putting extra vocal emphasis on the specific word being contrasted) using Kim Ford's
  real line: "Developers market these to people NOT FROM this community, primarily WHITE people." Model how the
  stressed words carry the actual contrast (who the homes are marketed to, versus who already lives there),
  while the surrounding words are said more quickly and lightly.
- Students mark the stressed words in two more comparison sentences built from the lesson's real content, then
  check their marking by listening again, then apply it in brief paired rehearsal of their Phase 2 comparisons,
  exaggerating the stress on the contrasted words rather than speaking in a flat, even rhythm.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Town Hall (20 min)**

- **Differentiated participation:** Level 5 prepares and rehearses one opening stakeholder statement with a
  partner before the Town Hall begins (a "prepared stakeholder statement"), rather than fully spontaneous
  speech from the first turn. **Foundation Support (below Level 5):** a student below the lowest task Level's
  floor holds a name card for one real stakeholder (Cotlon, Calhoun, Ford, Magor and Aaroe, Henry, Landrieu, or
  Campanella) and points to it when that person's viewpoint should be represented, contributing one rehearsed
  short line when called on.
- Set up a Town Hall: students take on roles drawn directly from the segment's real cast of stakeholders (a
  longtime resident, a newer resident, a community organizer, a city official). Prompt: "What should come next
  for a neighborhood recovering from disaster?" Each role speaks in turn, using Phase 1's comparison frames and
  today's contrastive-stress pattern to compare the neighborhood's past, present, and possible future.
- **Board-dependent moment:** a running board record of each stakeholder's actual stated position as the Town
  Hall unfolds, feeding the Closing Transfer Check.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

- Every student, in pairs and out loud, produces a new comparison on a topic unrelated to the source: comparing
  a place they used to know well (a childhood neighborhood, a favorite shop, a hometown) to how it is today, or
  how they imagine it might change in the future, using Phase 1's frames and today's contrastive-stress pattern.
  Teacher cold-calls two or three pairs.
- **Respectful Tiers check:** across the whole cycle, did Level 5 get a genuine interpretive step (Day 1) and a
  real production role (Day 2's rehearsed stakeholder statement feeding into the Town Hall), not fact-retrieval
  and silent participation only?
- Brief reflection: "Has a place you know well changed in a way that felt like a 'mixed blessing' - good and bad
  at the same time? What made it feel that way?" No Part 2/presentation-project extension is being used for this
  cycle (not yet built).
