## Target vocabulary (all six occur in the real transcript)

plaque (n) - a flat piece of metal, stone, or wood, fixed to a wall, usually carrying a picture or
lettering (Segment 1)
relief (n) - a carving or casting in which the figures stand out from a flat background, so the surface is
raised rather than flat (Segment 1: "figures in high relief")
commodities (n, pl) - goods that are bought, sold, and shipped in quantity, such as metal, cloth, or
shells (Segment 3)
prohibition (n) - an official rule forbidding something completely (Segment 4: "a total prohibition on the
export of the finished brass plaques")
punitive (adj) - intended as a punishment (Segment 5: "a punitive expedition")
artefacts (n, pl; also spelled artifacts) - objects made by people, especially ones kept and studied for
what they show about a culture (Segment 6). The British spelling is the one used in this episode; the
American spelling appears in the second source.

Context sentences (paraphrased from what the source says, not quoted verbatim):

1. Each brass plaque is roughly the size of a large sheet of paper and was nailed to the wall of the oba's
   palace.
2. The figures on the plaque are cast in high relief, so the oba stands out further from the background
   than anyone else.
3. Portuguese ships brought commodities from as far away as the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean to the
   oba's court.
4. A prohibition on exporting the finished plaques meant that none was seen in Europe before 1897.
5. After a British delegation was attacked, the British organized a punitive expedition against Benin City.
6. Wole Soyinka argues that these artefacts remain politically loaded today.

**Idioms to gloss (Proficient band, opaque, explicit gloss):**

- **"cut out the middle-men"** (Segment 3: "The Portuguese galleons cut out all the middle-men") - to
  remove the people who used to stand between a buyer and a seller, so the two now deal directly
- **"not least"** (Segment 3: "able to control all aspects of life and, not least, foreign trade") - a way
  of adding a final item to a list while marking it as the most important one, not the least

**Background Note 1:** the Kingdom of Benin was a West African state whose capital, Benin City, is in what
is now southern Nigeria. Its ruler is called the oba. **It is not the modern Republic of Benin**, which is
a separate country to the west of Nigeria and takes its name from the same bay.

**Background Note 2:** Wole Soyinka is a Nigerian poet and playwright. In 1986 he became the first African
writer to be awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature.

**Background Note 3:** "A History of the World in 100 Objects" was made by BBC Radio 4 together with the
British Museum, and every object in the series comes from that museum's collection. Neil MacGregor, who
wrote and presented it, was the director of the British Museum at the time. The British Museum holds Benin
plaques that were taken from Benin City in 1897.

**Phase 1: Hook, Good to Know & Vocabulary (15 min)**

- **K-W-L Chart:** the packet prints a three-column chart with the first two columns open. Students fill in,
  individually and in writing, what they already know about the Kingdom of Benin and about objects held in
  museums far from where they were made, and what they want to find out. They compare with a neighbor, and
  the teacher builds a shared version live on the board from what students report. The third column is
  completed at the end of the cycle. Nothing is posted on a wall and nobody moves.
- **Listening Skill Spotlight:** "Today we're practicing listening for signposts, the phrases a speaker uses
  to tell you what is coming next and how the parts of a talk fit together." This is the exact skill Phase 6's
  Closing Transfer Check will ask students to reproduce.
- Present the citation block above, and Background Notes 1, 2, and 3, all brief and informational only.
- Pre-teach the 6 target words using the context sentences. Gloss both idioms, "cut out the middle-men" and
  "not least."
- Frame the listen: "This is the director of a museum describing one object in his own museum. He is telling
  you where he is going next almost every time he changes subject. Listen for those phrases."
- Differentiated listening purpose, set before playback. One star: what the plaque looks like and which
  words tell you what he thinks of it. Two stars: which of his statements are facts and which are
  judgements. Three stars: which details he selects, and what a description built out of those details
  leaves out. Four stars: the places where his sentences go quiet about who did what.

**Phase 2: First Listen & Listening Notes (15 min)**

- Play the episode straight through, once. **Audio only:** captions off, no transcript on the page, the
  board, or the screen, and none of Phase 5's windows visible yet. This is the students' one uncued first
  contact with the episode and it cannot be given back.
- The episode runs about 14 minutes, which fills nearly the whole phase, so the organizer is filled in
  **while** students listen rather than afterwards. Flagged in the open items below.
- The organizer is a two-column table: "Signpost phrase" against "What he describes after it." It tracks
  the description itself while training the day's strategy on the same page.

**Phase 3: Listening Skill Instruction (10 min)**

- Teach signposting directly, modeling on **[Segment 1]**: "Throughout this week we are with objects that
  describe how Europe first encountered, and then traded with, the wider world in the sixteenth century.
  But the magnificent sculptures of this programme record the encounter from the other side - from the
  African side." Point out that the first sentence places the episode inside a bigger series and the second
  turns against it with "But," so before he has described anything at all, a listener already knows the
  subject and the angle.
- Show the three jobs signposts do, each with a real phrase from the episode:
  - **Pointing forward:** "In the next programme, I'll be talking about the Spanish conquest of Aztec
    Mexico."
  - **Pointing back at the object:** "When we look again at the plaque, we can see that one of the
    Europeans is indeed holding a manilla, and this is the key to the whole scene."
  - **Marking a summary:** "In short, Europeans visiting Benin, in the fifteenth and sixteenth century,
    discovered a society every bit as organised and structured as the great courts of Europe."
- Guided practice: replay the two sentences in **[Segment 6]**, "Why this strange amnesia? I think it's
  probably because the later relationship was so dominated by the transatlantic slave trade." Students name
  what that question does to the shape of the talk and what they expect to hear immediately after it.

**Phase 4: Second Listen & Differentiated Task, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Each student picks one task Level. The phase runs in three beats inside its own 20 minutes.

**Beat 1, Commit (about 10 min).** Students answer their Level's task in writing, from the first listen and
their Phase 2 organizer, before anything is replayed. Nothing of the episode's wording is on the page.

**Beat 2, Second listen (about 5 min).** Replay **Segments 4 to 6**, from "When we look again at the plaque"
to the end, still audio only. Fourteen minutes does not fit a five-minute beat, so the replay is the defined
stretch every Level's task turns on rather than the whole episode. Each Level listens under its own narrower
purpose, stated here and different from the purpose it was given in Phase 2:

- **One star:** count how many separate figures he names on the plaque.
- **Two stars:** listen for the two words he uses just before he sums up the Dutch visitor's description,
  and write what comes immediately after them.
- **Three stars:** listen for every time he names who bought or took the objects, and write the exact words
  he uses each time.
- **Four stars:** listen to the verbs in the two sentences about 1897 and note which of them have a person
  doing them and which do not.

**Beat 3, Revise (about 5 min).** Students change whatever they now hear differently and mark which of their
answers moved. What moved is what Phase 5 checks.

- **Level 5 (one star):**
  1. Using your notes, number these four things in the order he describes them: the 1897 raid, the figures
     on the plaque, the Portuguese ships, the size of the plaque.
  2. In two or three sentences, summarize how the episode is organized, from where it starts to where it
     ends.
  3. Find one word he uses about the plaques or about the kingdom that tells you what he thinks of them,
     even though he never says "I think" or "in my opinion." Write the word and what it describes.
  4. Near the end he calls the plaques "these beautiful and disturbing objects." From his words alone, what
     makes them disturbing to him? Say what, and quote the exact words that show it.

  **Answer note:** for 2, the episode opens on Africans in modern Britain, moves to what the plaques are
  and a close description of one of them, then to the Portuguese ships and the trade that supplied the
  brass, then to who controlled that trade, then to the 1897 raid and the plaques reaching Europe, and
  closes on what Europe forgot and what Nigerians still feel. For 3, available words include "magnificent,"
  "staggering," "feeble and ridiculous" (of the Europeans on the plaque), "wild" (of the theories), and
  "bewildering." For 4, the wound: "it left a wound in the consciousness of many Nigerians - a wound that's
  still felt keenly today," or Soyinka's "The looted objects are still today politically loaded."

- **Level 6 (two stars):**
  1. Write two things he states as facts about the plaques or the kingdom.
  2. Write two comments where he judges something rather than states it, and next to each, write the words
     that signal it is a judgement.
  3. He quotes an early Dutch visitor at length, and then gives his own one-sentence summary of what that
     description shows. Write his summary sentence, and write the two words he uses to signal that a
     summary is coming.

  **Answer note:** facts available include that the plaques are brass rather than bronze, that they are
  about the size of an A3 sheet of paper, that one German merchant-house agreed in 1548 to provide Portugal
  with 432 tons of brass manillas, and that none had been seen in Europe before 1897. Judgements with their
  signals include "the quantities involved are staggering" (the evaluative "staggering" attached to a plain
  number), "in fact they look both feeble and ridiculous" (the intensifier "in fact" plus two evaluative
  adjectives), and "It is a bewildering fact that..." For 3, the summary is "In short, Europeans visiting
  Benin, in the fifteenth and sixteenth century, discovered a society every bit as organised and structured
  as the great courts of Europe, with an administration able to control all aspects of life and, not least,
  foreign trade," and the signal is "In short."

- **Level 7 (three stars, uses both sources):** below are both sources' own words, paired by subject, so
  the comparison can be made from the page. Excerpts are verbatim, with ellipses at cuts.

  - *What the objects are.*
    - **BBC episode (Neil MacGregor):** "They're each about the size of an A3 sheet of paper, and they show
      figures in high relief that celebrate the battles won by the army of the Benin ruler, the oba, and
      the rituals of the oba's court."
    - **BBC episode (Neil MacGregor):** "The plaque is about two feet square (60 cm). Its colour strikes
      you as coppery rather than brassy, and there are five figures on it - three Africans and two
      Europeans."
    - **NPR (2022):** "Most of the items themselves aren't actually made of bronze; but the thousands of
      items spread across the globe include carved elephant tusks, ivory leopard statues, wooden heads, and
      hundreds of brass plaques that belonged to Benin's royal palace, according to the New York Times."

  - *How they left Benin City in 1897.*
    - **BBC episode (Neil MacGregor):** "the British, in ostensible revenge for the killing, organised a
      punitive expedition which raided Benin City, exiled the oba and created the British protectorate of
      Southern Nigeria."
    - **BBC episode (Neil MacGregor):** "Many of these objects were then auctioned off to cover the costs
      of the expedition, and they were bought by museums across the world."
    - **BBC episode (Neil MacGregor):** "The arrival and reception of these completely unknown sculptures
      caused a sensation in Europe."
    - **NPR (2022):** "Britain's 1897 raid on the Kingdom of Benin has been called a 'punitive expedition'
      and a retaliation for the killing of British officer James Phillips."
    - **NPR (2022):** "Soldiers effectively ended the kingdom during their raid, burning the palace,
      killing an unknown number of people, and confiscating the palace's artworks and artifacts."

  - *What should happen to them now.*
    - **BBC episode (Neil MacGregor):** "That raid, and the removal of some of Benin's great art works, may
      have spread knowledge of Benin's culture to the world, but it left a wound in the consciousness of
      many Nigerians - a wound that's still felt keenly today..."
    - **BBC episode (Wole Soyinka):** "The looted objects are still today politically loaded."
    - **NPR (2022):** "The Smithsonian Institution has made an agreement to return its full collection of
      Benin Kingdom Court Style artifacts to Nigeria, their original location before the 1897 British raid
      on Benin City."

  1. Both sources describe the same objects and the same raid. Write what impression of the raid a listener
     or reader is left with by each one, using the printed words as your evidence.
  2. Identify one interest or assumption behind one source's framing that neither source states outright.

  **Answer note:** for 1, the two accounts choose different words for the same act. The episode has the
  British organize an expedition that "raided" the city, and then the objects "arrive" in Europe and are
  "removed" from Benin; the article has soldiers "burning the palace, killing an unknown number of people,
  and confiscating the palace's artworks," and calls the same objects "stolen" and "looted" elsewhere in the
  piece. The episode ends on a wound that is felt; the article ends on an institution handing the objects
  back and on a Nigerian official asking who controls the display. For 2, the assumption under the episode's
  framing is that the value of what followed the raid, the world's knowledge of Benin's art, can be weighed
  against the raid itself, which is what the sentence beginning "That raid, and the removal of some of
  Benin's great art works, may have spread knowledge" actually does. A student may instead argue the
  interest: the speaker directs the museum that holds one of these plaques, so an account in which the
  objects were auctioned and "bought by museums across the world" places his own institution among many
  buyers rather than as a party to the raid. Accept any reading argued from the printed words.

- **Level 8 (four stars):** listen again to **[Segment 5]**, from "On 13 January 1897" to "caused a
  sensation in Europe," and to the sentence in **[Segment 6]** that begins "That raid, and the removal of
  some of Benin's great art works."
  1. Write the surface content of Segment 5 in one plain sentence: what happened, in his account?
  2. He says the details "are still far from clear, and have been vigorously disputed, but whatever the
     real facts." Write what that wording lets him avoid deciding.
  3. Write what the sentence beginning "The arrival and reception" does not say about how the sculptures
     got to Europe.
  4. The speaker directs the museum that holds one of these plaques. Write what he never says about that,
     anywhere in the episode.

  **Answer note:** the surface content is that a British delegation was attacked at Benin City, that the
  British responded with a punitive expedition which raided the city and exiled the oba, and that the
  objects taken were auctioned to pay for the expedition and bought by museums. For 2, the hedge suspends
  the question of who was at fault for the attack that the expedition was said to answer, and "in ostensible
  revenge" does the same work in a single adjective, marking the stated motive as the stated one without
  saying whether it was the real one. For 3, the sentence has the sculptures arriving, with no one bringing
  them: "arrival" is an event the objects perform by themselves, in a sentence that follows one about booty
  being auctioned, so the people who carried them off drop out of the grammar. The same move runs through
  "the removal of some of Benin's great art works," where a noun replaces a verb, nobody removes anything,
  and "some of" makes the quantity smaller than the hundreds he has just described. For 4, he never says
  that his own museum is one of the museums that bought them, or that the plaque he has spent the episode
  describing is in his own building; the curator he quotes, Charles Hercules Read, is introduced as "the
  British Museum curator" without any mention that Read was acquiring the objects for the museum the
  speaker now runs.

- The episode is built out of this move, and the language below is quoted from the presenter's own lines,
  so nothing in this spotlight is invented. Three moves, each with its real moment:
  - **Say who is about to speak, and why they are worth hearing.** Segment 6: "a wound that's still felt
    keenly today, as Wole Soyinka, the Nigerian poet and playwright, describes." The packet uses this
    attribution rather than the parallel one in Segment 5, which introduces the 1897 curator whose own
    quotation is deliberately not printed for students (see the open items).
  - **Say what kind of thing they said before they say it.** Segment 3: "The overall effect was
    enthusiastically described in detail by an early Dutch visitor."
  - **Boil a long quotation down to one sentence afterwards.** Segment 3, immediately after that Dutch
    description: "In short, Europeans visiting Benin, in the fifteenth and sixteenth century, discovered a
    society every bit as organised and structured as the great courts of Europe."
- Teach the frames these moves give: "As ___, the ___, describes: ___." / "___ was described in detail by
  ___." / "In short, ___."
- Point out the pattern: he never leaves a quotation to stand on its own. Something before it says whose it
  is, and something after it says what it proves.

**Phase 2: Differentiated Speaking Practice, Choose One Level (20 min)**

Each student picks one task Level.

- **Level 5 (one star):** give an extended spoken description of the plaque, organized so a listener can
  follow it, and include one word choice that signals what you think of it without you stating your opinion
  outright.
- **Level 6 (two stars):** describe one thing the plaque shows, moving between what it shows and why it
  matters, and explaining out loud why that one detail matters to what we can know about the kingdom. When
  your partner reacts, adjust how you are speaking once in response to their reaction.
- **Level 7 (three stars):** describe the plaques twice to the same partner, using only details that are
  true. The first time, choose and emphasize the details that leave the impression the episode leaves. The
  second time, choose and emphasize the details that leave the impression the 2022 article leaves.
  Somewhere in the two deliveries, catch a word of your own that is not quite accurate and repair it out
  loud as you go.
- **Level 8 (four stars):** describe, without preparing first, an object you have seen in a museum or a
  public place. Your partner reacts three times, once looking unconvinced, once asking where it came from,
  once saying they have seen it too. Each time, shift how you are speaking in response: more formal or
  less, more general or more specific. Correct yourself at least once when a word you have chosen turns out
  to be inaccurate or misleading.

**Phase 3: Pronunciation Mini-Focus (10 min)**

- Teach thought-group pausing, using the presenter's own audio as the model. His sentences are long, and he
  keeps them followable by breaking them into short groups with a small pause and a slight pitch reset at
  each break.
  - Segment 2: "In the centre, | in the proudest relief | and looking straight out at us, | is the oba." -
    three groups arrive before the subject does, and the sentence still holds together.
  - Segment 2: "The plaque is about two feet square (60 cm). | Its colour strikes you as coppery rather
    than brassy, | and there are five figures on it | - three Africans and two Europeans." (The transcript's
    own "(60 cm)" is a written gloss, not something said aloud.)
- Students mark the two printed lines where they expect a break, then check their marking by listening to
  those two moments again, then rehearse their own Phase 2 language in pairs, breaking their longest
  sentence into groups instead of running it together in one breath.

**Phase 4: Structured Oral Output - Jigsaw Expert Panels (20 min)**

- Four voices from the episode are printed in the packet, in the words below. Each small group becomes the
  expert group for one of them, prepares a summary of what their speaker said, and reports it to the class
  in turn, using the frames from Phase 1. All four quotations are printed for every student, so any group
  can take any voice and so every listener can follow every report.
  1. **Neil MacGregor, on what the plaque shows** (Segment 2): "In the centre, in the proudest relief and
     looking straight out at us, is the oba. He's on his throne wearing a high helmet-like crown."
  2. **An early Dutch visitor, on the oba's palace** (Segment 3): "The king's court is square. It is
     divided into many magnificent palaces, houses and apartments of the courtiers, and comprises beautiful
     and long square galleries, about as large as the Exchange at Amsterdam."
  3. **Sokari Douglas Camp, sculptor, on the coral the oba wears** (Segment 3): "You know, the remarkable
     thing about Nigeria is that all the coral and things don't actually come from our coast, they come
     from Portugal, and places like that. So all of that conversation has always been very important to me,
     you know we have things that are supposed to be totally traditional, yet they're traditional through
     trade."
  4. **Wole Soyinka, on what a Benin Bronze means to him** (Segment 6): "When I see a Benin Bronze, I
     immediately think of the mastery of technology and art - the welding of the two. I think immediately
     of a cohesive ancient civilisation."
- **The listener's task, for every student not currently reporting:** write down, for each panel, one thing
  their speaker said and one word the reporting group chose that their speaker did not use.
- **Differentiated participation:** one-star students rehearse their group's summary with a partner before
  the panel round begins and deliver the first sentence of it, rather than speaking unrehearsed.
  **Foundation Support (below Level 5):** a student below the lowest task Level's floor takes the
  listener's task for the whole round from the printed quotations, and contributes one rehearsed sentence
  naming their group's speaker when called on.
- **Board-dependent moment:** a running board record of each panel's summary sentence as it is actually
  spoken, set beside the speaker's own words, feeding Phase 5.

**Phase 5: Speaking Closing Transfer Check & Wrap (10 min)**

- Every student, in pairs and out loud, summarizes what their partner just said on a fresh topic unrelated
  to the episode: one partner describes something they own or somewhere they go, and the other summarizes
  it back using one of Phase 1's frames, naming whose words they are. Then they swap. Teacher cold-calls
  two or three pairs.
- **Finish the Chart:** students complete the third column of the chart they started on Day 1, and the
  class completes the shared board version.
- **Respectful Tiers check:** across the whole cycle, did the one-star Level get a genuine interpretive
  step on Day 1 (item 4, working out what makes the objects disturbing to the speaker and proving it from
  his words) and a real production role on Day 2 (a rehearsed opening sentence in the panel round), rather
  than fact-retrieval and silent listening?
- **Reflection (the one bounded question Quality Standards §E6 allows at this band, tied to a named
  sentence of the student's own):** "Look at the summary sentence you said back to your partner. Which word
  in it was yours rather than theirs?"
